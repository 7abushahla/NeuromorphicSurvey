1 Introduction

1.1 Why spiking computation matters

Machine learning increasingly operates where data are produced. Cameras, microphones, tactile arrays, and industrial sensors generate streams that must often be interpreted under limits on latency, communication, power, and local memory. Conventional deep networks have transformed perception, but their common abstraction is a sequence of dense tensor operations evaluated at chosen sampling intervals. That abstraction is effective and well supported. It does not make time, sparse change, or conditional computation first-class computational objects.

Spiking neural networks (SNNs) offer a different abstraction. A neuron retains state, receives events, and emits discrete spikes when its dynamics meet a condition. Information can therefore be represented in spike count, timing, order, population activity, or graded payloads. This makes SNNs natural candidates for temporal signals and event-based sensing, while also motivating hardware that can exploit conditional activity and sparse communication . Those properties are opportunities, not universal performance guarantees. Their practical value depends on the code, model, workload, platform, and measurement boundary.

Neuromorphic computing is consequently a field rather than a single device class or learning method. It spans dynamical models, neural codes, learning rules, software frameworks, compilers, and digital, analog, and mixed-signal execution systems. Choices made at one layer change what can be expressed at another. A temporal code can require state, precision, routing, or stopping behavior that a target does not provide. A model that is correct in one simulator can require an explicit transformation before a mapper or runtime can execute it .

Researchers reach SNNs through two principal routes. Direct training optimizes a spiking model, usually with local rules, surrogate gradients, or related approximations. ANN-to-SNN conversion starts from a trained artificial neural network (ANN) and maps its activations into spiking dynamics. The routes differ in optimization, representation, and common failure modes. They meet, however, when the resulting SNN must be represented in software and executed on a target .

1.2 Survey role, scope, and reader

This survey is a field-entry reference with a deployment-centered purpose. It develops the concepts needed to follow either route from computation and representation to a defensible model-to-chip claim. The intended reader is entering neuromorphic computing, preparing a thesis, selecting a deployment path, or interpreting an SNN result that spans algorithms and hardware. The survey does not begin by assuming a particular chip, code, or training route. It first establishes what a model represents, how its dynamics and time are defined, and what a decoder recovers. It then asks whether those semantics can be preserved, transformed, or measured on a named target.

The article does not attempt to replace specialist treatments of neuroscience, synaptic plasticity, surrogate-gradient theory, event-sensor physics, analog circuit design, or benchmark protocol. It gives the deployment-relevant concepts needed to connect these topics. For broad treatments of SNN learning, see Nunes et al. and Khan et al., which organize training methods and applications in greater depth . For event-based sensing and its representations, see Gallego et al., which treats the sensing literature beyond the computational boundary used here .

Three boundaries control the scope. First, the article distinguishes an algorithmic horizon from a hardware tick and from wall-clock latency. A small maximum horizon T is not a latency measurement. Second, it distinguishes a neural code from an input convention. Direct current injection, for example, is not automatically rate coding. Third, it separates a documented toolchain capability from a route exercised by a published model. These distinctions keep an algorithmic result, a software claim, and a hardware measurement from being treated as the same kind of evidence.

1.3 Existing surveys

Recent surveys have addressed spiking neural networks, neuromorphic computing, and event-driven systems from different angles, yet these topics are often treated separately. The existing literature can be broadly grouped into model- and learning-centered surveys, coding and sensing surveys, software and interoperability studies, hardware-platform surveys, and benchmarking, deployment, and application reviews. Each line establishes an important part of the field. None alone follows a trained SNN from its computational definition through software and hardware boundaries to a clearly stated measurement claim.

Models and learning. Foundational and algorithm-centered surveys establish the background needed to understand SNN construction. They compare neuron models, learning rules, surrogate gradients, conversion, and applications. Their main unit of analysis is a learning or conversion method rather than a complete deployment route . This literature explains why different training routes produce different constraints. It does not usually trace whether those constraints remain representable after export and mapping.

Coding. Code-centered work explains how a value becomes a spike representation and what a decoder recovers. That distinction matters for deployment because rate, first-spike-time, population, and graded-payload representations impose different state, payload, precision, and stopping requirements. The present survey uses these code families as execution contracts in Sections 4 and 10. It does not repeat a general coding survey. For a broader code taxonomy, see Auge et al., particularly for the signal-representation trade-offs outside ANN-to-SNN conversion .

Software and interoperability. Framework and toolchain reviews identify simulators, programming models, compilers, and mapping environments. NIR provides an important precedent. It makes neural primitives portable enough to compare implementations and exposes semantic disagreement across back ends . The remaining question is edge-specific. A graph format does not by itself state whether reset, delay, payload, readout, or host work is preserved, transformed, rejected, or only documented. Sections 10 and 11 make those alternatives explicit.

Hardware. Hardware surveys describe chips, circuits, execution styles, and selected applications. A platform-focused account can provide deep measured evidence for one target. Davies et al., for example, document Loihi results across several workloads. Broader hardware surveys compare implementation approaches and design constraints . Neither strength establishes that a model definition or compiler route transfers unchanged across platform families. Sections 12 and 13 therefore compare execution models through common contract fields before evaluating any named route.

Benchmarking, deployment, and applications. NeuroBench and the EdgeSNN survey make an essential separation between hardware-independent proxy evaluation and hardware-aware or physical-system evaluation . Application reviews show the breadth of vision, audio, control, and other workloads . These are strong precedents. They do not retrospectively classify each published claim by physical execution, documented path, model, or simulation, and they do not require a named edge-by-edge route from a trained network to a target. This survey bridges those two omissions through a deployable SNN contract, typed software and hardware edges, and an explicit measurement boundary.

The resulting gap is narrower than a claim that earlier work ignored deployment. Prior surveys address important pieces of the problem. The gap is an end-to-end synthesis that lets a reader ask the same questions of every route. What is mathematically defined? What can the software represent? What does the target execute? What was measured, where, and against which baseline? The answer may be a physical route, a transformed route, a blocked route, or an unexercised documented path. Each answer remains useful when its evidence boundary is stated.

1.4 Evidence guide

A benchmark protocol separates physical evaluation from a proxy so that a future submission can report both honestly. A literature survey has a different task. It must classify claims already in print, including the route evidence that surrounds an accuracy, energy, or latency result. The five classes below refine that distinction. They apply to a claim, not to an entire paper. One paper can therefore contain an E5 accuracy result and an E3 documented route, or an E2 fidelity result without an energy measurement. The classes are not quality grades.

Table 2: Evidence classes for individual deployment claims.
ClassMeaningWhat it does not establish
E1Physical hardware with measured latency, energy, power, or system performance.Equivalence to a different platform, workload, or boundary.
E2Physical hardware with accuracy or fidelity measurement only.Measured latency, power, or energy.
E3A documented but unexercised deployment route.A published run of the model across that route.
E4A hardware model, RTL synthesis, or cycle-level simulation without fabricated silicon.Physical execution.
E5Software simulation, an analytical proxy, or algorithm-only evidence.A hardware capability or deployment measurement.

The classes prevent several recurring substitutions. Fewer timesteps do not prove lower wall-clock latency. Fewer spikes do not prove lower energy. A lower operation count does not prove lower measured power. A documented exporter does not prove that a published model reached silicon. Sections 4, 11, and 14 define the terms needed to make these differences operational. The evidence guide then remains fixed throughout the article.

1.5 What this survey contributes

The first contribution is an explanatory path from computation and representation to deployment evidence. It gives direct training and conversion a common destination rather than treating them as separate fields. The destination is the deployable SNN contract. It records the semantic fields that must be defined mathematically, represented in software, and faithfully executed on a target.

The second contribution is a claim-level evidence framework. E1 through E5 distinguish physical measurement, physical fidelity, documented route, hardware model, and software evidence without collapsing them into a paper-level label. The framework is paired with a typed route vocabulary. Every edge records its input, output, preserved and transformed semantics, rejected features, route state, source, and evidence limit.

The third contribution is a deployment synthesis organized around routes and measurement boundaries. Platform capabilities are interpreted through official documentation, SDKs, mapper constraints, development kits, release notes, and examples. Applications are then grouped by workload rather than by vendor. This organization lets a reader compare what was actually executed and measured without assuming that a device family, a simulator, and a deployment route are interchangeable.

These tools motivate a question that the evidence synthesis will test rather than answer here. Recent low-timestep conversion papers improve finite-window accuracy in simulation. Do their own methods reach named physical silicon with measurement, or does deployment proceed through older pipelines, directly trained models, or platform-native codes? Section 16 gives the counting policy, falsifier, counterexamples, and qualifications. Section 1 deliberately does not present that result in advance.

1.6 Reading roadmap

Sections 2 through 4 define computation, state, neuron and synapse dynamics, temporal semantics, codes, and decoding. Sections 5 and 6 introduce the learning fork and bound direct-training detail. Sections 7 through 9 derive classical ANN-to-SNN conversion, locate its finite-time errors, and organize remedies by intervention. Section 10 defines the deployable SNN contract. Sections 11 through 13 follow that contract through software, hardware execution models, and complete routes. Sections 14 and 15 define measurement before organizing deployment evidence by workload. Sections 16 through 18 state what the evidence shows, derive open problems from failed contract edges, and provide a researcher entry guide.

The next three sections establish the vocabulary that every later deployment claim requires. They distinguish value, state, event, time, and code before a platform or conversion method is asked to preserve them.
