4 Temporal Semantics, Neural Codes, and Decoding

Section 3 wrote every neuron update in terms of a discrete step index \(t\) without stating what that index means physically. It also left the emitted event \(z_i^l[t]\) undefined as a carrier of information, beyond the fact that it is a threshold crossing under the reset rule fixed at reset and refractory behavior. Neither omission is accidental. What \(t\) denotes in wall-clock terms, and what a sequence of \(z_i^l[t]\) values is defined to represent, are choices made independently of the state update in the common state update. This section fixes both choices before any later section compares a training method, a software representation, or a hardware platform. Section 4.1 separates the several distinct things a reader might mean by time. Sections 4.2 through 4.4 define a neural code as an encoder, a transmitted representation, and a decoder, then compare eight codes through that template. Section 4.5 fixes vocabulary for terms that recur, and are frequently conflated, for the remainder of the article.

4.1 Time Vocabulary

Seven distinct quantities share the word time in this literature. Continuous physical time \(\tau\) is measured in seconds and is the axis a biological or physically modeled process evolves on. The integration step or hardware tick duration \(\Delta t\) is the interval a discretization or a clocked substrate advances by; a numerical simulation and a physical clock can each define a \(\Delta t\), and the two need not agree. The discrete algorithmic step index \(t\) indexes the update order fixed in Section 3 and never by itself denotes a duration. The algorithmic inference horizon \(T\) is the maximum number of such steps a simulation or an unrolled network is allowed to run. A static layer-specific horizon \(T_l\) assigns a different maximum to each layer rather than one shared value. An input-dependent stopping step \(T_{\mathrm{stop}}(x)\) is decided during inference for a specific input rather than fixed in advance. A reported effective or averaged horizon \(T_{\mathrm{eff}}\) is a summary statistic computed after the fact from a set of per-layer or per-input values, and it inherits whatever weighting and truncation produced it. On the hardware side, the tick duration \(\tau_{\mathrm{tick}}\) is the physical time a clocked update actually occupies, a synchronization barrier is a mechanism that holds every part of a system at the same algorithmic step until all parts have finished it, and asynchronous event time is the absence of any such barrier, so that a component acts as soon as its own input arrives. Wall-clock inference latency \(\tau_{\mathrm{inf}}\) and decision latency \(\tau_{\mathrm{decision}}\) close the vocabulary. Both name a duration between a stated start event and a stated stop event; \(\tau_{\mathrm{decision}}\) may be shorter than a declared maximum window when a decision becomes available before that window elapses. None of \(T\), \(T_l\), \(T_{\mathrm{stop}}(x)\), or \(T_{\mathrm{eff}}\) is a synonym for \(\tau_{\mathrm{inf}}\). \(T\) is a step count. It is not wall-clock latency, spike count, energy, or numeric precision, and a claim that reports one of these quantities in place of another has changed what it is measuring without saying so.

The remaining risk is not the definitions themselves but four ways an algorithmic step count is regularly, and incorrectly, read as a hardware schedule. The first is a static per-layer horizon. PASCAL, the quantization-aware conversion method QAC, and its successor MT-SNN each assign a separate, fixed timestep count \(T_l\) to every layer before inference and reconcile the resulting mismatch between adjacent layers with an explicit realignment step, in each case implemented as averaging a source layer's full temporal dimension and repeating that average to the target layer's length . QAC's own hardware-efficiency appendix states directly that a pipelined multicore accelerator built to this schedule can stall, because a downstream layer must wait for an upstream layer to finish a different number of steps before it can begin . The headline efficiency number each paper reports, an average or effective timestep count, is a derived summary of this per-layer allocation. QAC's reported 2.76 average timesteps for a ResNet-18 network on CIFAR-10 is exactly this kind of summary, not a measured latency, a sum of sequential layer work, or a hardware cycle count . This distinction generalizes. A mean over per-layer or per-sample horizons carries only the weighting and truncation used to compute it; it does not carry synchronization, communication, scheduling, or stall time, and reporting it as if it were a duration substitutes an algorithmic statistic for a wall-clock measurement.

The second mechanism is per-input early exit. SEENN and Dynamic Confidence each vary a single, whole-network terminal horizon \(T_{\mathrm{stop}}(x)\) per input, stopping every layer together once a confidence threshold or a learned policy is satisfied at runtime . This is a different control variable from the static per-layer allocation above. Every layer within one inference still shares the same running step count; only the stopping point varies from one input to the next. A reported average timestep for an early-exit method is therefore a distribution over per-sample stopping times, not a set of per-layer horizons, and the two summaries should not be read as evidence for the same underlying mechanism.

The third mechanism is asynchronous execution, and it is the one most often overextended. Asynchronous core computation or event delivery does not by itself establish independent discrete time coordinates for different layers. Loihi and Loihi 2 compute and communicate asynchronously within a core, yet both platforms advance a single, global algorithmic timestep across the whole chip through a barrier synchronization handshake, and TrueNorth and Tianjic each advance time through their own chip-wide synchronization signal . Asynchronous circuitry inside a core is therefore compatible with, and on these platforms coexists with, a synchronized global tick across cores. This is the reason asynchronous execution and independent per-layer horizons appear on the list of equivalences this survey will not assert without a specific derivation or measurement. A platform being internally asynchronous is not evidence that it can host the static per-layer schedule discussed above.

The fourth mechanism is timestep-free event-driven execution, and it is the one case among the four with a physical result. On a substrate such as Speck2e there is no vector of discrete per-layer horizons to execute at all, because state updates occur strictly on event arrival. Temporal Flexibility trains a single weight set with Mixed Time-step Training, an augmentation that samples a vector of timestep counts and assigns them to network stages during each training iteration, and it deploys the resulting weights to a physical Speck2e device, reporting 99.16 percent training-time accuracy, 98.56 percent event-driven simulator accuracy, and 98.57 percent on-chip accuracy for N-MNIST, together with a 3.92 percent simulator-to-chip spike-pattern difference . This is genuine physical-hardware evidence, but it is evidence for a claim distinct from the one PASCAL, QAC, and MT-SNN make. The chip execution is fully event-driven and timestep-free; the mixed-stage timestep vector trains a temporally robust weight set, and it is not scheduled on the chip as a set of distinct discrete per-layer horizons . No source examined for this section demonstrates a static, heterogeneous, discrete per-layer timestep schedule executing on physical synchronous hardware. That narrower claim, and the two-population evidence that surrounds it, is deferred to Section 16; the point established here is only the vocabulary needed to keep the four mechanisms separate.

The three execution styles just distinguished, a shared global step under a barrier or a timer tick, asynchronous event-driven execution, and analog continuous dynamics sampled by a digital control frame, are drawn in Figures 19 and 20 at the end of Section 4.4, after the codes whose horizons they carry.

4.2 Encoder, Channel, and Decoder

A neuron model, fixed in Section 3, specifies how one unit's state evolves and when it emits an event. A neural code is a separate choice. It specifies what a source variable \(x\) is turned into before it enters the network, what that transformation causes to travel through the network, and what a receiver must compute to recover an estimate of the original quantity. The same integrate-and-fire update can carry a rate code, a timing code, or a graded payload depending only on how its input is prepared and how its output is read, so the neuron equations already fixed do not by themselves determine the code in use.

The three roles are an encoder \(\mathcal{E}\), a channel \(\mathcal{C}\), and a decoder \(\mathcal{D}\). The encoder maps the source variable to a current, an event train, or another representation the network can consume; the channel is the network transformation the representation passes through, characterized by its neuron model, its code, its payload, and its time model; the decoder maps the channel's output at some observation window to a decoded estimate \(\hat{x}\) or a decision \(\hat{y}\). At the physical level, what a unit actually emits is the continuous-time spike train \(s(\tau)=\sum_k\delta(\tau-\tau_k)\), a sum of Dirac impulses placed at the event times \(\tau_k\), and the discrete \(z_i^l[t]\) fixed in Section 3 is what that train becomes once it is sampled onto the algorithmic step grid, as Section 3.6 derives. Writing a neuron's output this way makes it a point process rather than a scalar signal, the object Gerstner et al. treat throughout. Symbolically,

\[ \hat{x} \;=\; \mathcal{D}\Big(\mathcal{C}\big(\mathcal{E}(x)\big)\Big), \]

where every stage is defined independently. Changing the decoder while holding the encoder and channel fixed changes what is recovered from the same spike pattern; changing the encoder while holding the channel and decoder fixed changes what the same downstream mechanism is being asked to reconstruct. A frequent source of confusion is that an encoding convention is silently treated as a code. Applying a real-valued input as a constant current at every step, discussed as direct coding in Section 4.3, is an input convention. It is not automatically a neural code, because it does not by itself define an encoder that produces a spike-based representation, a channel that carries one, or a decoder that recovers a value from one.

The three-role decomposition just stated is Shannon's source, encoder, channel, decoder decomposition, and the reuse is deliberate rather than incidental. Four things carry over from it cleanly. The decomposition itself is what lets Section 4.3 compare eight codes on one template, encoder, channel, decoder, instead of eight unrelated descriptions. The finite-alphabet argument is the formal statement behind the \(1/T\) quantizer already fixed in this section, a count code observed over \(T\) steps has \(T+1\) possible symbols and can therefore carry at most \(\log_2(T+1)\) bits about \(x\), which is exactly the precision-against-latency trade Section 4.4 and Section 9.1 return to. Channel noise is the third borrowing. Poisson sampling variance is noise on the channel in Shannon's sense, and it is the reason a stochastic encoder degrades at a short horizon. The fourth is mutual information \(I(S;R)\), the measure of what any decoder could in principle recover from a response, independent of which decoder is actually built, which is how neuroscience quantifies a code without committing to a specific decoding rule, a measure due to Shannon that Borst and Theunissen review for its use in neural coding, with Quian Quiroga and Panzeri for the distinction between the information a code carries and what a stated decoder actually extracts from it.

Just as much is refused, stated once here so that no later section needs to qualify a code claim against it again. A neural code names what a response represents, not a designed mapping with an alphabet, a codebook, and error correction, and nothing in this section reads one into the term. Rate code is an experimental term, from Adrian's 1926 finding that impulse frequency tracks stimulus strength, that predates Shannon's 1948 theory by more than two decades, so its origin is physiological rather than information-theoretic, the same correction already registered in Section 2.1. Nothing in this survey asserts that a biological or a deployed spike train is optimal, error-corrected, or operating at capacity. MacKay and McCulloch's 1952 question of how much information a neuronal link can carry is the origin of that capacity question, and it is referred out rather than pursued here. The present discussion stops after stating that this survey does not compute or rely on a capacity claim. For a fuller information-theoretic treatment of neural coding capacity, see Rieke et al. and Dayan and Abbott, which are particularly useful for connecting the capacity question to specific decoding models.

Five input conventions recur across the sources this survey reviews, and casual usage collapses several of them into one label. Poisson rate encoding draws a stochastic spike train whose expected count over the window is proportional to \(x\), so the first layer receives a genuine, sampled binary event schedule. Deterministic count encoding instead computes a fixed number of evenly spaced spikes from \(x\) without sampling, producing a binary event schedule with no sampling variance but the same discrete \(1/T\) grid. Repeated-frame presentation holds the same real-valued \(x\) at every algorithmic step rather than converting it to spikes at all; the first layer that consumes it performs a full multiply-accumulate at every step, exactly as an ANN layer would, and only the number of repetitions varies with \(T\). Direct constant-current input, introduced above, applies \(x\) once as a constant drive rather than repeating a frame, so the same multiply-accumulate cost is paid without the repetition. Event-native sensing supplies timestamped events produced by acquisition itself, for instance by an event camera, so no software or host encoder converts a value into spikes at all; the network instead must accept whatever address, polarity, and timing resolution the sensor already fixed. The deployment consequence separates cleanly along this line. Poisson, deterministic count, and event-native input all hand the first layer genuine binary events and can therefore use accumulate-only arithmetic there, though Poisson and deterministic count still require a host-side or dedicated encoding step that event-native acquisition does not. Repeated-frame and direct-current input instead commit the first layer to multiply-accumulate arithmetic regardless of what the rest of the network does downstream.

Figure 16 places the five conventions just distinguished side by side, separating the four commonly grouped under the single label rate coding from event-native acquisition, to show that they hand the first layer different temporal objects, a binary event schedule, a held value, or an asynchronously timed record, rather than one shared representation.

Two quantities recur across the code comparison in Section 4.3. The spike count in a finite horizon, \(N_i^l(T) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} z_i^l[t]\), sums the same binary event \(z_i^l[t]\) that Section 3's update order emits at each step \(t\), over the closed window from the first step to the declared horizon \(T\); it is defined only for a binary discrete-time event variable, and it is not itself a rate until it is normalized. The discrete firing fraction \(\rho_i^l(T) = N_i^l(T)/T\) performs that normalization within the simulation, but it is not a physical rate unless it is further divided by a stated physical duration. Both quantities are decoder inputs, not code definitions on their own; what makes a code a rate code is the choice to read \(N_i^l(T)\) or \(\rho_i^l(T)\) as the encoded value, as Section 4.3 makes explicit for the first entry in the comparison.

4.3 Code Families

The per-code template applied throughout this subsection, the represented variable, the transmitted object, and the decoder, is the source-encoder-channel-decoder decomposition of Section 4.2 applied one code at a time.

Eight codes recur in the ANN-to-SNN conversion and direct-training literature reviewed for this survey. Each is described below through the same five questions established in Section 4.2. What variable does it represent. How does the encoder produce a representation. What travels through the channel. What does the decoder compute. What horizon, precision, spike cost, and hardware requirement does the comparison in Table 7 record for it. The codes are not interchangeable ways of carrying the same information at the same cost; each trades a different resource, principally time, neurons, payload width, or absolute amplitude, for its stated cost.

Rate coding. The represented variable is a scalar, typically a non-negative activation value. The encoder is either a Poisson process whose instantaneous rate is set by the value, or a deterministic integration of a constant input current; the channel carries a sequence of binary events \(z_i^l[t]\); the decoder is the spike count \(N_i^l(T)\) or firing fraction \(\rho_i^l(T)\) defined in Section 4.2, or, in several conversion frameworks, the pre-threshold membrane potential of a non-spiking final layer read out directly . With a fixed threshold, the achievable spike counts over a \(T\)-step window are the integers from zero to \(T\), so the achievable output levels are spaced uniformly at intervals of \(1/T\); this uniform grid is a consequence of the counting operation itself, not a design choice made by any particular method . Deterministic count coding's quantization error scales as \(1/T\), while stochastic Poisson coding's variance instead scales as \(1/\sqrt{T}\), so Poisson input requires quadratically more steps for the same precision, which is why essentially all low-latency conversion work now uses deterministic input . The deployment consequence is that rate coding's cost is paid in steps, one spike per representable level in the worst case, and that cost is what Sections 8 and 9 later show low-latency conversion methods attempting to reduce without abandoning the underlying rate-matching correspondence.

Time-to-first-spike coding. The represented variable is again a scalar, but the encoder maps it to the latency of a single spike relative to a reference onset, typically with larger values producing earlier spikes; each neuron fires at most once. The channel carries one spike-timing event per neuron, and the decoder is a winner-take-all over first-spike time or a learned temporal-weighting decoder that favors earlier spikes . A single spike's continuous-valued timing carries, in principle, arbitrary precision, which is the code's appeal, but a physical substrate must quantize firing time to its own discrete tick, reintroducing a precision floor the idealized formulation does not have . The single-spike constraint also concentrates a layer's entire informational content into precise timing, which produces sparse, hard-to-train gradients relative to rate coding . Quartz is a converted, physically deployed instance of this code. Its activation representation is time to first spike with a balancing event per neuron rather than a spike count, and because each neuron fires at most once, its neurons need no soft-reset mechanism of the kind Section 3 makes load-bearing for rate coding . Quartz reports on-chip static power, dynamic power, latency, energy per inference, and energy-delay product on physical Loihi 1, at 65 timesteps for MNIST and 1211 timesteps for CIFAR-10, error rates the paper itself frames as an accuracy-energy trade against rate-coded results on the same dataset rather than an unconditional improvement .

Phase coding. The represented variable is a scalar. The encoder assigns each spike a weight given by its position within a repeating cycle of \(K\) timesteps, typically the powers of two \(2^{K-1}\) down to \(2^0\), analogous to binary place value . The channel carries binary events whose phase within the cycle is meaningful; the decoder sums the weights of the phases at which a spike occurred. Because the weights are powers of two, the representable outputs are subset sums of a geometric sequence, an exponentially weighted grid of \(2^K\) levels rather than the uniform \(1/T\) grid rate coding produces, so precision grows exponentially in cycle length rather than linearly in step count. The cost is bounded by the cycle length shared by the whole network rather than by any single spike; one report finds phase coding alone requiring on the order of three thousand simulation steps for a 32-layer network on CIFAR-100, and a related bistable-neuron method combining phase coding with a bistability mechanism reports the same order of magnitude for CIFAR-100 . Phase coding is documented as a software or training-time convention layered on an ordinary binary-spike substrate in the sources examined for this section rather than as a dedicated hardware decoding primitive, which is an absence in the material reviewed and not a demonstrated impossibility.

Burst coding. The represented variable is a scalar and the encoder is the same spike-count mechanism as rate coding, with one change. The neuron is permitted to emit more than one spike within a single timestep when its residual post-threshold membrane potential remains large, lifting the one-spike-per-step ceiling ordinary rate coding assumes . The channel therefore carries the same unit-spike count as rate coding, but that count can be packed into fewer timesteps because several unit spikes may occur in one step. The decoder is unchanged from rate coding, a count over the observation window. The hardware requirement is a neuron capable of multi-spike emission within one step; Speck documents such a mode, but the specific deployment cited alongside it in fact used a plain, single-spike-per-step neuron rather than the multi-spike variant, which is a documented capability that this section does not treat as an exercised route for that deployment .

Population coding. The represented variable can again be a scalar, but the encoder distributes it across the activity pattern of a population of \(N\) neurons, for example through per-neuron tuning curves, rather than through any single neuron's rate or timing. The channel carries the joint activity of the population, and the decoder is an ensemble readout over that activity. Population coding is best understood as an orthogonal, spatial axis rather than a fifth primitive alongside rate and temporal codes, since each neuron inside the population can itself be rate coded or time coded internally . Its cost is paid in neurons rather than in steps, which is the opposite trade from every temporal code above. Adaptive Fission illustrates the trade concretely. It replaces additional temporal integration with a group of binary neurons carrying unequal fixed weights, proposing a population code layered on top of existing converted or directly trained networks rather than a change to rate coding itself, and it reports physical execution on a Lynxi HP201 accelerator with per-epoch memory, time, and system energy measured from board sensors, a system-level rather than a core-only measurement boundary .

Sigma-delta coding. The represented variable is again a scalar activation, typically one that changes slowly from step to step. The encoder is a delta transmitter that emits a spike only when the change in activation since the last transmission exceeds a threshold, and that spike carries the size of the change as its payload rather than a count of unit events. The channel therefore requires a payload wider than one bit; a sigma-delta spike's precision is not equivalent to an ordinary binary spike, and reading it as one discards the information the code depends on. The decoder is a running sum that reconstructs the activation from the sequence of transmitted changes. Loihi 2 is the confirmed native case, supporting graded spikes that carry up to 24 bits of signed integer magnitude directly in the spike message in the documented sigma-delta route , narrower than the platform's general graded-payload ceiling of up to 32 bits stated in Intel's brief . Brehove et al. build a sigma-delta conversion pipeline on this feature and report latency and energy-delay product measured on physical, multi-chip Loihi 2 hardware against a Jetson Xavier baseline for a video object-detection workload, transmitting signed graded activation deltas rather than a firing rate estimated from binary counts. Loihi 1 lacks a native graded-spike channel and approximates the mechanism with a two-compartment adaptive neuron at twice the compartment cost, which one group uses to reach a reported state of the art for on-chip speech classification with three-bit quantization-aware weights . Lava-DL documents sigma-delta blocks as a first-class, trainable layer type; this is a documented software capability, and this section records it as such rather than as an independently exercised deployment .

Rank order coding. The represented variable is not a magnitude at all. Each neuron in a population fires at most once, modeled as an analog-to-delay converter in which a more strongly driven neuron crosses threshold sooner, and the encoded content is purely the relative order in which the population's neurons fire during one feedforward wave . The channel carries that ordering; the canonical decoder is a shunting-inhibition mechanism in which each arriving spike progressively desensitizes the postsynaptic neuron, so an afferent's influence decays with its arrival rank . With \(N\) neurons the code's channel capacity is on the order of \(\log_2(N!)\) bits, obtained with roughly \(N\) spikes in a single feedforward wave rather than the count a rate code would need over a multi-step window . Rank order coding is contrast-invariant, because rescaling an input's intensity changes every latency but not their relative order, and this robustness is purchased by discarding absolute amplitude entirely; the ordering alone cannot recover how large the original values were . This is also why the code sits outside the rate-matching correspondence formalized in Section 7. Every conversion proof surveyed for this article, matching a per-layer firing rate between an artificial network and a spiking network, is stated over a magnitude, and an ordinal object drawn from \(N!\) possible permutations has no natural target for that kind of convergence argument . The unifying framework of Deneve et al. places the code precisely among its relatives; time-to-first-spike coding reads a continuous timing value, rank order coding discards timing and keeps only order, and N-of-M coding discards order as well in exchange for needing only binary synapses. The clearest measured hardware comparison is MorphIC, a 65-nanometer four-core digital processor on which rate coding reaches 97.8 percent accuracy at 205 microjoules per classification on the same chip and the same MNIST task where rank order coding reaches 95.9 percent accuracy at 21.8 microjoules per classification, a measured tenfold energy reduction for a 1.9-percentage-point accuracy cost . BrainChip's Akida processor carries a genuine rank-order-coding lineage; the company acquired Simon Thorpe's rank-order-coding research program in 2016, and an independent trade-press account corroborates a design pivot from an earlier rate-coding approach to rank coding . BrainChip's own documentation and an independent community summary describe the native input-encoding stage as rank-order-coded . The standard QuantizeML-to-CNN2SNN deployment route that essentially all published Akida results use, however, collapses to a single-pass, order-agnostic threshold comparison rather than either an order-sensitive mechanism or genuine multi-step rate coding, the transformation Section 9.6 and Section 13.3 develop in full. A dedicated application-specific rank-order-coding circuit with unsupervised local plasticity separately reports 90.2 percent accuracy at 6.79 microjoules per classification on MNIST on fabricated 65-nanometer silicon .

Direct coding. The represented variable is again a scalar, typically a normalized pixel intensity or another real-valued input feature. What Section 4.2 calls direct coding here is, strictly, an input convention rather than a spike code. Instead of an encoder that produces a spike train, a real-valued input is applied as a constant analog current to a layer's neurons at every step, and only that layer's output is spiking. Rueckauer et al. introduce this alongside soft-reset semantics and weight normalization, reporting that switching a first layer from Poisson spike-train input to constant analog current, with all other settings fixed, raised CIFAR-10 accuracy substantially in a controlled comparison. There is no channel-level spike code and no decoder at the input stage; the cost is instead architectural. The layer receiving direct current performs a genuine multiply-accumulate on real-valued input rather than an accumulate over binary events, forfeiting for that one layer the accumulate-only efficiency the rest of a spiking network is built to exploit, an overhead reported as small relative to the network as a whole precisely because it is confined to a single layer .

4.4 Cross-Code Trade-Offs

Precision throughout this comparison is stated as bits of the decoded value where a count is well defined, as it is for rate, burst, and phase coding, and this survey does not state a bits-per-spike figure for a timing code such as time-to-first-spike or rank order coding without a source that derives one.

The eight codes divide along the resource each one spends to buy precision. Rate and burst coding spend steps, filling a fixed \(1/T\) grid one unit at a time; phase coding spends a shared cycle length to buy an exponential grid, so its precision grows faster in cycle length than rate coding's grows in step count, at the price of a per-network cost that scales with the deepest cycle any layer requires. Time-to-first-spike coding spends timing resolution instead of step count, which is why it can in principle reach high precision in a single spike, but a physical tick grid puts a floor back under that precision once the code leaves the idealized continuous-time formulation. Population coding spends neurons rather than time, which makes it attractive precisely where neuron budget is cheaper than step count, and unattractive where it is not. Sigma-delta coding spends payload width, exchanging one wide graded spike for the many binary spikes a rate code would need to carry the same change, but only where the underlying activation is well behaved enough that changes are infrequent and a graded-spike channel exists to carry them. Rank order coding spends none of these and instead gives up amplitude entirely, trading a magnitude for an ordering that a single wave can transmit.

These trades interact with robustness in ways a cost count alone does not show. A rate code degrades gracefully under small perturbations to individual events, because the decoder only needs an approximately correct count. A rank order code is comparatively fragile to timing jitter when several neurons' latencies cluster closely, because the decoder depends on their relative order rather than on any single event's exact position . A sigma-delta code is only efficient when the represented signal changes slowly enough that few deltas cross threshold; a rapidly varying input erodes exactly the temporal redundancy the code is built to exploit. None of these trade-offs is visible from a spike count or a step count in isolation, which is the reason Table 7 records represented variable, decoder, and hardware requirement as separate fields rather than collapsing the comparison to a single cost number.

A second interaction concerns training rather than execution. Codes that concentrate information into few events, most sharply time to first spike and rank order coding, correspondingly concentrate the gradient signal available to a learning rule into few events, which is part of why time-to-first-spike coding trains comparatively slowly despite its fast inference convergence . A code choice is therefore not independent of the learning route that will be used to obtain the weights that exploit it, a dependency Section 5 develops once both learning branches are on the table.

4.5 Deployment Terminology

Six terms recur for the rest of this article with a precise meaning this survey holds fixed. Table 8 states that meaning beside the conflation it is meant to prevent.

Table 8: Six recurring terms, the meaning fixed in this survey, and the conflation each definition rejects.
TermMeaning used in this surveyCommon conflation rejected
Timestep, \(T\)A discrete algorithmic step count, at most the declared inference horizon.Treating \(T\) as itself a duration, a spike count, an energy value, or a precision.
Latency, \(\tau_{\mathrm{inf}}\) or \(\tau_{\mathrm{decision}}\)A wall-clock duration between a stated start event and a stated stop event, on a stated measurement boundary.Reading a lower step count as a lower measured latency without stating the start and stop events, the boundary, and the batching used.
Event-driven (execution)State updates triggered by the arrival of discrete events, without a conventional shared clock advancing the whole system; one of the four senses Section 2.4 distinguishes.Treating event-native sensor input as proof that every downstream stage of a pipeline executes without a clock.
SparsityThe fraction of neurons or steps that do not emit an event within a stated measurement boundary.Treating fewer emitted spikes as proof, on its own, of fewer hardware operations or lower measured energy.
Energy, \(E_{\mathrm{inf}}\)A measured quantity, or one derived from measured power and latency, within a stated boundary.Substituting an analytical per-operation constant multiplied by a spike or operation count for a measured energy value.
ConversionA specific transformation of ANN parameters into spiking dynamics that preserves a stated correspondence, most often rate matching, under stated assumptions.Treating conversion as one code-agnostic recipe rather than a derivation whose assumptions change under an alternative code.

These distinctions are not administrative. A code's represented variable, payload width, observation window, and hardware requirement are exactly the properties that determine what a learning rule must produce and what a target must execute. Section 5 takes up the first half of that dependency, showing why generating and propagating a gradient through the encoder and decoder fixed here is harder for some codes than others and how direct training and conversion respond to that difficulty differently at the fork between the two branches. Sections 10 and 12 take up the second half, recording the neural code and spike payload as fields of the deployable SNN contract and comparing which hardware execution models can carry a given payload width and time model without a workaround.
