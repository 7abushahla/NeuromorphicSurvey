15 Deployment Measurement

Sections 8 and 9 already flagged the gap this section closes. A finite-time error term or an intervention against it is a mechanism or inference claim until it is tied to a number obtained from a stated experiment, and Section 4 (Section 4.5) fixed latency and energy as quantities that require a stated boundary without yet naming what the candidate boundaries are. This section names them, states what a comparable report of accuracy, time, power, energy, memory, and communication must contain, and explains why a Pareto claim across several of these axes is only as trustworthy as its weakest axis. Section 16 applies this vocabulary case by case; this section defines it once.

15.1 Deployment Metrics

A deployment claim mixes several quantities that this survey keeps separate rather than folding into one headline number. Accuracy or task quality states what was measured, on which dataset split, with which preprocessing, and whether the reported figure came from software evaluation or from the deployed device itself. Decision time \(\tau_{\mathrm{decision}}\) is the time until a valid output becomes available, and it can fall short of a declared maximum horizon \(T\) when a stopping rule intervenes, the distinction Section 4.1 already fixes for \(T_{\mathrm{stop}}(x)\). Wall-clock latency \(\tau_{\mathrm{inf}}\) is a duration between a stated start event and a stated stop event, and, as Table 8 already warns, a lower step count is not evidence of a lower \(\tau_{\mathrm{inf}}\) without that pair of events, a measurement boundary, and a batch size all stated together. Throughput \(R\) reports decisions per unit time at a stated batch size, and a peak-throughput figure at a large batch answers a different question than an interactive batch-one latency. Static and dynamic power, \(P_{\mathrm{static}}\) and \(P_{\mathrm{dyn}}\), require a stated subtraction method and boundary before they can be compared. Energy per inference \(E_{\mathrm{inf}}\) is either measured directly or computed from measured power and latency, and the product \(E_{\mathrm{inf}} \cdot \tau_{\mathrm{inf}}\), the EDP, is a single combined figure of merit only when both factors share the same boundary. Memory, on-chip weight storage, and routing or spike-packet communication cost round out the set, and, as Table 27 records, they are the fields most often left unstated in the audited evidence. Finally, a proxy metric, a spike count \(N_{\mathrm{spk}}\) or a synaptic-operation count \(N_{\mathrm{synop}}\), estimates one of the quantities above under an assumed per-operation energy constant rather than measuring it, and this survey treats a proxy as informative only when the source states the constant, the population it is applied to, and that the result is an estimate rather than a measurement.

15.2 Measurement Boundaries

A metric from Table 27 is only comparable to another instance of the same metric when both share a measurement boundary. This survey fixes six. A chip-only boundary includes only the neurocore or die itself, excluding host input and output (I/O) traffic, off-chip memory access, and any board-level peripheral. A board-level boundary adds the chip's own board, its power supply, memory, and I/O controllers, while still excluding a driving host machine. A host-inclusive boundary adds that driving host's own encode, decode, or streaming overhead on top of the board. A sensor-to-decision boundary spans the full path from physical sensor acquisition to a decision or actuation signal, with no host-side frame accumulation inserted along the way. A modeled boundary is an RTL synthesis, a cycle-accurate simulation, or an analytical or SynOps-style estimate, with no fabricated silicon executed at all. A physical boundary, finally, is a fabricated device measured directly and reported without further decomposition into the finer boundaries above, typically a bare die or packaged part on a bench rather than a system with its own board and host layers. The non-equivalences this survey holds fixed follow directly from Table 28. A chip-only number is not a system number, and a modeled number is not a physical one, because each boundary admits or excludes a different set of costs by construction, not by the quality of the paper reporting it.

15.3 Baseline Matching and Batching

A ratio against a baseline is only informative when the baseline shares the workload, precision, and, where relevant, the boundary of the result it is compared against. Kelber et al.'s own benchmarking table illustrates the failure mode directly. Within one comparison, SpiNNaker's and Spikey's energy figures are measured with real power meters, a physical boundary, while the BrainScaleS figure for the same comparison is estimated from previously published per-event data, a modeled boundary, so a reader who treats the table's three numbers as three measurements of the same kind is instead comparing two physical results against one modeled estimate. Ortone et al.'s tactile localization system reports the reverse pairing deliberately, contrasting its physical DYNAP-SE measurement against an explicitly estimated milliwatt-class conventional implementation of the same dynamics, stating the comparator's own boundary rather than presenting it as an equivalent measurement. Batch size carries the same requirement and is more often left unstated. Adaptive Fission's board-level throughput and energy figures name batch size 32 explicitly, one of the few records in the audited evidence to do so; most of the other cases surveyed in Section 16 report a single-sample or unspecified batching condition, which leaves the usual throughput-versus-latency trade unresolved for a reader trying to compare across them. A force-control policy's own comparison against a named CPU and GPU baseline states the comparator platforms explicitly without stating their numeric precision, a documentation gap this survey records rather than fills in.

15.4 Event Rate and Static Power

Dynamic power \(P_{\mathrm{dyn}}\) scales with the rate of state-changing events a substrate processes within its declared boundary, while static power \(P_{\mathrm{static}}\) is drawn continuously whenever the device is powered, independent of that rate. The tactile localization system above reports both terms directly on physical, mixed-signal hardware, a resting power an order of magnitude below its median inference power, the two figures separated rather than folded into one number. Yao et al.'s event-driven dynamic-computing chip ties the same distinction to an explicit intervention, reporting real-time power reduced through input masking that lowers the incoming event rate itself, a measured link between event rate and power on physical silicon rather than an assumed proportionality. Because a synchronous digital substrate keeps its static term running for the full duration it is powered and mapped, a schedule that shortens the algorithmic horizon \(T\) reduces only the dynamic contribution to \(E_{\mathrm{inf}}\), and the size of that reduction depends on what fraction of the reported energy was dynamic in the first place, a fraction this survey cannot state as a fixed proportion because none of the records above disclose it as one.

15.5 Uncertainty and Pareto Analysis

A single reported number carries no information about how much it would vary on a second run, a second die, or a second chip of the same design. Du et al.'s Temporal Flexibility reports two such figures for the identical physical deployment, a simulator-to-chip spike-pattern difference and a separate repeated-chip difference, the second obtained by comparing the same trained weights across more than one physical unit. ODIN's and ReckOn's own results are drawn from nine and five fabricated chips respectively, a die-to-die replication rather than a single-sample measurement, though neither source states the resulting spread as an explicit uncertainty figure. A Pareto claim, a reported frontier across two or more of the axes in Table 27 rather than one operating point, inherits the measurement boundary of its weakest axis. SEENN's and Dynamic Confidence's own accuracy-versus-average-timestep frontiers, already placed at E5 in Section 9.5, are constructed entirely in software simulation with a spike-count energy proxy standing in for the energy axis, and Dynamic Confidence's own text states that the real power saving on neuromorphic hardware is unknown to its authors. An accuracy-timestep curve built this way is not an accuracy-energy-latency Pareto surface measured on hardware, and this survey does not read it as one.

15.6 Measurement Checklist

The fields above recur often enough across Section 16's cases that a short checklist is more useful here than repeating the argument once per case. A comparable deployment report states each of the following, and Section 16 records where a given source leaves one unstated rather than treating the omission as a hidden zero.

  • The measurement boundary from Table 28, and, where a source reports more than one condition, which condition each number belongs to.
  • The baseline's workload, precision, and boundary, stated rather than implied by proximity in a table.
  • Batch size, or an explicit statement that the reported figure is a single-sample result.
  • Whether static and dynamic power are separated, and the subtraction method if so.
  • Whether energy or operation counts are measured or are a stated proxy, and, if a proxy, the assumed constant and the population it scales.
  • Any replication across chips, dies, or runs, and the resulting spread, where available.
  • For a frontier or Pareto claim, the boundary of every axis plotted, not only the axis the paper foregrounds.

Section 16 organizes the survey's deployment evidence by the workload each case addresses, vision, audio and temporal sensing, control and robotics, and scientific or industrial monitoring, and applies exactly this vocabulary to state, for every case, what was measured, at what boundary, against what baseline, and at what evidence class.
