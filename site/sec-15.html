15 Researcher Entry Guide and Conclusion

The preceding seventeen sections build a dependency chain from computation and representation to open research problems. A reader entering the field at a specific point, rather than from Section 1 forward, needs a shorter path through that chain and a small set of practical questions rather than a restated outline. This section gives four reading paths, a route-choice guide built from the contract fields Sections 9 through 11 already fix, a minimum reporting checklist, and referrals to the specialist surveys this survey has bounded itself against throughout. It closes with a short conclusion that states this survey's deployment argument rather than summarizing what came before it.

15.1 Reading Paths

A reader arriving from conventional deep learning who wants to understand how a trained ANN becomes an SNN should read Section 3's neuron and synapse dynamics, then Section 4's codes and decoding, then Section 8's complete conversion derivation in full before sampling Section 8's interventions by name. Section 8's finite-time error taxonomy is optional on a first pass but load-bearing before evaluating any specific low-latency method's claimed timestep reduction.

A reader who already has a specific chip in hand and wants to know what it can execute should start from Section 9's eleven-field contract, read the execution family that chip belongs to in Section 5, and check Section 9.5's Table 24 for that platform's row before consulting Section 11 for whether a named route from a training framework to that chip has actually been traced. Section 15.2 below turns this reading path into a short list of questions rather than a chapter list. A reader who has no chip in hand and wants to run a spiking network on a GPU, a CPU, or a microcontroller should start from Section 5, Where an SNN Runs, for what such a run can claim, and then read Section 11.3, Routes to Conventional Targets, for the five measured routes onto those processors.

A reader auditing a specific deployment claim, for a thesis, a peer review, or a competing method's stated baseline, should apply Section 1.3's five evidence classes to that specific claim rather than to the paper as a whole, check the claim against Section 12.2's six measurement boundaries and Section 12.6's checklist, and consult Section 13 for whether a similar claim in this survey's own audited corpus required a qualification the claim under review omits.

A reader designing a new low-latency conversion method, the population Section 13.7 and Section 14.6 both return to directly, should read Section 8 to identify which named error mechanism the new method targets, Section 8 to check whether an existing intervention already targets the same mechanism, and Section 14.1 before committing to a target platform, since the reset, precision, and operator gaps documented there apply to a new method exactly as they apply to every audited one.

15.2 Route Choice Guide

Section 11.6 already gives one worked instance of the general question this subsection generalizes, that a practitioner's actual requirement determines which of several physical routes is usable, not which route is most often cited. Before committing to a target, a practitioner should be able to answer four questions from the contract fields Section 9.2 fixes. Which of the eleven fields does the deployment claim actually depend on, and is that field non-negotiable, for example does the claim require reset by subtraction executing without a resource-doubling emulation, in which case Table 32 narrows the physical option set to Speck through Sinabs, Xylo through Rockpool, or Loihi 1 through NxTF at the stated compartment cost, and rules out both SpiNNaker generations and the QuantizeML-to-Akida chain even though three of those four chains are otherwise physical. Does the claim depend on the classical rate-matching correspondence Section 8 derives, in which case the Akida route's own transformation of that correspondence, documented in Table 32, disqualifies it regardless of how physical its execution is. Does the claim require on-chip plasticity rather than a fixed, host-trained weight set, in which case Table 13's native-versus-exercised distinction applies, native learning machinery on Loihi 1 and Loihi 2 is not evidence that a specific published route exercises it.

A second set of questions is about the chip and toolchain themselves rather than the field. What generation is being targeted, and does the cited capability record apply to that generation specifically, given that Akida 1.0 and Akida 2.0 already diverge on plasticity and reset status in Table 24. What toolchain version produced the cited result, given that NIR's reset field, Sinabs's mapper behavior, and Lava's built-in neuron semantics have each changed materially across the versions this survey audited. Is the toolchain still maintained, given that the entire Lava, Lava-DL, and NetX stack was archived in 2026 with no successor announced. Is access to the target gated or regionally restricted, given that Loihi requires Neuromorphic Research Community membership and Lynxi's toolchain is documented as effectively unavailable outside mainland China. A route that answers all four contract questions favorably but fails one of these four access questions is not a usable route for a practitioner without that access, independent of its technical merit.

15.3 Minimum Reporting Checklist

A deployment claim this survey would classify without qualification states each of the following. The first eleven items are the contract fields Table 21 fixes; the last three are the boundary, baseline, and evidence-class fields Section 12 and Section 1.3 fix separately from the contract itself.

  • The network graph and which operators it uses, and whether the target's mapper accepted, transformed, split, or rejected any of them.
  • The neuron equations and update order, named as IF, LIF, CUBA-LIF, ALIF, multi-compartment, or another explicitly stated form.
  • Synaptic dynamics and whether delay is a configured parameter, a fixed step, or absent.
  • Threshold and reset semantics, reset-to-zero or reset-by-subtraction, and whether that semantics is native, transformed, or emulated on the named target.
  • The neural code and spike payload, and how many bits or what magnitude range it carries.
  • The input encoder and output decoder, and which of encoding and decoding, if either, executes on-chip rather than on a host.
  • Precision for weights, thresholds, and state, reported separately rather than as one bit-width figure.
  • The time model and termination condition, a static horizon, a per-input stopping rule, a barrier-synchronized tick, or an unbounded event stream.
  • Connectivity, fan-in, fan-out, and routing, including whether the mapped graph fit the target's resource budget without splitting.
  • Plasticity and state persistence, whether a weight update executes on the device and whether state persists across inferences.
  • Required host-side operations, stated explicitly rather than left implicit in a system diagram.
  • The measurement boundary from Section 12.2, chip-only, board-level, host-inclusive, sensor-to-decision, modeled, or physical.
  • The baseline's workload, precision, and boundary, stated rather than implied by table proximity, and the batch size or an explicit single-sample statement.
  • The evidence class from Section 1.3, E1 through E5, assigned to the specific claim being reported rather than to the paper as a whole.

15.4 Specialist Referrals

This survey bounds several topics at the depth deployment claims require and refers a reader who needs more to a named specialist source, repeating here the referrals already placed at their point of use so that a reader who begins at Section 15 has them in one place. For learning theory and algorithms beyond what a deployment comparison needs, see Nunes et al., useful for its breadth across neuron models, coding, and learning, and Khan et al., useful for training methodologies developed considerably further than Section 7 attempts. For local learning rules and open benchmarking of STDP and related mechanisms, see Caviglia et al.. For event-based sensing, representation, and algorithms ahead of the point this survey treats a sensor as a source of a code rather than a device, see Gallego et al., particularly useful for the sensing and preprocessing detail Section 4 and Section 12 do not repeat.

For a data-science comparison of SNN training and simulation frameworks by usability and API design, see Manna et al.. For an extension of the software-implementation view to GPU and RISC-V accelerators and a discussion of incompatible cross-toolchain reporting practices, see Farsa et al.. For NIR's own account of the semantic disagreements it exposes across simulators and hardware back ends, beyond the reset-specific case Section 10 develops in depth, see Pedersen et al.. For hardware-platform surveys with a longer view of circuit and architecture families than Section 5's deployment-contract framing needs, see Schuman et al.'s early integration of models, algorithms, hardware, and applications, Bouvier et al.'s account of SNN algorithms and their hardware-implementation benchmarks, Davies et al.'s synthesis of measured Loihi results, and Shrestha et al.'s connection of model semantics to platform-specific compiler support.

For system-level deployment barriers and scaling challenges beyond the field-by-field contract this survey builds, see Schuman et al.'s later review connecting algorithms and applications to full-stack deployment barriers and Kudithipudi et al.'s account of the challenges in scaling neuromorphic systems and their software ecosystems. For a formal benchmarking framework that separates proxy evaluation from hardware-aware measurement, a distinction Section 12 applies but does not itself standardize, see NeuroBench. For edge-oriented deployment methods organized by proxy-versus-hardware evaluation depth, see Deng et al.'s EdgeSNN survey.

15.5 Conclusion

A deployment claim about a spiking neural network is not one fact but a bundle of eleven separable fields, each of which can be mathematically defined, representable in software, and faithfully executed on a target, or can fail any one of those three tests while passing the other two. This survey's central argument is that the field-by-field contract in Section 9, not a single adjective like portable, supported, or efficient, is the unit a reader should demand before trusting a stated route, a reported timestep count, or a measured energy figure. Applied at that granularity across sixteen sections of derivation and audit, the evidence shows a field that is genuinely more capable than a one-line verdict would suggest, physical silicon reached from the synchronous digital family through Loihi 1, the asynchronous digital family through Speck, the software-programmable many-core family through SpiNNaker 1, and the FPGA and custom-ASIC family through Akida 1.0, with the analog and mixed-signal family's own audited route to BrainScaleS-2 physical only for its non-spiking hagen mode rather than for a spiking network, on-chip learning machinery present but rarely exercised, event-driven substrates executing without any discrete timestep at all, and also a field narrower and more code-dependent than a survey organized around headline accuracy numbers would reveal, a specific and repeatedly confirmed gap between a low-timestep rate-coded conversion method's own published contribution and that same method's own measurement on named hardware, closed in the audited literature only by abandoning the rate code itself or by a later, unrelated paper's engineering effort.

Neither half of that picture is this survey's verdict on whether neuromorphic computing succeeds or fails as a research program. The gap documented in Section 13 is a demonstrated finding about a bounded, named population of papers audited through a stated date, not a general claim about what rate coding, or any other representation, can or cannot achieve on future silicon. What this survey offers instead is the vocabulary and the audit method that let that gap, and the dozens of narrower findings alongside it, be stated precisely enough to check, to cite, and eventually to close.
