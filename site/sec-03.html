3 Neuron and Synapse Dynamics

Section 2 distinguished a value from a state, an event, and a temporal signal without committing to any particular neuron equation. This section makes that commitment. It fixes one staged update that every neuron model used later in the survey instantiates, so that a difference between models is stated as a difference in one named stage rather than as an unrelated new equation. The same staging separates a model's continuous mathematical description from its discrete-time simulation and from its finite-precision hardware realization, three questions that later sections keep asking separately about reset, coding, and execution.

Figure 5 traces the same leak and integration terms back to the idealized parallel RC circuit they come from, showing the integrate-and-fire model as that circuit's zero-leak limit rather than as an unrelated simplification.

The circuit in Figure 5 is passive by itself. Its capacitance and leak conductance account for integration and decay alone, so a spike decision requires an active mechanism outside that branch. Threshold, refractory behavior, and reset are therefore separate mechanisms added to the circuit, not further passive RC behavior. Section 3.1 gives the threshold its place in the staged update, Section 3.3 places refractory behavior inside the adaptation mechanism, and Section 3.4 derives the two reset rules in full. The zero-leak limit \(g_L=0\), equivalently \(R_m=1/g_L\rightarrow\infty\), is the electrical statement of the integrate-and-fire model derived below, the point at which the circuit retains every unit of charge it receives rather than discharging any of it toward \(E_L\). The informal phrase ‘IF has no resistor’ is acceptable shorthand for this limit, not a substitute for it. Current and voltage also carry different meanings across the three settings this survey keeps separate throughout. In the circuit above they denote quantities in an idealized model. In a software simulator or a digital accelerator they are ordinarily dimensionless or fixed-point state variables, often realized as integer accumulators, that satisfy the same recurrence without representing literal amperes or volts. In an analog or mixed-signal substrate they may instead be physical circuit quantities that a probe could measure directly. Section 3.5 returns to this separation in general, for the discretization and hardware realization of every neuron model this section derives, not only the RC-derived one.

3.1 A Common Staged State Update

A spiking neuron in layer \(l\) receives events from presynaptic units \(j\), maintains an internal state, and emits an event \(z_i^l[t] \in \{0,1\}\) when that state crosses a threshold. Rather than writing this as one overloaded recurrence, this survey factors it into three stages, following the notation fixed for the whole article. A synaptic stage \(\mathcal{S}^l\) turns incoming events into a synaptic state or filtered current \(q_i^l[t]\). A membrane stage \(\mathcal{M}^l\) combines that current with the previous membrane state \(u_i^l[t-1]\), an optional adaptation state \(a_i^l[t-1]\), and a bias \(b_i^l\) into a pre-reset candidate \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]\). A threshold function \(\mathcal{H}\) compares that candidate against a threshold \(\theta_i^l[t]\) to decide whether an event is emitted. A reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\) then turns the pre-reset candidate, the emitted event, and the threshold into the post-reset state \(u_i^l[t]\) that stage \(\mathcal{M}^l\) reads at the next step.

\[ q_i^l[t] = \mathcal{S}^l\!\left(q_i^l[t-1],\{z_j^{l-1}[t-d_{ij}^l]\}_j,\{w_{ij}^l\}_j\right), \] \[ \tilde{u}_i^l[t] = \mathcal{M}^l\!\left(u_i^l[t-1],q_i^l[t],a_i^l[t-1],b_i^l\right), \qquad z_i^l[t] = \mathcal{H}\!\left(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]-\theta_i^l[t]\right), \] \[ u_i^l[t] = \mathcal{R}^l\!\left(\tilde{u}_i^l[t],z_i^l[t],\theta_i^l[t]\right). \]

Two indexing conventions hold throughout this section. First, the update order is fixed as written. \(\mathcal{S}^l\) is evaluated before \(\mathcal{M}^l\), and \(\mathcal{H}\) is evaluated on the pre-reset candidate \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]\), never on the already-reset \(u_i^l[t-1]\). Second, \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]\) always denotes the value before the reset stage has acted and \(u_i^l[t]\) always denotes the value after it, so a later equation that writes \(u_i^l[t]\) is referring to the quantity that stage \(\mathcal{M}^l\) will read at step \(t+1\). This vocabulary is a bookkeeping device, not a claim about implementation. It does not assert that every simulator or every chip literally computes four separate intermediate arrays in this order; Section 3.5 returns to what a hardware or software implementation is free to change and what it is not.

Framing every model this way lets the rest of the section ask one question per model. Which stage does this neuron modify relative to the plainest case, and does it introduce a new persistent state variable or only a new coefficient inside an existing stage? The plainest case, developed next, uses a trivial \(\mathcal{S}^l\) with no memory of its own, an \(\mathcal{M}^l\) with no decay, and a fixed threshold, which is the discrete-time abstraction that the classical literature on spiking neurons and network models builds on [maass1997].

3.2 The Neuron Model Family

Integrate-and-fire (IF) is the baseline instance of the staged update. Its synaptic stage has no memory, computing \(q_i^l[t]=\sum_j w_{ij}^l z_j^{l-1}[t-d_{ij}^l]\) fresh at every step with nothing carried over from \(q_i^l[t-1]\). Its membrane stage adds that current to the previous state with no decay, \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]=u_i^l[t-1]+q_i^l[t]+b_i^l\), and its threshold is fixed, \(\theta_i^l[t]=\theta_i^l\). The mechanism is deliberately the simplest possible instance of the three stages. Its mathematical effect is that every unit of input charge that ever arrives is eventually spent as output, so a finite-window spike count is not systematically damped relative to the current driving it. This is the property the classical ANN-to-SNN conversion literature relies on when it equates a spike count with a target rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation, and it is the reason that literature targets IF neurons specifically rather than a leaky variant [cao2015], [rueckauer]. The trade-off is that IF has no mechanism for forgetting old input, which conversion accepts as a feature but which direct training on temporally structured data usually treats as a limitation. The deployment consequence is that IF's simplicity makes it the easiest model to realize with a single accumulator register; the evidence boundary is that this is a mechanism-level statement about the target of a mapping, not a deployment measurement on its own.

Leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) keeps IF's trivial synaptic stage and modifies only the membrane stage, introducing a per-layer leak factor \(\alpha^l\) with \(0<\alpha^l<1\) that this survey treats as a new local symbol, distinct from the activation or threshold scale \(\lambda^l\) that later sections use for conversion, giving \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]=\alpha^l u_i^l[t-1]+q_i^l[t]+b_i^l\). The membrane now relaxes toward its resting value between inputs instead of holding charge indefinitely. The trade-off is exactly the property IF avoids. A unit of charge that arrives and is not soon followed by enough further input to cross threshold is partially forgotten rather than eventually spent, so a naive spike count over a short window is no longer an unbiased read of the driving current. This is one reason the rate-coding conversion literature above targets IF rather than LIF even though LIF is the more common default in general spiking-network practice. LIF is also available as a selectable leaky or leak-disabled mode on Sinabs-backed DYNAP-CNN and Speck deployments, where enabling the leak requires an accompanying bias term and an externally configured slow-clock rate [sinabs-docs].

Current-based LIF (CUBA-LIF) is the first model in this family that gives the synaptic stage genuine memory. Instead of the instantaneous sum used by IF and LIF, \(q_i^l[t]\) is itself a leaky integrator of the incoming weighted events, \(q_i^l[t]=\beta^l q_i^l[t-1]+\sum_j w_{ij}^l z_j^{l-1}[t-d_{ij}^l]\), and the membrane stage integrates this filtered current the same way LIF integrates the raw sum, \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]=\alpha^l u_i^l[t-1]+q_i^l[t]+b_i^l\). The mechanism adds a second decaying state variable, so a single presynaptic event spreads its influence over several steps instead of landing on the membrane in one step. The consequence for a hardware target is that this two-stage filter is exactly what a single-compartment current-based primitive is built to execute directly. CUBA-LIF is documented as Loihi's native default neuron dynamics and is exposed in Lava-DL as the SLAYER current-based integration block [shrestha2023], [lava-dl-slayer-docs]. The same synaptic-stage filter is sPyNNaker's documented default on first-generation SpiNNaker's PyNN front end, whose built-in cell type IF_curr_exp pairs a current-based synapse with an exponentially decaying synaptic current rather than the instantaneous sum IF and LIF use, which places SpiNNaker's default neuron in this row rather than under LIF above [rhodes2018-spynnaker]. The trade-off is the same forgetting property LIF introduces, now applied twice, once to the synaptic state and once to the membrane state, so a CUBA-LIF spike count is a still less direct read of any single driving input than an LIF count. Its native single-compartment reset defaults to reset-to-zero, a fact Section 3.4 returns to directly.

Adaptive LIF (ALIF) leaves the synaptic and membrane stages as in IF or LIF but couples the threshold itself to the neuron's own recent spiking through the adaptation state \(a_i^l[t]\) that the common update already reserves. A representative discrete form raises the threshold after every spike and lets it relax back toward a baseline between spikes, \[ \theta_i^l[t]=\theta_0^l+\kappa^l a_i^l[t-1], \qquad a_i^l[t]=\gamma^l a_i^l[t-1]+z_i^l[t], \] with \(0<\gamma^l<1\) a second local decay constant and \(\kappa^l\) a coupling gain, following the discrete adaptive formulation used for long-memory spiking networks [bellec2018]. The adaptation update runs alongside the reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\), triggered by the same emitted event \(z_i^l[t]\), rather than being one of the three canonical stages itself. The semantic effect is that a constant input no longer produces a constant firing rate; the rate falls as the threshold climbs, giving the neuron a longer effective memory than a single leak constant can provide without an explicit multiplicative gate. The trade-off is a second persistent state variable per neuron and a nonstationary threshold that later sections must track wherever they otherwise treat \(\theta_i^l\) as fixed. Native support for this mechanism is uneven. It is documented as a directly implemented analog adaptation current on BrainScaleS-2 [brainscales-pehle2022], while on Loihi it is realized through the multi-compartment mechanism introduced next, at the cost of a second compartment per adaptive neuron [nxtf2021].

Multi-compartment neurons generalize the single state triple \((q_i^l,u_i^l,a_i^l)\) into several coupled instances indexed by a compartment \(c\), each running its own copy of the staged update, with the output of one compartment entering another compartment's synaptic or membrane stage as an additional term. Only a designated somatic compartment evaluates \(\mathcal{H}\) and emits \(z_i^l[t]\); the other compartments exist purely to hold auxiliary state that a single-compartment primitive cannot represent. In the deployment-relevant literature this survey reviews, the mechanism is used less for dendritic realism than as an engineering workaround. On Loihi, a second compartment is documented as the way both a subtractive reset and spike-frequency adaptation are recovered from a hardware primitive whose native single-compartment behavior does neither, at the declared cost of doubling compartment allocation per affected neuron [nxtf2021]. On BrainScaleS-2, multi-compartment structure is instead documented as native dendritic computation with genuine branch-dependent behavior, not only a workaround for a missing single-compartment feature [brainscales-pehle2022]. The deployment consequence is that a model description naming "a multi-compartment neuron" is incomplete until it states which of these two roles the extra compartment plays and at what resource cost, a distinction Section 10 revisits as part of the deployable contract's neuron-equation field.

The staged update as written assumes a binary event \(z_i^l[t]\in\{0,1\}\). Nothing in \(\mathcal{S}^l\), \(\mathcal{M}^l\), or \(\mathcal{R}^l\) requires this. The threshold function \(\mathcal{H}\) can instead emit a signed or graded payload \(p_{ik}\), so that a neuron reports a magnitude and a sign rather than a bare occurrence. This is a change to what the emission stage produces and to what the next layer's synaptic stage \(\mathcal{S}^{l+1}\) consumes, not a change to which of the five dynamical variants above is running underneath it; the survey therefore treats signed or graded emission as a payload property, developed further as a neural-code question in Section 4, rather than as a sixth neuron family. The distinction has a deployment motivation. A unipolar event cannot represent a negative activation, and batch normalization together with residual connections routinely produce substantial negative mass in a pre-activation distribution. Software mechanisms that keep a binary payload while still representing a signed quantity include tracking a running net spike count and gating negative spikes so the effective rate cannot fall below zero [snm], and maintaining a shadow membrane potential that fires a compensating negative spike whenever it diverges from the true potential past a negative threshold [zou2023negspike], [ijcai2025negspike]. A graded integer payload carrying a signed magnitude directly in the spike message is documented for Loihi 2's microcode-enabled neurons [shrestha2023]; this survey has not located an equivalent documented primitive elsewhere in the sources reviewed for this section, which is a statement about what has been located, not a claim that no other platform can do so.

The present discussion stops after the discrete state variables that ANN-to-SNN conversion and the neuromorphic targets covered later in this survey actually use. For a fuller treatment of continuous biophysical spike generation, see the Hodgkin-Huxley conductance model [hodgkin1952], which is particularly useful for the ionic mechanism underlying an action potential, and for the diversity of observed firing patterns arising from richer bifurcation dynamics, see the Izhikevich two-variable model [izhikevich2003]. This literature treats both as a ceiling on biological realism rather than as a deployment target.

3.3 Synaptic State, Delay, and Recurrent Input

The synaptic stage \(\mathcal{S}^l\) is where the common update already accounts for filtering, delay, and recurrence, so this subsection names what varies inside it rather than introducing new stages. Filtering is the difference between IF or LIF's instantaneous sum and CUBA-LIF's leaky current developed above; a model family can therefore be extended with a different synaptic filter, for instance a second-order or dual-exponential current, by changing only \(\mathcal{S}^l\)'s internal recursion, without touching \(\mathcal{M}^l\), \(\mathcal{H}\), or \(\mathcal{R}^l\).

Delay enters through the index \(d_{ij}^l\) already present in \(\mathcal{S}^l\)'s argument list, which shifts a presynaptic event \(z_j^{l-1}\) by \(d_{ij}^l\) steps before it contributes to \(q_i^l[t]\). Two properties of \(d_{ij}^l\) matter for later sections and must not be conflated. First, whether delay is a configured per-connection parameter or a fixed architectural consequence of the time model itself is a separate question, developed once Section 4 fixes the vocabulary for hardware ticks and asynchronous propagation; this subsection only fixes that \(d_{ij}^l\) can be measured in discrete steps, hardware ticks, or physical time, and that a citation using it must state which. Second, a per-connection configurable delay is a documented capability on some targets and not others. SpiNNaker's software-defined synapse model exposes a programmable per-synapse delay, and a training pipeline built around Loihi 2's native delay mechanism learns per-synapse delays of up to several dozen steps and reports feedforward latency and dynamic energy for the resulting network on physical hardware [spinnaker-neuron-lab], [meszaros-2025-loihi2-delays]. Neither example licenses a claim about delay support on a platform not named in that source.

Recurrent state is not a new stage either. A recurrent connection is a synaptic weight \(w_{ij}^l\) whose presynaptic index \(j\) ranges over units in the same layer, or over a later layer through a delay \(d_{ij}^l\) of at least one step so that the update in Section 3.1 remains causal. The adaptation state \(a_i^l[t]\) introduced for ALIF is a second, complementary way a neuron's own history feeds back into itself, operating through the threshold rather than through an explicit recurrent synapse. Refractory behavior, a period after a spike during which a neuron cannot fire again, is a boundary case of the same adaptation mechanism rather than a variable this survey reserves a new symbol for. It is realized by transiently raising \(\theta_i^l[t]\) to a value \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]\) cannot cross, using the same coupling that produces spike-frequency adaptation, rather than by suppressing the update or the emission stage directly.

3.4 Reset Semantics

The reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\) is the last of the four functions in Section 3.1's update, and it is the one this survey treats as load-bearing rather than incidental, because the correctness argument behind rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion depends on which reset rule it implements. Two rules recur across every model in Section 3.2; the literature and this survey call them reset-to-zero and reset-by-subtraction, with hard reset and soft reset as the common aliases, which later sections use only where a cited source or toolchain names the rule that way.

Reset-to-zero clamps the post-reset state to a fixed baseline, usually zero, whenever an event is emitted, and otherwise leaves the pre-reset candidate unchanged, as follows. \[ u_i^l[t]=\bigl(1-z_i^l[t]\bigr)\,\tilde{u}_i^l[t]+z_i^l[t]\,u_{\mathrm{reset}}^l . \] Reset-by-subtraction instead removes only the threshold that was just crossed, carrying any remainder forward into the next step, as follows. \[ u_i^l[t]=\tilde{u}_i^l[t]-z_i^l[t]\,\theta_i^l[t]. \] Both rules are read directly off the general reset term \(r_i^l[t]\) that the notation reserves for this purpose. Define the residual charge at a spike event as \(r_i^l[t]=\tilde{u}_i^l[t]-\theta_i^l[t]\), the amount by which the pre-reset candidate exceeded threshold. Reset-to-zero discards \(r_i^l[t]\) outright; reset-by-subtraction retains it inside \(u_i^l[t]\), where the membrane stage will integrate it again at the next step. This is the entire mechanical difference between the two rules, and it is the reason they are not interchangeable implementation details of the same neuron.

The semantic consequence of discarding \(r_i^l[t]\) is not zero-mean noise that a longer simulation window averages away. Because \(r_i^l[t]\) depends on the input driving the neuron rather than on any random process, reset-to-zero introduces a systematic, input-dependent bias in the resulting spike count relative to the current that produced it, one that compounds across the layers of a deep network. The derivation behind rate-coded conversion shows that reset-by-subtraction removes this bias term entirely, leaving only a bounded discretization error that shrinks as the simulation horizon \(T\) grows, rather than a persistent offset that does not [rueckauer]. This is a cited asymptotic claim about the discrete-time model developed in Section 7, not a general property of every deployed system with finite \(T\); a numeric result reported under one target's discretization and precision should not be read as identical to a result on another target unless that other target implements the same reset stage over the same finite window.

The deployment consequence is that reset is not a property a toolchain name can be trusted to fix once. It must be tracked separately at the neuron equation, at the framework or conversion default, at the exporter or importer, and at the hardware execution primitive, because each of these boundaries can implement a different rule from its neighbor. NIR illustrates the first boundary directly. NIR 1.0.8's current schema encodes a post-spike reset to a fixed value and has no field for reset-by-subtraction, so a missing or null reset value is materialized as reset-to-zero rather than left as reset-by-subtraction [neuroir-docs], [nir-neuron-primitives-source]. SpikingJelly 2.0.0rc1 illustrates the second and third boundaries together. Its generic neuron constructors default to reset-to-zero, while its own rate-coding ANN-to-SNN conversion recipe instead instantiates reset-by-subtraction neurons by default, and both its NIR exporter and its Lava exporter reject that converted default rather than silently reinterpreting it as a fixed-value reset [a18-sourcecode-comparison], [spj], [spikingjelly-source-tonir], [spj-lava]. NxTF illustrates the hardware boundary. It recovers reset-by-subtraction on Loihi 1, whose native single-compartment primitive resets to zero, by allocating a second compartment that absorbs the residual and inhibits the first back to the correct level, which the source documents as doubling compartment allocation for every neuron configured this way; this is an emulated capability, not a native one, and the same source reports physical accuracy, execution time, and energy measurements on a Nahuku32 board, with the qualification that not every benchmark in that paper used the soft-reset configuration [nxtf], [nxtf-text]. Sinabs and Speck illustrate a case where the mapping is documented as native rather than emulated. Sinabs 3.1.3 defaults ANN conversion to reset-by-subtraction, and its DYNAP-CNN mapper carries that behavior into Speck's own configurable reset mode; other parts of the same route, including pooling and weight precision, remain transformed even where reset itself is preserved [sinabs], [sinabs-docs]. A custom-microcode route for reset-by-subtraction on Loihi 2 outside the public Lava and NetX toolchains is undocumented in the sources reviewed for this survey; an absence of documentation supports neither a claim that such a route exists nor a claim that it cannot.

None of these examples licenses a general verdict about a framework or a chip family. Each states a capability status, native, transformed, or emulated, three of the six capability statuses Section 6.5 fixes, scoped to a named version and a named target, together with whatever evidence class its accompanying measurement supports. Section 10 returns to reset as one of the fields a deployable contract must state explicitly, and Section 11 returns to the exporter and importer boundaries above as instances of a general software-boundary schema.

3.5 Continuous Dynamics, Discretization, and Hardware Realization

Every membrane equation in Section 3.2 can be read at three distinct levels, and this survey keeps them separate on purpose. The first is a continuous dynamical description in physical or modeled time \(\tau\). LIF's membrane, for instance, is conventionally written as a first-order relaxation toward a resting potential under an input current \(I_i^l(\tau)\), with a membrane time constant \(\tau_m^l\) governing the relaxation rate. The second level is the numerical discretization that turns this differential equation into the recurrence \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]=\alpha^l u_i^l[t-1]+q_i^l[t]+b_i^l\) used above, at a chosen step size \(\Delta t\). A forward-Euler discretization gives \(\alpha^l=1-\Delta t/\tau_m^l\), valid only while \(\Delta t\) is small enough to keep this quantity inside \((0,1)\); the exact discretization of the same linear equation instead gives \(\alpha^l=\exp(-\Delta t/\tau_m^l)\), which remains a valid decay factor for any \(\Delta t\). These are two different, individually defensible ways of turning the same continuous model into the same discrete stage, and they do not in general produce identical trajectories for the same \(\tau_m^l\) and \(\Delta t\). The third level is hardware realization. A target executes the chosen discrete recurrence with a finite-precision state and weight representation, using bit widths \(b_u\), \(b_w\), and \(b_\theta\) that are independent design choices from one another, and it advances that recurrence according to its own tick semantics, whether a fixed hardware quantity, a configurable parameter, or a scheduling convention layered on an otherwise asynchronous substrate.

Keeping these three levels distinct matters because a claim that is true at one level does not transfer automatically to the next. Accepting a graph that specifies \(\tau_m^l\) and a reset rule does not by itself establish which discretization a given backend applies to it, and a cross-platform intermediate representation is explicit that it declares idealized primitives without constraining backend-specific integration order, spike timing, reset timing, or numerical quantization, so a syntactically accepted graph does not establish execution-equivalent dynamics across two different backends [neuroir-docs], [pedersen-2024-nir]. A tick duration illustrates the same separation from the hardware side. SpiNNaker's software convention sets a default tick near one millisecond to match a biological time reference, a scheduling convention rather than a hardware limit, since finer ticks trade a proportionally slower real-time factor for finer resolution [spinnaker-1ms], while an asynchronous barrier-synchronized target advances a fixed-size algorithmic step whose own wall-clock duration is not fixed by the model at all [loihi1]. Section 4 fixes the full vocabulary needed to compare an algorithmic step, a hardware tick, and wall-clock latency without conflating them, and Section 12 returns to how execution families differ at exactly this third level.

3.6 Semantics That Become Contract Fields

The staged update, the neuron model family it generates, and the reset derivation above are not only a vocabulary for this section. They fix the semantics that Section 10 later records as explicit fields of a deployable SNN contract, because a route that changes any of them without saying so has changed what the network computes rather than merely how it is executed. Those semantics are the neuron equations and their update order, fixed in Section 3.1; synaptic dynamics and delay, fixed in Section 3.3; threshold and reset, fixed in Section 3.4; spike payload, raised in Section 3.2 wherever emission carries more than a bare occurrence; and the precision assigned separately to weights, thresholds, and state, raised in Section 3.5. Time itself, the remaining quantity this section has deliberately used only through the symbols \(\tau\), \(\Delta t\), and \(\tau_{\mathrm{tick}}\) without comparing execution families, is defined in full in Section 4, together with the neural codes that decide what a sequence of these staged updates is understood to represent.
