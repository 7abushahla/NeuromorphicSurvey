16 Applications and Deployment Evidence

This section inventories the deployment evidence located during this survey's research passes, organized by the workload each case addresses rather than by the vendor or chip it targets. A platform inventory belongs to Section 13 and a route trace belongs to Section 14; this section asks a different question, what was actually run, on what input, and measured how. For every case, this survey records input provenance, the learning route that produced the deployed weights, direct training or ANN-to-SNN conversion in the sense Section 5 fixes, the neural code carried at inference, the portion of the pipeline that executes on the neuromorphic device itself rather than on a host, the comparator baseline, the measurement boundary from Section 15.2, what the source states about reproducibility, and the claim-level evidence class from Section 5.4's E1 through E5 scheme. Direct-training deployments already carry a dedicated treatment in Section 6, including the on-chip-learning capability survey of that section's Table 12, and several cases below cross-reference that treatment rather than repeating it; conversion deployments receive the same workload-level treatment here for the first time. Both routes appear side by side in every subsection, because the workload, not the route, is the organizing axis.

16.1 Vision Workloads

Section 8 already noted a gap this subsection returns to. None of the finite-time-error mechanisms and none of the low-latency conversion interventions surveyed in Sections 8 and 9 report execution on physical neuromorphic silicon in their own papers; every case in this subsection that reaches silicon under a rate code does so through a separate, later engineering paper applying a documented toolchain to a classical or QCFS-lineage correspondence, not through the algorithm paper that first proposed the correspondence. The NxTF compiler of Rueckauer et al. illustrates the pattern from two directions within one paper. It carries a four-layer convolutional network trained conventionally and converted through SNN Toolbox's rate-coded, reset-by-subtraction pipeline to physical Loihi 1 for MNIST and, separately, an off-the-shelf MobileNet converted the same way for CIFAR-10, and it also carries a SLAYER-trained convolutional network for N-MNIST and DVS Gestures on the identical physical chip, a direct-training result rather than a converted one. Massa et al. deploy a gesture-recognition network to physical Loihi 1 through the same SNN Toolbox and NxSDK route, but their pipeline first accumulates DVS events into frames before conversion, so the reported accuracy and per-frame latency answer a question about a frame-accumulated input, not about native event input, a distinction Section 15.1's first row already flags. Patiño-Saucedo et al. carry the same two-route pattern onto SpiNNaker, converting a LeNet-style network through SNN Toolbox for MNIST and separately training a network directly by spatio-temporal backpropagation (STBP) for N-MNIST on the same 48-chip SpiNNaker-103 machine, predating the QCFS lineage by construction. Chen, Gao, and Fu's Cerebron, Panchapakesan, Fang, and Li's SyncNN, and Stromatias et al.'s spiking deep belief network extend the same rate-coded, converted-network pattern to physical FPGAs and to SpiNNaker respectively, each reporting accuracy alongside latency, throughput, or power measured on real boards rather than synthesis estimates. Li et al.'s FireFly v2 accelerator reports the highest clock frequency among the FPGA accelerators surveyed here, though its own record does not disclose whether the evaluated networks were converted or directly trained. Read together, this cluster is the clearest physical evidence in the audited corpus that a rate-coded, IF-neuron convolutional network, trained by ordinary means and converted through a documented toolchain, reaches physical silicon, and every instance of it that does so is an engineering paper built on top of an already-published correspondence rather than the correspondence's own proposing paper.

A second cluster abandons the rate code at the point of conversion itself, already established as a code-family and intervention-family case in Sections 4 and 9. Lenz, Orchard, and Sheik's Quartz converts to a time-to-first-spike code and reaches physical Loihi 1 for MNIST and CIFAR-10, an accuracy-energy trade against rate-coded baselines rather than an unconditional improvement (Section 4.3). Brehove et al.'s Sigma-Delta Neural Network conversion targets Loihi 2's native graded-spike channel for a frame-based workload, and Adaptive Fission's population-coding transformation reports, inside its own hardware table, unmodified QCFS and SRP baseline configurations measured on the same physical accelerator, the one documented instance in this survey's audited evidence in which an unmodified rate-coded QCFS-lineage output has been measured on physical silicon, achieved by a different paper's non-rate-coded contribution rather than by QCFS's or SRP's own authors. Section 17 returns to what this pattern implies about the two learning populations; the point recorded here is only that abandoning the rate code, in every confirmed vision case in this subsection, is what carried a low-latency conversion method to physical silicon.

A third cluster processes event-native vision directly, without a frame-accumulation stage, and is dominated by direct training. Amir et al.'s TrueNorth gesture-recognition system reads a live DVS128 camera feed straight through to a decision with no host-side frame reconstruction, the strongest sensor-to-decision case in this subsection. Yao et al.'s Speck-based dynamic-computing chip and Richter et al.'s Speck1, already introduced in Section 6 for their surrogate-gradient training route, both carry directly trained networks to physical event-native silicon, and Richter et al.'s own head-to-head comparison of BPTT training against an ANN-to-SNN converted baseline on the identical chip is the clearest same-chip route comparison this survey found for a vision workload. The HiAER-Spike and CRI project converts a SpikingJelly-trained network to physical FPGA-based neuromorphic hardware, one of a small number of SpikingJelly-trained networks in the audited evidence that reach any physical chip, cross-referenced from Section 6. Du et al.'s Temporal Flexibility deploys a single weight set trained with a mixed-timestep augmentation to a physical Speck2e device, reporting on-chip accuracy alongside a measured simulator-to-chip difference, an E2 result already introduced in Section 4 for its timestep-free, event-driven execution rather than a discrete per-layer schedule. Esser et al.'s EEDN trains a network directly under TrueNorth's own hardware constraints, binary neurons and trinary synapses via constrained backpropagation, mapping natively onto the chip rather than converting a separately trained ANN, and reports accuracy, throughput, and power measured on physical silicon across several frame-based vision benchmarks. uBrain's fabricated 40-nanometer ASIC is explicitly designed to ease ANN-to-SNN conversion, reporting power and energy per classification on a radar hand-gesture dataset and on a converted MNIST network from the same fabricated die, and Frenkel et al.'s ODIN reports on-chip local plasticity for a small MNIST network at measured energy per synaptic operation, also on fabricated silicon. For the FPGA accelerators and the single-board neuromorphic systems below whose papers report the programmed device measured on its development board without stating what share of the measurement a driving host contributed, Table 29 assigns the board-level boundary by convention, and a reader who needs the host share should consult the cited paper directly.

Frame-based and event-native vision together supply most of this survey's physical deployment evidence, and every route, rate-coded conversion, non-rate-coded conversion, and direct training, is represented on real silicon somewhere in Table 29. Section 16.2 turns to workloads defined by a temporal signal rather than a static image, where the case count audited here is smaller and skews further toward direct training.

16.2 Audio and Temporal Workloads

Mészáros et al.'s EventProp pipeline trains a leaky integrate-and-fire network with learned synaptic delays through an exact spike-time gradient and deploys it to a physical single-chip Loihi 2 for keyword spotting on the Spiking Heidelberg Digits and Spiking Speech Commands benchmarks, already introduced in Section 6 for its training mechanism; the workload-relevant addition here is that both benchmarks are genuinely temporal classification tasks, not frame-accumulated vision relabeled as audio. Bos and Muir's Xylo Audio 2 deployment for the Aloha keyword-spotting benchmark follows a commercial, direct-training-first pipeline through Rockpool, reporting accuracy, dynamic power, and dynamic energy per inference on physical, purpose-built silicon compared against GPU, CPU, and other embedded baselines. Carpegna, Savino, and Di Carlo's Spiker+ framework, already noted in Section 16.1 for its configurable reset, evaluates the same generated accelerator on the Spiking Heidelberg Dataset and on AudioMNIST alongside its MNIST result, reporting accuracy, latency, and power from physical FPGA synthesis and deployment. Frenkel and Indiveri's ReckOn is a single fabricated processor evaluated across three different workloads, DVS Gestures, Spiking Heidelberg Digits, and a synthetic rodent-navigation task, using an eligibility-propagation rule for on-chip learning and reporting accuracy and energy per synaptic operation from five fabricated dies; its Spiking Heidelberg Digits result is the case recorded here, and its other two workloads recur in Table 30. Ortone et al.'s tactile-localization system belongs in this subsection rather than in vision because its represented variable is a continuously varying touch signal rather than a static image, and it is a hand-designed circuit, not a trained network in the direct-training or conversion sense; a Bragg-grating-based e-skin drives a population of DYNAP-SE analog neurons for tactile localization, reporting localization error against a software baseline alongside resting and median inference power measured on physical, mixed-signal silicon. Biosignal decoding, electromyography-based gesture classification and electroencephalography-based seizure prediction, is documented in Section 6 (Section 6.2) at the same physical evidence class; those cases fall outside the measurement-index population this subsection draws its workload-level fields from, so they are referenced rather than re-tabulated here.

Every case in this subsection is either directly trained or hand-designed with local plasticity; none is an ANN-to-SNN conversion. This is a narrower population than vision's, and this survey does not read the absence of a converted audio or temporal case here as evidence that no such conversion has ever been deployed, only that this survey's audited corpus did not locate one.

16.3 Control and Robotics

Robotics is the workload domain in which this survey's audited evidence is most heavily direct-trained or hand-designed, and in which a single case most often combines several evidence-relevant properties at once, closed-loop actuation, a non-rate code, and a comparison against a conventional embedded baseline. Arfa et al. combine hardware-aware fine-tuning with eight-bit quantization to deploy directly trained spiking Q-networks, reinforcement-learning policies for CartPole and Acrobot, to physical SpiNNaker2, already introduced in Section 6 for its quantization mechanism; the workload here is closed-loop control rather than classification, and the policy's action at each tick is itself the deployed output. Tang et al.'s mapless-navigation policy, introduced in Section 5 as a hybrid method that applies a Loihi-specific integer weight-rescaling step after gradient-based training, is measured on physical Loihi hardware at a reported reduction in energy per inference relative to a conventional deep-reinforcement-learning baseline on an embedded GPU. A separate, industrial peg-in-hole force-control policy, also introduced in Section 6, trains a spiking reinforcement-learning policy end to end in simulation and ports it to both physical Loihi 1 and Loihi 2 for a KUKA robotic arm, reporting latency and dynamic energy against named CPU and GPU comparators on the same insertion task. Cheng and Nikolić's goalkeeper robot, already placed in Section 6's hand-designed local-plasticity cluster, maps a live DVS camera stream through SpiNNaker to a servomotor in a closed loop disconnected from any host PC, reporting response latency, interception accuracy against balls moving up to one meter per second, and maximum system power, the clearest sensor-to-decision robotics case in the audited evidence. Tang et al.'s head-direction SLAM circuit, from the same hand-designed cluster, runs on physical Loihi and reports dynamic power roughly two orders of magnitude below a GMapping baseline on a conventional CPU at comparable localization accuracy. Pei et al.'s Tianjic chip demonstrates a hybrid ANN and SNN architecture in a single physical chip driving a real-time robotics demonstration spanning object detection, tracking, voice recognition, obstacle avoidance, and balance control, reporting throughput and power-efficiency ratios against GPU baselines from a hardware-in-the-loop demonstration rather than a benchmark table. CLP-SNN, already discussed in Section 6's on-chip-learning survey (Table 12), performs online continual learning directly on physical Loihi 2 for a few-shot robotic-vision task, reporting latency and energy against a Jetson Orin Nano baseline; it sits at the boundary between vision and robotics because its workload is object recognition, but its deployment claim is about on-chip adaptation for a robotic platform rather than a fixed classifier.

Every closed-loop case in this cluster, the goalkeeper, the SLAM circuit, and Tianjic's demonstration, reports either a hand-designed circuit or a hybrid architecture rather than a network converted from a pretrained ANN by the classical or QCFS-lineage correspondence Sections 7 through 9 formalize. The reinforcement-learning cases, Arfa et al., Tang et al.'s navigation policy, and the force-control policy, are directly trained by gradient descent. No case in this subsection is a rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion of the kind Section 16.1 documents for vision, a gap this survey states rather than explains, since a causal account of why the two learning branches concentrate differently across workloads is not something the audited evidence itself supports.

16.4 Scientific and Industrial Workloads

This survey's audited evidence for scientific and industrial sensing outside vision, audio, and closed-loop control is thin, two confirmed cases, and this subsection states that population size honestly rather than padding it with cases that belong more naturally elsewhere. Imam and Cleland deploy a hand-designed olfactory-bulb-model circuit that learns odor identity online through one-shot local plasticity on physical Loihi silicon, with no backpropagation and no ANN-to-SNN conversion step anywhere in the pipeline, reporting latency and energy per sniff against a wind-tunnel chemosensor dataset. This survey records a caveat the audited evidence itself carries. A 2023 replication study by Dennler, van Schaik, and Schmuker found that the underlying chemosensor dataset suffers from sensor drift and a non-randomized acquisition protocol, and that a simple hash-table baseline matches or exceeds the circuit's reported accuracy once the data is split properly. The hardware latency and energy measurement itself is not invalidated by this finding, but the algorithmic and scientific claim attached to it is substantially undercut, and this survey reports the measurement at E1 while flagging the accuracy claim as unresolved rather than folding the two together into one verdict. Andrei et al. extend Few-Spikes conversion to complex-valued layers for an unfolded multidimensional harmonic-retrieval network, a signal-processing workload rather than a classification benchmark, and map the result to a physical single-chip SpiNNaker2 board, reporting board power against a Jetson Xavier baseline for a serialized temporal Few-Spikes code rather than a spike-count rate estimate, already introduced in Section 9 for its non-rate-coded conversion.

This survey's own research passes did not locate a physically or near-physically deployed case of industrial anomaly detection, large-scale neural simulation informing a deployment claim, or an emerging sequence or transformer-style SNN workload with a matching measurement-index record. Their absence here is a statement about what this survey's audited corpus contains, not a claim that such deployments do not exist.

Whether this distribution reflects something about the workloads themselves, about which communities publish hardware results, or about which correspondence a given code admits, is a question this section does not answer. Section 17 takes up the two-population question this evidence bears on directly, states the counts and the falsifier search this table's cases were drawn from, and separates what the evidence demonstrates from what remains an interpretation.
