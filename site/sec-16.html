16 What the Evidence Shows

Section 1 asked whether a recent low-timestep ANN-to-SNN conversion method's own deployment route reaches named physical silicon with measurement, or whether deployment instead proceeds through an older pipeline, a directly trained model, or a platform-native code. Section 15.5 closed by naming this section as the place that question is answered, with counts, a falsifier, and counterexamples rather than an assumed verdict. This section does that under an explicit counting policy stated before every number, and it keeps a demonstrated finding, a count or a verified claim, separate from a causal or sociological reading of that finding. Sections 16.1 through 16.4 report only what the audited evidence demonstrates. Section 16.5 states, and marks as such, the interpretations that evidence does not itself adjudicate.

16.1 Population Counts

Every count in this subsection is computed from data/evidence-papers.json, the audited paper population underlying this survey's conversion-track evidence, current to 16 September 2026, the same audit cutoff already named for the falsifier search in Section 9.7. Each of its 78 records carries a population field with three possible values, algorithm, deployment, or both, and a family field naming the method cluster the record belongs to. The counting rule is the field value itself, tallied by population and cross-tabulated by family, with no manual reclassification. Population and evidence class are independent axes by construction, exactly as Section 5.4 already establishes for individual claims generally, so a record tagged algorithm can still carry hardware evidence up to E3 or E4 without being tagged deployment, and a handful of the algorithm-tagged records in this corpus do exactly that.

An exclusive count partitions the 78 records into three disjoint classes. Algorithm-only is 42 records that propose a method without the proposing paper itself reporting that method's execution on named hardware. Deployment-only is 32 records that execute an existing or hardware-oriented design rather than introducing a new one. Both is 4 records that introduce a method and report that same method's own physical hardware execution. An inclusive count instead asks two separate questions of the same corpus. How many records propose an algorithm at all? Adding the 4 both-tagged records to the 42 algorithm-only records gives 46. How many records report a hardware measurement at all? Adding the same 4 to the 32 deployment-only records gives 36. Neither count is more authoritative than the other; they answer different questions, and this survey states both rather than collapsing them into a single headline figure.

16.2 The Rate-Coding Falsifier and Its Counterexamples

The rate-coding falsifier, that no audited source both proposes a recent, low-timestep, rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion algorithm and reports that same algorithm measured on named physical silicon (Section 9.7), is this subsection's starting point. What follows reads that finding directly off Table 23 and widens it with three verified counterexamples.

Table 23's low-latency-conversion row makes the finding concrete. Of its 16 records, 13 are algorithm-only, every one of them rate, burst, or signed-rate coded, and every one E5, software simulation with no chip named in the experiments section. The remaining 3 are tagged both, meaning the proposing paper itself reports E1 physical hardware execution, and all 3 abandon rate coding at the point of conversion to do so. Quartz converts to a time-to-first-spike code and reaches physical Loihi 1. Brehove et al. convert to Loihi 2's native graded sigma-delta channel. Andrei et al. extend Few-Spikes conversion to a serialized temporal code on physical SpiNNaker2. Zero of the 16 records in this row are both rate-coded and E1 in their own paper. This is exactly the gap the falsifier names, read directly off Table 23 rather than asserted separately from it.

Three verified counterexamples widen this picture without weakening the narrow claim above, and each answers one of three separate questions this survey does not collapse into one. Did the proposing paper deploy its own contribution? Adaptive Fission's own hardware table includes unmodified no-Fission QCFS and SRP baseline rows measured on a physical Lynxi HP201 accelerator, an E1 result neither the QCFS nor the SRP paper itself reported. Rate-coded QCFS-lineage networks have therefore reached silicon, achieved by a later, different paper's population-coding contribution rather than by QCFS's or SRP's own authors. Is a rate-coded, low-timestep SNN reaching silicon confined to conversion at all? Arfa et al. report physical SpiNNaker2 latency, power, and energy for rate-coded spiking Q-networks run for 10 or 20 ticks, a genuine rate-coded, low-timestep result on named silicon that falls outside the falsifier's scope because the method is direct Q-learning with hardware-aware fine-tuning, not ANN-to-SNN conversion. Does a vendor's own product label settle the coding question? BrainChip's Akida deployments are physical and are called SNNs by the vendor, yet its standard conversion route collapses to a single feedforward pass with no timestep window and no reset by subtraction (Section 9.6). Akida's physical results therefore do not satisfy the falsifier's requirement that hidden activations retain a binary spike-count or firing-rate representation over a finite window, independent of what the runtime is named.

This falsifier's scope excludes direct-training methods, application papers that reuse an existing converter, classical conversion deployments audited under Section 7's earlier correspondence, non-silicon RTL or cycle-level synthesis, and later third-party baseline deployments of an existing method. A future paper that introduces a new low-timestep conversion algorithm whose hidden activations retain a rate or spike-count representation, and that itself reports at least one on-device measurement on named physical neuromorphic silicon, weakens this finding. None of the sources audited here does so.

16.3 Semantic Findings on Reset, Operators, and Time

Reset semantics are the field this survey has traced in the greatest cross-toolchain depth, and the corpus-level pattern is asymmetric. Of the eleven typed edges Table 16 applies the input, output, preservation, transformation, rejection, and evidence schema to, two reject the source model outright, four transform subtractive reset into a different rule, one is undocumented, one concerns the sample boundary rather than the reset rule, and only two preserve subtractive reset, one only through the two-compartment emulation NxTF requires on Loihi 1 and the other natively through Sinabs's route to Speck. A third preserving row, Lava-DL NetX HDF5 to the Lava LIF process, preserves a hard-zero reset instead. At platform level, Table 18's nine audited platforms range across every capability status this survey defines for the threshold-and-reset field, native on Speck, Xylo, and BrainScaleS-2, transformed on Loihi 1 and both Akida generations, unsupported on the audited routes to Loihi 2 and both SpiNNaker generations. No audited platform in Table 18 recovers subtractive reset through a native, single-compartment hardware primitive on a chip whose own default behavior is reset-to-zero.

Graph operators show the opposite pattern, uniform rather than spread. Every one of the nine platforms in Table 18 carries a transformed status for graph operators and supported operations, meaning no platform accepts an arbitrary computation graph without a mapper restructuring, splitting, or rejecting unsupported topology first. Encoder and decoder status is equally uniform, host-assisted at all nine platforms, confirming at corpus scale that no audited platform performs both input encoding and output decoding entirely on-chip.

Time model findings come from a different axis than either field above. Section 4.1 and Audit A distinguish four mechanisms that this survey's evidence keeps structurally separate, a static per-layer horizon set before inference, a per-input early-exit horizon set during inference, asynchronous within-core computation coexisting with a chip-wide barrier, and timestep-free event-driven execution with no discrete horizon at all. Among PASCAL, QAC, MT-SNN, and Temporal Flexibility, the four sources this survey audited for static heterogeneous per-layer scheduling, none demonstrates that schedule executing on physical or even cycle-simulated hardware; PASCAL's and APEX's own RTL synthesis evidence covers only a uniform-timestep neuron overhead check, not the mixed-horizon schedule itself. Barrier synchronization coexisting with asynchronous within-core computation is independently confirmed for Loihi and Tianjic-lineage systems, and this coexistence does not, on its own, establish that different layers hold independent discrete time coordinates, the non-equivalence Section 4.1 already states. Timestep-free execution is the one mechanism in this group with a confirmed physical result, Temporal Flexibility's deployment to a physical Speck2e device, and that result is event-driven and horizon-free at the point of execution, not a demonstration of the static mixed-horizon schedule the other three sources study only in software.

Measurement practice across the workload evidence in Table 21 and Table 22 shows a parallel pattern to the route table. Only the fabricated dies, ODIN, ReckOn, and MorphIC, report bench-measured energy per synaptic operation rather than a proxy or an analytical estimate; static and dynamic power are separated in a small minority of the audited records, the tactile-localization system being the clean confirmed instance; memory and communication cost are left unstated in most E1 records rather than reported as zero; and a Pareto claim inherits the measurement boundary of its weakest axis, exactly the finding Section 14.5 already states for SEENN's and Dynamic Confidence's own accuracy-versus-timestep frontiers, both built from a spike-count energy proxy in software simulation rather than a measured energy axis.

Accessibility findings sit alongside the route and measurement findings rather than below them, because a route's state can change independent of its own technical merit. Intel's 2026 archival of the entire Lava, Lava-DL, and NetX stack, freezing every Table 16 edge that terminates at Loihi 2 through that stack at whatever state it held at archival (Section 11.5), is the clearest instance of that risk. Loihi access more broadly remains gated to members of Intel's Neuromorphic Research Community rather than openly available. Lynxi's BIDL, Lyngor, and LynSDK toolchain is documented in the sources this survey reviewed as effectively unavailable outside mainland China. Every platform-capability record behind Table 18 remains provisional pending full-text promotion of its underlying vendor manual, the limitation Section 10.4 states once for the whole comparison.

16.5 Evidence and Interpretation

Sections 16.1 through 16.4 report counts, verified claims, route states, and sourced facts. Nothing above this point assigns a cause to any of those findings. The paragraphs below state candidate explanations explicitly as interpretation, not as an extension of the evidence.

Why zero of the 16 low-latency-conversion records in Table 23 pair rate coding with the proposing paper's own physical hardware measurement admits several candidate explanations that the audited evidence does not adjudicate between. One reading is architectural, that a multi-step, binary, count-based representation is a genuinely poor match for the barrier-synchronized or fully asynchronous execution families Section 12 compares, so groups that abandon rate coding are responding to a real hardware constraint. A second reading is a matter of engineering cost, that building a hardware-specific pipeline for one platform is a separate, substantial effort from the software contribution a conversion paper is otherwise built around, so most conversion papers simply stop before that step regardless of the code's hardware suitability. A third reading is about publication incentives, that a venue rewarding a simulated accuracy curve across many timestep values gives an author little additional credit for the months of engineering a physical deployment would cost. A fourth reading is about access, that the platforms with the clearest documented capability, Loihi 2 and the archived Lava stack among them, are also the platforms gated behind community membership or now frozen without a successor toolchain, which would depress physical results independent of any property of the code itself. This survey's audited evidence is consistent with all four readings and does not distinguish among them.

Section 15.5 records a parallel, workload-level version of the same open question. Vision carries every confirmed rate-coded and non-rate-coded conversion case in the audited application evidence, while audio, temporal sensing, control, robotics, and the two scientific and industrial cases skew toward direct training and hand-designed circuits. Whether that skew reflects a property of the workloads themselves, a property of which research communities publish hardware results, or a property of which correspondence a given code admits for a given signal class is a question this survey's evidence does not answer, and it is restated here as an open interpretation rather than resolved by restating it.

One reading this survey does reject as unsupported by its own evidence is a strong impossibility claim, that rate-coded low-timestep conversion cannot reach silicon. Table 23 and Table 24 already falsify that stronger reading directly, through the Adaptive Fission baseline rows and through Akida's physical, if semantically transformed, route. The narrower, demonstrated finding is that no audited paper has yet closed the loop between proposing such a method and measuring that same method on hardware. Section 17 takes each of Section 16's demonstrated gaps, a failed contract edge, a blocked route, an unsupported or undocumented platform cell, and turns it into a stated research problem rather than repeating this section's findings as unresolved questions.
