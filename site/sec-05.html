5 Where an SNN Runs

5.1 Three Targets, One Network

The target of a deployment claim is the machine the claim describes, not the paper's headline platform. Three buckets cover every target this survey audits. A conventional processor carries the chip words CPU, GPU, MCU, RISC-V, NPU, and event NPU, whatever software steps the network. A simulated target carries simulator, a model of neuromorphic hardware that is not the silicon. A neuromorphic target carries neuromorphic or FPGA, fabricated silicon or a custom spiking accelerator measured as its own target. Table 3 in Section 1.3, How Claims Are Graded, gives the meaning and the exclusion fixed for each bucket.

The three buckets restate machinery Figure 4 and Figure 8 already draw. The simulated target is the second crossing Figure 4 draws, the right column's equations stepped on the left column's machine, a spiking network run as a program again with its state in memory and its time the clock. The two execution stacks Figure 8 lays side by side, a trained network compiled down to a CPU, GPU, MCU, or NPU on one side and mapped down to fabricated silicon on the other, are the two ends of the same walk this section audits. What a chip model can reproduce on paper and what only silicon measures is exactly what the contract fields of Section 9.5, Platforms Compared Through the Contract, compare field by field, native neuron dynamics against emulated reset, transformed precision against native code.

The table's shape carries the meaning, not its cells. A converted network reaches every bucket, because its ANN form already runs unmodified on a conventional processor and its converted spiking form is exactly what a simulator or a chip is built to accept. A directly trained network reaches the neuromorphic bucket only where the chip's own neuron model and reset rule are already native to it, the same native-versus-emulated split Table 24 in Section 9.5, Platforms Compared Through the Contract, states field by field for nine audited platforms. Choosing a path is therefore not a matter of convenience. It is a choice of what the resulting claim will be allowed to say about the network once it runs.

5.2 Conventional Platforms

On a general-purpose processor a spiking network is a program again, its state resident in memory and its time the processor's own clock, the crossing Figure 4 draws from the neuromorphic column back to the conventional one. A spike becomes a value inside a tensor rather than an event on a bus, and sparsity in that tensor pays off only where the kernel underneath it is written to skip a zero, the distinction Section 2.3, Dense Computation, Sparse Activity, and Conditional Execution, already draws and Section 2.5, Why Representation Constrains Execution, extends to hardware. SpikingJelly, snnTorch, and Norse step this representation on PyTorch, GeNN and mlGeNN step it through code generation, and NEST and Brian2 step it on CPUs; Section 10.2, Simulator and Library Catalog, catalogs what each executes and reaches, not repeated here. Four measured runs anchor the bucket rather than a library's own claim about itself. On an NVIDIA A100 GPU, a training benchmark reaches up to an eleven-fold speedup over two comparator libraries (E1 GPU). On a twelve-gigabyte Titan V GPU, a converted VGG-16 model runs two-and-a-half times faster than a comparator simulator (E1 GPU). On a thirty-two-node Intel Xeon cluster, a full-scale cortical microcircuit reaches a comparable energy per synaptic event to physical SpiNNaker only after roughly a 4.6-fold slowdown (E1 CPU), and across the K computer's 82,944 nodes, the same simulation software's own scaling report measures wall-clock time from 128 nodes upward (E1 CPU). Each of the four states what it measured on which processor, not a property of any chip its network was converted from or destined for.

A converted network on a microcontroller runs as compiled fixed-point code rather than an interpreted graph, the pattern CMSIS-NN set for quantized CNNs on Cortex-M cores. On an ARM Cortex-M4, a converted spiking model measures 6.1 milliseconds and 48 microjoules per inference, a 23 percent reduction against the 60 microjoules its 8-bit CNN counterpart draws on the same board (E1 MCU). RISC-V accelerators for spiking inference are surveyed at length by Farsa et al., edge-device benchmarking on CPUs, GPUs, and NPUs by Du et al., and on a RISC-V soft vector processor synthesized onto a Kria KV260 FPGA, an SNN classifier runs faster than an embedded GPU and faster than physical Loihi while using half the energy (E1 RISC-V). What either run measures is the core's own cycle count and its energy at the board boundary. Neither measures a property of neuromorphic silicon, whatever chip a comparison sentence in the same paper cites alongside it.

A dense neural processing unit executes low-bit matrix multiplication natively rather than stepping a simulator on general-purpose cores. A vendor toolchain can also carry one converted network to two buckets as a documented option; BIDL's multi-backend compiler routes through code generation either to Lynxi's many-core, compute-in-memory chip, a neuromorphic target, or to a conventional CPU or GPU fallback, within a single build. IBM's NorthPole belongs in this bucket for a reason worth stating precisely. It is a purely digital, low-precision matrix-multiply accelerator with no spiking or event-driven neuron model and no membrane-potential dynamics, so its reported efficiency comes from memory locality and low-bit arithmetic, not from event-driven temporal sparsity; it carries the NPU chip rather than the neuromorphic bucket on the strength of a shared vendor lineage alone. An STM32-embedded neural accelerator sits in the same bucket at smaller scale, executing an already-quantized spiking model 7.6 times faster and at under a quarter the energy per sample of the same board's Cortex-M55 CPU path, measured in a degree project (E1 NPU). BrainChip's Akida is a distinct case, an event-domain accelerator whose documented route runs a converted, quantized CNN as a single pass of sparse events and exposes no spiking neuron model integrated over T, which is why it carries the separate event NPU chip rather than the dense NPU word above.

5.3 Simulated and Emulated Neuromorphic Hardware

A chip's own simulator ships with the same toolchain that targets the physical part, and what it reproduces is stated, or left unstated, in that toolchain's own documentation. Loihi's software stack ships a software model of the chip's behavior, runnable on a conventional CPU before any board is touched. Xylo's own simulator, distributed with its host framework, is documented for training and evaluating a network before deployment to the physical digital core; whether it also reproduces the core's timing is not stated in the sources read for this survey. Akida's software backend runs a converted network on the host CPU by default; once a model is mapped to a device, inference runs on the device and its power and energy per inference are read from the device's own meter. BrainScaleS-2's own interface distinguishes a mock, numerical simulation mode from genuine execution on the physical chip explicitly, rather than treating the two as equivalent. SpiNNaker's own software stack, by contrast, is documented as reaching SpiNNaker hardware exclusively; no software-only simulator path for it is stated in the sources read for this survey.

A register-transfer-level or cycle-level model of a spiking accelerator produces a count before any silicon is taped out, and that count is exactly as reliable as the synthesis or simulation tool that generated it, not as a fabricated die. One dual-sparsity design synthesized in 40-nanometer CMOS reports 1.3 to 5.4 percent power overhead and an energy per inference of 69.23 millijoules at eight timesteps against a 181.76-millijoule baseline at 32, entirely from RTL synthesis and cycle-level simulation (E4 simulator). A column-level hybrid scheduler reports 57 to 67 percent energy-delay-product reduction against an ANN-only baseline from the same kind of synthesis model, with no fabricated die behind the figure (E4 simulator). A RISC-V-controlled digital neuromorphic architecture reports 0.47 square millimeters per core and 2.8 picojoules per synaptic operation from synthesis and power-estimation software, with an accompanying thesis stating explicitly that no physical chip implementation exists at the time of writing (E4 simulator). A number from any of the three is a property of the model, not of a bench.

Two different things share the word FPGA in this literature, and the boundary between them is stated once here rather than at every mention. An FPGA that reimplements a named chip's own architecture, neuron block, router, and scheduler, to check it against that chip's own reference simulator is a simulated target, carrying the simulator chip; one such reimplementation of IBM's TrueNorth on a Xilinx Zynq UltraScale+ board verifies functional equivalence against TrueNorth's own Compass simulator and reports a 50 percent resource reduction from an architectural fix, a claim about the reimplementation, not about fabricated TrueNorth silicon (E4 simulator). An FPGA that is itself the measured target, a custom spiking accelerator synthesized and benchmarked as its own design rather than standing in for another chip, carries the FPGA chip and the neuromorphic bucket instead. What separates the two is not the silicon technology, an FPGA is programmable logic in both cases, but which chip the resulting number is a claim about. Only fabricated, physically probed silicon establishes a number at the physical boundary of Section 12.2, Measurement Boundaries,; a model, however cycle-accurate, and an emulation, however faithful, remain claims about the model and the emulation.

Every entry in the catalog above, including the four hardware-emulation designs, is software or a design that exists as software; what it runs on ranges from a program on a processor to a model of a circuit to fabricated silicon, and only the last crosses the physical boundary this bucket names. Fabricated neuromorphic silicon is different in kind. BrainChip's Akida is absent from this bucket because its documented route runs a converted network as a single event-domain pass and exposes no spiking neuron model over T, the reason Section 5.2, Conventional Platforms, gives it the event NPU chip. What runs on that silicon is the circuit's own dynamics, not a program stepped by a separate processor reading instructions from memory, and the measurement boundary a claim about it must cross is the one the evidence classes already fix, physical execution or physical accuracy read from that die itself, not a proxy computed anywhere else. Section 5, Where an SNN Runs, takes up the families this bucket contains. Section 11, Complete Deployment Routes, takes up how a trained network actually reaches one of them.

5.4 Synchronous Digital Execution

Section 10.7 closed by noting that software representation is necessary but not sufficient for a deployment claim. This section takes up the remaining question, whether a target that accepts some representation of a contract field executes it as specified. It organizes hardware by execution model rather than by vendor, because two chips from different manufacturers that both advance a barrier-synchronized global tick share more with each other, for the purposes of the deployable contract, than either shares with a chip from the same lineage built around a fundamentally different timing model. Platform examples throughout this section carry a stated generation, toolchain version, and capability status rather than a bare vendor name, and every capability record drawn from this survey's generated platform-capability data remains provisional in the sense Section 9.4 already fixes, pending full-text promotion of the underlying vendor manual. The neuron circuit that Sections 3 and 4 derive is only part of the neuromorphic engineering problem this section audits. At scale, routing, on-chip memory, and event delivery, address-event representation, network-on-chip fabrics, and multicast routing tables, cost more silicon area and energy than the neuron arithmetic itself, on Loihi's asynchronous mesh, on SpiNNaker's multicast router, and on Speck's asynchronous event fabric alike, and the family-by-family subsections below state which of these mechanisms each audited platform actually uses.

A synchronous digital system advances computation through a global or barrier-based progress signal that every core or region of the chip must reach before the next step begins, regardless of whether the circuitry beneath that signal is itself clocked uniformly. Section 4.1 already established the sharpest instance of this pattern for Intel's Loihi family. Loihi 1 and Loihi 2 compute and communicate asynchronously within a core, yet both advance a single, global algorithmic timestep across the whole chip through a barrier synchronization handshake, and IBM's TrueNorth and Tsinghua's Tianjic each advance time through their own chip-wide synchronization signal in the same structural sense . TrueNorth evaluates its entire network once per one-kilohertz global tick, encoding activation magnitude spatially across redundant, trinary-weighted neuron copies within that single tick rather than through multi-timestep temporal accumulation, a code-and-timing pairing distinct from the rate-coded correspondence Section 8 derives . SynSense's Xylo family sits in the same family from the opposite direction, a genuinely clocked, synchronous digital ASIC running an internal clock in the tens of megahertz with a fixed recurrent CuBa-LIF core and a global timestep duration configured by the host .

The mechanism this family shares is a chip-wide agreement on step boundaries, whatever happens inside one step. The semantic effect is that every layer of a mapped network advances at the same rate as every other layer within one inference, which is exactly the assumption the static per-layer horizons of Section 8.10 would need a barrier-synchronized target to relax, a demonstration Section 4.1 already states no audited source in this survey provides on physical synchronous hardware. The trade-off is that a barrier's own wall-clock duration is not fixed by the model and stretches with the slowest core's spike traffic in a given step, so a reported timestep count on this family of hardware is never automatically a fixed physical duration . The deployment consequence, developed field by field in Section 9.5, is that Loihi's own neuron dynamics field is native while its threshold and reset field on the audited route to Loihi 1 is only recoverable through the two-compartment emulation Section 10 already traces in full, a divergence that a single verdict about Loihi's synchronous execution model would not reveal.

5.5 Asynchronous Digital Execution

An asynchronous event-driven digital pipeline has no chip-wide barrier at all. A core or a processing stage advances only when an event demands it, and the chip has no shared clock signal for a barrier to be built on top of. SynSense's Speck and its predecessor core DYNAP-CNN are the confirmed instances in this survey's audited evidence, a fully asynchronous, clockless system-on-chip integrating a DVS with nine fixed convolution, integrate-and-fire, and pooling cores, reporting a single event crossing all nine layers in under four microseconds regardless of how many timesteps a software model believed it was simulating . This is Section 4.1's fourth mechanism, timestep-free event-driven execution, and it is the one mechanism in that discussion with a confirmed physical result behind it, Temporal Flexibility's deployment of a Mixed Time-step Training network to a physical Speck2e device .

The mechanism is that state updates occur strictly on event arrival, with no vector of per-layer discrete horizons for a scheduler to execute at all. The semantic effect is that a reported timestep count for a network destined for this family of hardware describes only the training-time simulation, not a quantity the chip itself schedules; Section 4.1 already states this distinction and this section does not relax it. The trade-off is that removing the barrier removes with it any single, chip-wide notion of when one inference ends and the next begins, which is why Section 10.5 records sample-duration configuration and output aggregation as host responsibilities for this family rather than native chip behavior. The deployment consequence, confirmed directly in Section 9.5's comparison table, is that Speck's time-and-termination field is recorded as host-assisted rather than native, the one point in that table where the asynchronous family's own defining property, no chip-wide tick, produces a different status than the barrier-synchronized family's native time-and-termination field, even though both families ultimately depend on a host to declare when a result should be read.

5.6 Software-Programmable Many-Core Systems

A software-programmable many-core system executes a user-supplied program on each of many general-purpose or near-general-purpose cores rather than realizing one fixed neuron circuit in hardware. SpiNNaker 1 and SpiNNaker 2 are this survey's confirmed instances, an ARM-core-based digital many-core architecture that simulates arbitrary software-defined neuron models rather than one built-in dynamical primitive, at a default software convention near one millisecond per tick that Section 3.6 already identifies as a scheduling convention rather than a hardware limit . This is also the family in which Table 7's CUBA-LIF row places sPyNNaker's own documented default cell type, IF_curr_exp, a current-based synapse paired with an exponentially decaying current rather than the instantaneous sum IF and LIF assume .

The mechanism is that neuron and synapse behavior is compiled C code running on a general-purpose core rather than a fixed circuit, so this family's capability status for a given contract field tracks what a user is willing to implement rather than what the silicon fixes in place. The semantic effect is direct for the reset-and-threshold field. SpiNNaker's built-in cell types use PyNN-conventional hard-reset semantics by default, and reset by subtraction is achievable only by writing a bespoke C neuron model with a custom spike-detection callback, not by selecting a built-in option, which is why Section 10 records the SNN Toolbox to sPyNNaker route's threshold-and-reset field as unsupported for the audited default configuration even though the route as a whole reaches physical silicon . The trade-off is a place-and-route resource budget rather than a silicon-area one; SpiNNTools compresses multicast routing tables toward a roughly 1024-entry-per-chip router budget and partitions the application graph at close to 256 atoms per core given per-core memory limits, a mapping constraint distinct from anything a barrier-synchronized or fully asynchronous chip needs to satisfy . The deployment consequence is that this family's programmability makes an absolute claim about what it cannot do particularly risky. A capability recorded as unsupported for this family describes the audited default configuration and toolchain, not a hardware ceiling a custom kernel could not in principle raise.

5.7 Analog and Mixed-Signal Execution

An analog or mixed-signal system realizes neuron and synapse dynamics as physical circuit behavior evolving in continuous time, typically at a fixed acceleration factor relative to biological or wall-clock time, rather than as a discrete-time numerical recurrence. Heidelberg's BrainScaleS-1 and BrainScaleS-2 are the confirmed instances developed in depth in this survey's audited evidence, wafer-scale and single-chip analog systems physically emulating adaptive-exponential or leaky-integrate-and-fire circuits at roughly one thousand to ten thousand times accelerated analog time, requiring per-neuron calibration to correct manufacturing mismatch before a mapped network reaches usable accuracy . SynSense's DYNAP-SE2 and Innatera's Pulsar extend the same execution family to smaller, commercially oriented mixed-signal parts; this survey's audited evidence located no rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion pipeline for DYNAP-SE2, and every quantitative performance figure it located for Innatera Pulsar is vendor-sourced without an independent peer-reviewed benchmark, a gap this survey states rather than resolves .

The mechanism is that threshold crossing and reset are properties of a physical neuron circuit rather than a digital comparison against a stored number, so the threshold-and-reset field is native by construction on hardware in this family, exactly the status Section 9.5's comparison table records for BrainScaleS-2. The semantic effect is that numeric precision is not a bit width chosen by a compiler but a resolution set by circuit mismatch and calibration quality, which is why the same table records BrainScaleS-2's precision field as transformed rather than native, a mismatch-and-calibration process standing in for what a digital platform would call quantization. A caution belongs here, not deferred to a later section, because the audited physical route for hxtorch, the PyTorch interface to BrainScaleS-2, targets its non-spiking hagen mode, executing analog matrix multiplication directly rather than the spiking, leaky-integrate-and-fire hxtorch.snn mode . This survey's own source notes this distinction explicitly, and the physical latency and energy measurement Stradmann et al. report for a mobile electrocardiogram application must not be read as a measurement of the spiking execution of hxtorch.snn . The separate hardware-in-the-loop evidence for BrainScaleS-2's own spiking mode, hxtorch.snn's injection of measured membrane traces into automatic differentiation for the Yin-Yang classification task, is Section 7.3's E2 result, a genuinely spiking, genuinely physical claim, but a distinct one from the non-spiking route this section's platform-capability comparison audits . The trade-off for the whole family is that accelerated time is a design feature for some workloads and a representational obstacle for others; a network trained assuming discrete algorithmic steps must be re-expressed against continuous circuit dynamics before either the timing or the precision fields can be evaluated as this survey's contract requires.

5.8 FPGA and Custom-ASIC Implementations

An FPGA or custom-ASIC implementation realizes a fixed or reconfigurable digital circuit purpose-built for one execution model rather than adapting a general-purpose many-core architecture or a continuous analog substrate to the task. BrainChip's Akida family is this survey's most thoroughly audited instance, a commercial neuromorphic system-on-chip whose standard QuantizeML-to-CNN2SNN deployment route collapses the multi-step firing-rate window the classical correspondence assumes into a single feedforward pass (Section 8.11, Section 11.4). UCLouvain's MorphIC is the clearest controlled same-silicon comparison available in this survey's audited evidence between two neural codes, reporting rate coding reaching 97.8 percent accuracy at 205 microjoules per classification against rank order coding reaching 95.9 percent accuracy at 21.8 microjoules per classification on the identical MNIST task and chip, a result Section 4.3 already develops in full . A broader FPGA family, including Cerebron, SyncNN, and other accelerators surveyed for their code-fidelity properties in earlier sections, implements the classical IF-plus-reset-by-subtraction-plus-rate-coding pipeline with varying fidelity per design; SyncNN and a subset of these designs expose reset by subtraction as an explicit, user-selectable configuration, while others do not document their reset polarity at all .

The mechanism this family shares is that the execution model is fixed into the circuit at design time rather than negotiated by a general-purpose core's software or a continuous physical process. The semantic effect is that a custom ASIC or a synthesized FPGA design can realize almost any of the neuron models Section 3.3 derives natively, at the cost of committing that choice before fabrication or synthesis rather than after. The trade-off is exactly the one Section 8.11 and Section 8.12 already establish for Akida specifically. Every confirmed instance in this survey's audited corpus of a conversion method reaching physical silicon through this family abandons the classical rate-coded correspondence for a code native to the target rather than adapting the target to carry an unmodified rate code. The deployment consequence is that a capability status reported for this family is a statement about one fabricated or synthesized design, not about what a different FPGA bitstream or a different ASIC tapeout targeting the same neuron family could in principle achieve; Section 8.10's APEX and NeuroFlex accelerator models, synthesized in RTL rather than fabricated, are E4 hardware-model evidence for exactly this reason, a documented design rather than a measured chip.

5.9 Emerging Devices

Conventional processors as targets are the subject of Section 5.2, Conventional Platforms,; this subsection keeps the devices that are neither conventional nor yet a platform.

In-memory and emerging-device systems are covered here only to deployment-relevant depth, consistent with the referral Section 2.6 already gives to the underlying device physics. Lynxi's KA200, a compute-in-memory, many-core, heterogeneous-fusion chip built on the Tianjic lineage and natively supporting both deep-learning ANNs and spiking networks with mixed-precision compute, is this survey's clearest confirmed instance, and its own access limitation, a deployment path effectively unavailable outside mainland China per the sources reviewed, is itself a deployment-relevant fact rather than a detail this survey sets aside . The present discussion stops after this deployment-relevant depth. For the underlying device-level physics of brain-inspired and analog or emerging-device neuromorphic substrates, see Zolfagharinejad et al. , already referred to at the same boundary in Section 2.6.
