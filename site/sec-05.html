5 Where an SNN Runs

5.1 How Claims Are Graded

Every deployment claim this survey reports carries a grade, and the grade is set before the claim is read, not after. A deployment claim about a spiking network is graded by what was actually executed and measured to support that specific claim, whether the claim concerns latency, energy, accuracy, or a route that has merely been documented. One paper routinely carries claims of several different classes at once, an accuracy figure measured on physical silicon alongside a route that was only ever described and never run. Grading at the claim level keeps a strong result reported in one part of a paper from lending unearned credibility to a weaker claim stated nearby in the same publication. Table 9 sorts claims along that axis into five classes; they are not a quality ranking of the work that produced them.

E2Physical hardware with accuracy or fidelity measurement only.Measured latency, power, or energy.

E4A hardware model, register-transfer level (RTL) synthesis, or cycle-level simulation without fabricated silicon.Physical execution.

Table 9 grades how a claim was established. The chip beside a grade names the machine the claim describes, one of the nine target words Section 5.2, Three Targets, One Network, defines next, and a paper can carry claims on two targets.

The classes block several substitutions that recur across this literature. Fewer timesteps do not prove lower wall-clock latency. Fewer spikes do not prove lower energy. A lower operation count does not prove lower measured power. A documented exporter does not prove that a model reached silicon. Treating any one of these proxies as the measurement it stands in for turns a route that is merely documented, or a result that is merely simulated, into a claim about physical hardware that the record does not support. The time symbols of Section 4.1, the software boundaries of Section 10, and the measurement boundaries of Section 12.2 define these distinctions, and the classes apply from the first measured run in this section onward, with Section 7, Direct SNN Training, carrying the on-chip-learning deployments tabulated there.

5.2 Three Targets, One Network

The target of a deployment claim is the machine the claim describes, not the paper's headline platform. Three questions separate every machine this section audits, and the vocabulary of each is already fixed. Where does the weight sit relative to the arithmetic that uses it, the contrast Section 2.1, One Stack, Many Vocabularies, draws down the two columns of Figure 4 and Section 2.5, Why Representation Constrains Execution, prices at the memory hierarchy. What does the silicon compute, a static function evaluated once per input or a system whose state persists across time, the value and the state Section 2.2, Values, State, and Events, separates. And how does time pass, on a clock or on the arrival of an event, the axis Section 2.4, The Meanings of Neuromorphic and Event-Driven, fixes in Table 3. The first question sorts targets by Figure 18, the second by the three buckets of Table 10 that Table 11 in Section 5.5, The Event NPU, crosses, and the third by the families inside the neuromorphic bucket that Section 5.6, Physical Neuromorphic Hardware, organizes.

Machines differ physically in where the memory sits relative to the arithmetic, the von Neumann bottleneck Section 2.1 already dates to Von Neumann's 1945 report and to Backus's 1978 name for the word-at-a-time traffic a weight must cross to reach the arithmetic that uses it. A dense neural processing unit narrows the traffic, an array of multiply-accumulate units fed from a buffer so that fewer operands are read from it, but its memory stays outside the array as a processor's does. A neuromorphic chip removes the shared memory instead. TrueNorth and Loihi build the neuron from digital cores and keep the synapse memory beside each core, the synchronous digital family Section 5.6.1, Synchronous Digital Execution, audits. Section 5.6.6, Emerging Devices, covers the chips that place the memory inside the core, Lynxi's compute-in-memory KA200 among them, together with the emerging devices. Figure 18 draws the three architectures on one axis from von Neumann to non-von Neumann, in the panel layout of the EdgeSNN survey's comparison. The subsections that follow place every target on that axis before anything is said about what the target measures.

Three buckets cover every target this survey audits, and the second question draws their boundaries. A conventional processor carries the chip words CPU, GPU, MCU, RISC-V, NPU, and event NPU, whatever software steps the network. A simulated target carries simulator, a model of neuromorphic hardware that is not the silicon. A neuromorphic target carries neuromorphic or FPGA, fabricated silicon or a custom spiking accelerator measured as its own target. Table 10 fixes the three buckets once; every chip on the page belongs to one of its rows. Its evidence-ceiling column names the highest of the five classes of Section 5.1, How Claims Are Graded, that a claim on that bucket can reach.

Two boundaries follow from reading the target as the machine the claim describes. A simulator running on a GPU is a conventional claim about the GPU when the claim is about the run, and a simulated claim about the chip only when the simulator is the chip's own model and the claim is about the chip's semantics. The same reading separates a custom FPGA accelerator that is itself the measured target from an FPGA that stands in for a named chip (Section 5.4).

The simulated bucket is the second crossing of Figure 4, Section 2.1; the two execution stacks of Figure 8 are the two ends of the walk this section audits.

5.3 Conventional Platforms

On a general-purpose processor a spiking network is a program again, its state in memory and its time the processor's own clock (Figure 4), panel (a) of Figure 18, where the bottleneck is paid once per timestep and a network stepped for T timesteps reads and writes that state T times, each access costing more energy than the arithmetic it feeds. On the first two questions of Section 5.2 the processor is therefore von Neumann carrying state across time, and on the third its time is the clock. Sparsity in the resulting tensor pays off only where the kernel underneath it is written to skip a zero (Section 2.3, Dense Computation, Sparse Activity, and Conditional Execution). SpikingJelly, snnTorch, Norse, GeNN, mlGeNN, NEST, and Brian2 step this representation on PyTorch, through code generation, or on CPUs (Section 10.2, Simulator and Library Catalog). Four measured runs anchor the bucket rather than a library's own claim about itself, each stating only what it measured on which processor, not a property of any chip its network was converted from or destined for (Section 11.3 carries the versions and caveats behind each number). An NVIDIA A100 GPU reaches an eleven-fold training speedup over a comparator library (E1 GPU); a Titan V GPU runs a converted VGG-16 model two-and-a-half times faster than a comparator simulator (E1 GPU); a thirty-two-node Intel Xeon cluster matches SpiNNaker's energy per synaptic event only after a 4.6-fold slowdown (E1 CPU); and the K computer's 82,944 nodes measure wall-clock time from 128 nodes upward (E1 CPU).

A quantized CNN on a microcontroller runs as compiled fixed-point code rather than an interpreted graph, the pattern CMSIS-NN set for Cortex-M cores, extended to parallel ultra-low-power RISC-V clusters and to mixed low-precision kernels more broadly and surveyed at length for the microcontroller case by Abushahla et al.. On an ARM Cortex-M4, a converted spiking model measures about 6 milliseconds per inference (E1 MCU; Section 11.3). RISC-V accelerators for spiking inference are surveyed at length by Farsa et al., edge-device benchmarking on CPUs, GPUs, and NPUs by Du et al., and on a RISC-V soft vector processor synthesized onto a Kria KV260 FPGA, an SNN classifier runs twice as fast as an embedded GPU under desktop Linux (E1 RISC-V; Section 11.3). What either run measures is the core's own cycle count and its energy at the board boundary, not a property of neuromorphic silicon, whatever chip a comparison sentence in the same paper cites alongside it.

A dense neural processing unit executes low-bit matrix multiplication natively rather than stepping a simulator on general-purpose cores. It is panel (b) of Figure 18, a buffer feeding an array of multiply-accumulate units so that fewer operands cross the gap, with the memory still outside the array. On the first question it narrows the bottleneck without removing it, and on the second it computes a static function, one pass per input. The accumulate-only strategy Section 2.5 admits for an event is not what the array performs, since a multiply-accumulate unit multiplies whether its operand is a spike or a value, so whatever a spiking network gains on this hardware comes from low precision and locality rather than from events. A vendor toolchain can also carry one converted network to two buckets as a documented option; BIDL's multi-backend compiler routes through code generation either to Lynxi's many-core, compute-in-memory chip, a neuromorphic target, or to a conventional CPU or GPU fallback, within a single build. IBM's NorthPole belongs in this bucket because it is a purely digital, low-precision matrix-multiply accelerator with no spiking or event-driven neuron model and no membrane-potential dynamics, so its reported efficiency comes from memory locality and low-bit arithmetic, not from event-driven temporal sparsity; it carries the NPU chip rather than the neuromorphic bucket on the strength of a shared vendor lineage alone. An STM32-embedded neural accelerator, a closely coupled on-die accelerator of the kind benchmarked more broadly by Millar et al. and by Balbi et al., sits in the same bucket at smaller scale, executing an already-quantized spiking model 7.6 times faster and at under a quarter the energy per sample of the same board's Cortex-M55 CPU path, measured in a degree project (E1 NPU).

5.4 Simulated and Emulated Neuromorphic Hardware

Nothing in this subsection moves on the first question of Section 5.2, and everything in it stays on the far side of the second. A vendor's software simulator is panel (a) of Figure 18 stepping the equations of panel (c), the second crossing Figure 4 draws, so its state is again a word in the host's memory and its time the host's clock, whatever the chip it models does with either. A register-transfer or cycle-level model describes a panel (c) circuit that does not yet exist, and an FPGA that emulates a named chip realizes panel (c)'s organization in programmable logic. What differs among the three is not the architecture the number is about but how far the number stands from fabricated silicon, which is what the classes of Section 5.1 grade.

A chip's own simulator ships with the same toolchain that targets the physical part, and what it reproduces is stated, or left unstated, in that toolchain's own documentation. Loihi's software stack ships a software model of the chip's behavior, runnable on a conventional CPU before any board is touched. Xylo's own simulator, distributed with its host framework, is documented for training and evaluating a network before deployment to the physical digital core; whether it also reproduces the core's timing is not stated in the sources read for this survey. Akida's software backend runs a converted network on the host CPU by default; once a model is mapped to a device, inference runs on the device and its power and energy per inference are read from the device's own meter. BrainScaleS-2's own interface distinguishes a mock, numerical simulation mode from genuine execution on the physical chip explicitly, rather than treating the two as equivalent. SpiNNaker's own software stack, by contrast, is documented as reaching SpiNNaker hardware exclusively; no software-only simulator path for it is stated in the sources read for this survey.

A register-transfer-level or cycle-level model of a spiking accelerator produces a count before any silicon is taped out, and that count is exactly as reliable as the synthesis or simulation tool that generated it, not as a fabricated die. APEX, a dual-sparsity design synthesized in 40-nanometer CMOS, is E4 simulator evidence entirely from RTL synthesis and cycle-level simulation, not fabricated silicon. NeuroFlex, a column-level hybrid scheduler, reaches the same class from the same kind of synthesis model, with no fabricated die behind its figures (E4 simulator). SENECA, a RISC-V-controlled digital neuromorphic architecture, reaches it too, its accompanying thesis stating explicitly that no physical chip implementation exists at the time of writing (E4 simulator). Section 10.2 carries each design's numbers; a number from any of the three is a property of the model, not of a bench.

Two different things share the word FPGA in this literature, and the boundary between them is stated once here rather than at every mention. An FPGA that reimplements a named chip's own architecture, neuron block, router, and scheduler, to check it against that chip's own reference simulator is a simulated target, carrying the simulator chip; one such reimplementation of IBM's TrueNorth on a Xilinx Zynq UltraScale+ board verifies functional equivalence against TrueNorth's own Compass simulator and reports a 50 percent resource reduction from an architectural fix, a claim about the reimplementation, not about fabricated TrueNorth silicon (E4 simulator). An FPGA that is itself the measured target, a custom spiking accelerator synthesized and benchmarked as its own design rather than standing in for another chip, carries the FPGA chip and the neuromorphic bucket instead. What separates the two is not the silicon technology, an FPGA is programmable logic in both cases, but which chip the resulting number is a claim about. Only fabricated, physically probed silicon establishes a number at the physical boundary of Section 12.2, Measurement Boundaries,; a model, however cycle-accurate, and an emulation, however faithful, remain claims about the model and the emulation.

5.5 The Event NPU

BrainChip's Akida is the one chip on the page that answers the first two questions of Section 5.2 in opposite directions, and the two answers are why it carries a word of its own. It is fabricated silicon sold as a neuromorphic system on chip, and the company's tech brief describes an event domain processor in which events or spikes indicate useful information, with the neural network control, the parameter memory, and the neuronal mathematics integrated on the processor. The AKD1000 part places an Arm Cortex-M4 host processor on the die beside a mesh of neural processing cores joined by an interconnect for spike transmission, and the AKD1500 that followed it entered production shipments in June 2026 on a 22-nanometer process. Figure 19 draws the AKD1000 as its own product brief and user guide describe it. On the first question the part has left von Neumann. The weights rest beside the circuits that use them, a neuron integrates a weight only when an incoming event releases it, and the tech brief states that power is consumed only when a neuron's inputs exceed its predetermined threshold, the near-memory placement of panel (c) and the event-driven execution model of Section 2.4's third sense.

On the second question the part has not moved, and what executes on that fabric is the reason the chip carries its own word. The documented deployment route is the MetaTF toolchain, QuantizeML to CNN2SNN to the Akida runtime, which takes a trained convolutional network, quantizes its weights and activations to the low bit widths the first generation accepts, and converts the result to the runtime's model format; the CNN2SNN guide states that batch normalization and the quantized activation function are wrapped into the spike-generating thresholds of the Akida model and that the associated scaling operations are never performed during inference. The integrate-and-fire structure of the circuit is real, an accumulator that fires when its inputs cross a threshold, but on this route the threshold is computed once at conversion from the quantization step, and the converted network runs as one feedforward pass of events that carry low-bit activation values. No simulation horizon T is exposed, no membrane potential carries across timesteps, and nothing is reset by subtraction. Two groups that trained and ran networks on physical AKD1000 hardware say so in their own words, a robotics group observing that the chip squashes the rate-code approximation of the ReLU into one time step, where a step-wise quantized ReLU represents it, and a space-applications benchmark finding that the part does not support leaky neurons and that its converted model reduces the computation to a single time step; a reading of the toolkit's source, recorded by this survey and not independently verified, shows the activation step folded into the stored threshold with a half-step rounding offset. An event on this route is a nonzero quantized activation. In the terms of Section 2.2 and Section 2.4, the event carries a value with no persistent state, so the chip is event-driven silicon executing a quantized CNN as a static function evaluated once per input, not a spiking network integrated over time, and it belongs neither to the dense NPU of Section 5.3, Conventional Platforms, nor to the families Section 5.6, Physical Neuromorphic Hardware, describes. The event NPU word of Section 5.2 names exactly this shape, and the chip sits in the conventional bucket because its workload is a converted CNN and a claim made on it is a claim about that program.

The event-based half of the description is not a marketing gloss. BrainChip acquired Spikenet Technology in 2016, the company built on Simon Thorpe's rank order coding, in which a neuron's information is carried by the order in which spikes arrive rather than by their count, and the tech brief names rank coding, information expressed as the time and place it occurs, as the chip's encoding scheme. That lineage lives at the input stage; inside the hidden layers of the converted route the comparison is order-agnostic, the point Section 8.3.6 makes when it declines to count Akida results toward either the rate-coded or the rank-order family. On-chip learning is likewise real and narrow, restricted on the first generation to a final fully connected layer with one-bit weights and one-bit input. And the deployment contract is that of an integer CNN accelerator, a Keras graph with bounded ReLU, same padding, a fixed set of kernel sizes, and unsigned eight-bit input, rather than a simulator accepting an arbitrary spiking topology. The second-generation Akida IP is documented by the same guide and is unexercised in this survey's audited corpus; it broadens the operator set with temporal and attention processors, keeps inference pass based, with temporal blocks holding bounded sequence context rather than a user-selected horizon, and drops edge learning.

What a claim on the part establishes follows from that. A latency, power, or energy figure read from the device's own meter after mapping is graded E1 event NPU, the reading Section 10.2 takes from BrainChip's documentation, and Section 11.4, Approximate and Transformed Routes, traces the QuantizeML to CNN2SNN to Akida chain as a physical route whose threshold-and-reset field is transformed on the way, the status Section 9.5 records. Such a claim says nothing about the multi-timestep, reset-by-subtraction execution the single-pass verdict above already rules out, which is why Section 13.7 does not let Akida's physical results satisfy the rate-coding falsifier.

5.6 Physical Neuromorphic Hardware

Fabricated neuromorphic silicon is different in kind, the bottom-right cell of Table 11, panel (c) of Figure 18 executing its own dynamics. The near-memory half of that panel, synapse memory beside each digital core, is the organization TrueNorth and Loihi share in Section 5.6.1; the in-memory half is the compute-in-memory KA200 of Section 5.6.6; and the analog family of Section 5.6.4 keeps the state in the physics of the circuit rather than in a stored number. BrainChip's Akida is absent from this bucket, one cell short of it (Section 5.5, The Event NPU). What separates the chips inside the cell is the third question of Section 5.2, how time passes, the axis of Table 3 in Section 2.4 read a second time; the six execution families that follow organize the bucket by it.

The families are organized by execution model rather than by vendor, because two chips from different manufacturers that both advance a barrier-synchronized global tick share more with each other, for the purposes of the deployable contract, than either shares with a chip from the same lineage built around a fundamentally different timing model. Every capability record drawn from this survey's generated platform-capability data remains provisional pending full-text promotion of the underlying vendor manual (Section 9.4). At scale, routing, on-chip memory, and event delivery, address-event representation, network-on-chip fabrics, and multicast routing tables, cost more silicon area and energy than the neuron arithmetic itself, on Loihi's asynchronous mesh, on SpiNNaker's multicast router, and on Speck's asynchronous event fabric alike, and the family-by-family subsections below state which of these mechanisms each audited platform actually uses.

5.6.1 Synchronous Digital Execution

A synchronous digital system advances computation through a global or barrier-based progress signal that every core or region of the chip must reach before the next step begins, regardless of whether the circuitry beneath that signal is itself clocked uniformly. Intel's Loihi 1 and Loihi 2, IBM's TrueNorth, and Tsinghua's Tianjic each advance a chip-wide synchronization signal in this sense, the sharpest instance of the pattern Section 4.1 already established; Loihi 1 packs 128 such cores supporting on the order of one hundred thousand neurons and one hundred million synapses, and TrueNorth packs 4,096 neurosynaptic cores of 256 neurons each, roughly one million neurons and hundreds of millions of synapses on one die. TrueNorth evaluates its entire network once per one-kilohertz global tick, encoding activation magnitude spatially across redundant, trinary-weighted neuron copies within that single tick rather than through multi-timestep temporal accumulation, a code-and-timing pairing distinct from the rate-coded correspondence Section 8 derives . SynSense's Xylo family sits in the same family from the opposite direction, a genuinely clocked, synchronous digital ASIC running an internal clock in the tens of megahertz with a fixed recurrent CuBa-LIF core and a global timestep duration configured by the host .

The mechanism this family shares is a chip-wide agreement on step boundaries, whatever happens inside one step. The semantic effect is that every layer of a mapped network advances at the same rate as every other layer within one inference, which is exactly the assumption the static per-layer horizons of Section 8.3.4 would need a barrier-synchronized target to relax (Section 13.8). The trade-off is that a barrier's own wall-clock duration is not fixed by the model and stretches with the slowest core's spike traffic in a given step, so a reported timestep count on this family of hardware is never automatically a fixed physical duration . The deployment consequence is that Loihi's neuron dynamics field is native while its threshold and reset field on the audited route to Loihi 1 is only recoverable through emulation, a divergence Table 24 in Section 9.5 records field by field.

5.6.2 Asynchronous Digital Execution

An asynchronous event-driven digital pipeline has no chip-wide barrier at all. A core or a processing stage advances only when an event demands it, and the chip has no shared clock signal for a barrier to be built on top of. SynSense's Speck and its predecessor core DYNAP-CNN are the confirmed instances in this survey's audited evidence, a fully asynchronous, clockless system-on-chip integrating a DVS with nine fixed convolution, integrate-and-fire, and pooling cores, reporting a single event crossing all nine layers in under four microseconds regardless of how many timesteps a software model believed it was simulating . This is Section 4.1's fourth mechanism, timestep-free event-driven execution, and it is the one mechanism in that discussion with a confirmed physical result behind it, Temporal Flexibility's deployment of a Mixed Time-step Training network to a physical Speck2e device .

The mechanism is that state updates occur strictly on event arrival, with no vector of per-layer discrete horizons for a scheduler to execute at all. The semantic effect is that a reported timestep count for a network destined for this family of hardware describes only the training-time simulation, not a quantity the chip itself schedules; Section 4.1 already states this distinction and this section does not relax it. The trade-off is that removing the barrier removes with it any single, chip-wide notion of when one inference ends and the next begins, which is why Section 10.5 records sample-duration configuration and output aggregation as host responsibilities for this family rather than native chip behavior. The deployment consequence is that Speck's time-and-termination field is recorded as host-assisted rather than native, the one point where this family's status diverges from the barrier-synchronized family's, even though both ultimately depend on a host to declare when a result should be read (Section 9.5).

5.6.3 Software-Programmable Many-Core Systems

A software-programmable many-core system executes a user-supplied program on each of many general-purpose or near-general-purpose cores rather than realizing one fixed neuron circuit in hardware. SpiNNaker 1 and SpiNNaker 2 are this survey's confirmed instances, an ARM-core-based digital many-core architecture that simulates arbitrary software-defined neuron models rather than one built-in dynamical primitive, at a default software convention near one millisecond per tick that Section 3.6 already identifies as a scheduling convention rather than a hardware limit . This is also the family in which Table 5's CUBA-LIF row places sPyNNaker's own documented default cell type, IF_curr_exp (Section 3.3).

The mechanism is that neuron and synapse behavior is compiled C code running on a general-purpose core rather than a fixed circuit, so this family's capability status for a given contract field tracks what a user is willing to implement rather than what the silicon fixes in place. The semantic effect is direct for the reset-and-threshold field. SpiNNaker's built-in cell types use PyNN-conventional hard-reset semantics by default, and reset by subtraction is achievable only by writing a bespoke C neuron model with a custom spike-detection callback, not by selecting a built-in option, which is why Section 10 records the SNN Toolbox to sPyNNaker route's threshold-and-reset field as unsupported for the audited default configuration even though the route as a whole reaches physical silicon . The trade-off is a place-and-route resource budget rather than a silicon-area one; SpiNNTools compresses multicast routing tables toward a roughly 1024-entry-per-chip router budget and partitions the application graph at close to 256 atoms per core given per-core memory limits, a mapping constraint distinct from anything a barrier-synchronized or fully asynchronous chip needs to satisfy . The deployment consequence is that this family's programmability makes an absolute claim about what it cannot do particularly risky. A capability recorded as unsupported for this family describes the audited default configuration and toolchain, not a hardware ceiling a custom kernel could not in principle raise.

5.6.4 Analog and Mixed-Signal Execution

An analog or mixed-signal system realizes neuron and synapse dynamics as physical circuit behavior evolving in continuous time, typically at a fixed acceleration factor relative to biological or wall-clock time, rather than as a discrete-time numerical recurrence. Heidelberg's BrainScaleS-1 and BrainScaleS-2 are the confirmed instances developed in depth in this survey's audited evidence, wafer-scale and single-chip analog systems physically emulating adaptive-exponential or leaky-integrate-and-fire circuits at roughly one thousand to ten thousand times accelerated analog time, requiring per-neuron calibration to correct manufacturing mismatch before a mapped network reaches usable accuracy , with its own experiment control scheduling stimulus and readout in fixed wall-clock frames around that accelerated core rather than on a barrier-synchronized tick . SynSense's DYNAP-SE2 and Innatera's Pulsar extend the same execution family to smaller, commercially oriented mixed-signal parts; this survey's audited evidence located no rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion pipeline for DYNAP-SE2, and every quantitative performance figure it located for Innatera Pulsar is vendor-sourced without an independent peer-reviewed benchmark, a gap this survey states rather than resolves .

The mechanism is that threshold crossing and reset are properties of a physical neuron circuit rather than a digital comparison against a stored number, so the threshold-and-reset field is native by construction on hardware in this family, exactly the status Section 9.5's comparison table records for BrainScaleS-2. The semantic effect is that numeric precision is not a bit width chosen by a compiler but a resolution set by circuit mismatch and calibration quality, which is why the same table records BrainScaleS-2's precision field as transformed rather than native, a mismatch-and-calibration process standing in for what a digital platform would call quantization. A caution belongs here rather than deferred to a later section. The audited physical route for hxtorch, the PyTorch interface to BrainScaleS-2, targets its non-spiking hagen mode rather than the spiking hxtorch.snn mode; the genuinely spiking, genuinely physical evidence for the chip is the hardware-in-the-loop Yin-Yang result of Section 7.3, and Section 11.2 traces both routes in full. The trade-off for the whole family is that accelerated time is a design feature for some workloads and a representational obstacle for others; a network trained assuming discrete algorithmic steps must be re-expressed against continuous circuit dynamics before either the timing or the precision fields can be evaluated as this survey's contract requires.

5.6.5 FPGA and Custom-ASIC Implementations

An FPGA or custom-ASIC implementation realizes a fixed or reconfigurable digital circuit purpose-built for one execution model rather than adapting a general-purpose many-core architecture or a continuous analog substrate to the task. UCLouvain's MorphIC is the clearest controlled same-silicon comparison available in this survey's audited evidence between two neural codes, its same-chip rate-versus-rank comparison already developed in full in Section 4.3 . A broader FPGA family, including Cerebron, SyncNN, and other accelerators surveyed for their code-fidelity properties in Section 13.1, Vision Workloads, implements the classical IF-plus-reset-by-subtraction-plus-rate-coding pipeline with varying fidelity per design; SyncNN and a subset of these designs expose reset by subtraction as an explicit, user-selectable configuration, while others do not document their reset polarity at all .

The mechanism this family shares is that the execution model is fixed into the circuit at design time rather than negotiated by a general-purpose core's software or a continuous physical process. The semantic effect is that a custom ASIC or a synthesized FPGA design can realize almost any of the neuron models Section 3.3 derives natively, at the cost of committing that choice before fabrication or synthesis rather than after. The trade-off is that the code and the reset polarity are fixed with the circuit; MorphIC carries a rate code and a rank code on one die because its designers built both in, and the FPGA designs that implement the classical pipeline document reset by subtraction only where their authors chose to expose it. The deployment consequence is that a capability status reported for this family is a statement about one fabricated or synthesized design, not about what a different FPGA bitstream or ASIC tapeout could in principle achieve; Section 8.3.4's APEX and NeuroFlex accelerator models are E4 hardware-model evidence for exactly this reason, a documented design rather than a measured chip.

5.6.6 Emerging Devices

In-memory and emerging-device systems are covered here only to deployment-relevant depth, consistent with the referral Section 2.6 already gives to the underlying device physics. Lynxi's KA200, a compute-in-memory, many-core, heterogeneous-fusion chip built on the Tianjic lineage and natively supporting both deep-learning ANNs and spiking networks with mixed-precision compute, is this survey's clearest confirmed instance, and its own access limitation, a deployment path effectively unavailable outside mainland China per the sources reviewed, is itself a deployment-relevant fact rather than a detail this survey sets aside .
