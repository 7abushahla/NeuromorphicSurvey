11 Where an SNN Runs

11.1 Three Targets, One Network

The target of a deployment claim is the machine the claim describes, not the paper's headline platform. Three buckets cover every target this survey audits. A conventional processor carries the chip words CPU, GPU, MCU, RISC-V, NPU, and event NPU, whatever software steps the network. A simulated target carries simulator, a model of neuromorphic hardware that is not the silicon. A neuromorphic target carries neuromorphic or FPGA, fabricated silicon or a custom spiking accelerator measured as its own target. Table 99 in Section 5.4, Evidence Guide, gives the meaning and the exclusion fixed for each bucket.

The three buckets restate machinery Figure 4 and Figure 8 already draw. The simulated target is the second crossing Figure 4 draws, the right column's equations stepped on the left column's machine, a spiking network run as a program again with its state in memory and its time the clock. The two execution stacks Figure 8 lays side by side, a trained network compiled down to a CPU, GPU, MCU, or NPU on one side and mapped down to fabricated silicon on the other, are the two ends of the same walk this section audits. What a chip model can reproduce on paper and what only silicon measures is exactly what the contract fields of Section 13.7, Platforms Compared Through the Contract, compare field by field, native neuron dynamics against emulated reset, transformed precision against native code.

Table 98 places the three targets side by side against what each executes, the evidence ceiling it can reach, what its latency and energy numbers measure, what they cannot show, and when a converted or a directly trained network belongs on it.

11.2 Conventional Platforms

On a general-purpose processor a spiking network is a program again, its state resident in memory and its time the processor's own clock, the crossing Figure 4 draws from the neuromorphic column back to the conventional one. A spike becomes a value inside a tensor rather than an event on a bus, and sparsity in that tensor pays off only where the kernel underneath it is written to skip a zero, the distinction Section 2.3, Dense Computation, Sparse Activity, and Conditional Execution, already draws and Section 2.5, Why Representation Constrains Execution, extends to hardware. SpikingJelly, snnTorch, and Norse step this representation on PyTorch, GeNN and mlGeNN step it through code generation, and NEST and Brian2 step it on CPU clusters; Section 11.4, Simulator and Library Catalog, catalogs what each executes and reaches, not repeated here. Four measured runs anchor the bucket rather than a library's own claim about itself. On an NVIDIA A100 GPU, a training benchmark reaches up to an eleven-fold speedup over two comparator libraries (E1 GPU). On a twelve-gigabyte Titan V GPU, a converted VGG-16 model runs two-and-a-half times faster than a comparator simulator (E1 GPU). On a thirty-two-node Intel Xeon cluster, a full-scale cortical microcircuit reaches a comparable energy per synaptic event to physical SpiNNaker only after roughly a 4.6-fold slowdown (E1 CPU), and across the K computer's 82,944 nodes, the same simulation software's own scaling report measures wall-clock time from 128 nodes upward (E1 CPU). Each of the four states what it measured on which processor, not a property of any chip its network was converted from or destined for.

A converted network on a microcontroller runs as compiled fixed-point code rather than an interpreted graph, the pattern the CMSIS-NN kernels of Lai et al. set for Cortex-M cores. On an ARM Cortex-M4, a converted spiking model measures 6.1 milliseconds and 48 microjoules per inference, a 23 percent reduction against the 60 microjoules its 8-bit CNN counterpart draws on the same board (E1 MCU). RISC-V accelerators for spiking inference are surveyed at length by Du et al. and by Farsa et al., and on a RISC-V soft vector processor synthesized onto a Kria KV260 FPGA, an SNN classifier runs faster than an embedded GPU and faster than physical Loihi while using half the energy (E1 RISC-V). What either run measures is the core's own cycle count and its energy at the board boundary. Neither measures a property of neuromorphic silicon, whatever chip a comparison sentence in the same paper cites alongside it.

A dense neural processing unit executes low-bit matrix multiplication natively rather than stepping a simulator on general-purpose cores, and a vendor toolchain can carry one converted network to either target as a documented option, one multi-backend compiler routing through code generation to either a many-core, compute-in-memory chip or a conventional CPU or GPU fallback within a single build. IBM's NorthPole belongs in this bucket for a reason worth stating precisely. It is a purely digital, low-precision matrix-multiply accelerator with no spiking or event-driven neuron model and no membrane-potential dynamics, so its reported efficiency comes from memory locality and low-bit arithmetic, not from event-driven temporal sparsity; it carries the NPU chip rather than the neuromorphic bucket on the strength of a shared vendor lineage alone. An STM32-embedded neural accelerator sits in the same bucket at smaller scale, executing an already-quantized spiking model 7.6 times faster and at under a quarter the energy per sample of the same board's Cortex-M55 CPU path (E1 NPU). BrainChip's Akida is a distinct case, an event-domain accelerator whose documented route runs a converted, quantized CNN as a single pass of sparse events and exposes no spiking neuron model integrated over T, which is why it carries the separate event NPU chip rather than the dense NPU word above.

11.3 Simulated and Emulated Neuromorphic Hardware

A chip's own simulator ships with the same toolchain that targets the physical part, and what it reproduces is stated, or left unstated, in that toolchain's own documentation. Loihi's software stack ships a bit-accurate simulation of the chip's own behavior, runnable on a conventional CPU before any board is touched. Xylo's own simulator, distributed with its host framework, is documented for training and evaluating a network before deployment to the physical digital core; whether it also reproduces the core's timing is not stated in the sources read for this survey. Akida's software backend converts and quantizes a network and estimates its energy per inference through a power-meter model, a documented estimate rather than a bench measurement of the fabricated part. BrainScaleS-2's own interface distinguishes a mock, numerical simulation mode from genuine execution on the physical chip explicitly, rather than treating the two as equivalent. SpiNNaker's own software stack, by contrast, is documented as reaching SpiNNaker hardware exclusively; no software-only simulator path for it is stated in the sources read for this survey.

A register-transfer-level or cycle-level model of a spiking accelerator produces a count before any silicon is taped out, and that count is exactly as reliable as the synthesis or simulation tool that generated it, not as a fabricated die. One dual-sparsity design synthesized in 40-nanometer CMOS reports 1.3 to 5.4 percent power overhead and an energy per inference of 69.23 millijoules at eight timesteps against a 181.76-millijoule baseline at 32, entirely from RTL synthesis and cycle-level simulation (E4 simulator). A column-level hybrid scheduler reports 57 to 67 percent energy-delay-product reduction against an ANN-only baseline from the same kind of synthesis model, with no fabricated die behind the figure (E4 simulator). A RISC-V-controlled digital neuromorphic architecture reports 0.47 square millimeters per core and 2.8 picojoules per synaptic operation from synthesis and power-estimation software, with an accompanying thesis stating explicitly that no physical chip implementation exists at the time of writing (E4 simulator). A number from any of the three is a property of the model, not of a bench.

Two different things share the word FPGA in this literature, and the boundary between them is stated once here rather than at every mention. An FPGA that reimplements a named chip's own architecture, neuron block, router, and scheduler, to check it against that chip's own reference simulator is a simulated target, carrying the simulator chip; one such reimplementation of IBM's TrueNorth on a Xilinx Zynq UltraScale+ board verifies functional equivalence against TrueNorth's own Compass simulator and reports a 50 percent resource reduction from an architectural fix, a claim about the reimplementation, not about fabricated TrueNorth silicon (E4 simulator). An FPGA that is itself the measured target, a custom spiking accelerator synthesized and benchmarked as its own design rather than standing in for another chip, carries the FPGA chip and the neuromorphic bucket instead. What separates the two is not the silicon technology, an FPGA is programmable logic in both cases, but which chip the resulting number is a claim about. Only fabricated, physically probed silicon establishes a number at the physical boundary of Section 15.2, Measurement Boundaries,; a model, however cycle-accurate, and an emulation, however faithful, remain claims about the model and the emulation.

The six brain simulators exist to study biologically detailed neuron and synapse dynamics at network scale, not to deploy a trained model to a target. NEST and NEURON both integrate large libraries of neuron and synapse equations on ordinary CPU clusters, and neither states GPU support or a path onto neuromorphic silicon. Brian2 takes the same CPU-bound approach but compiles differential equations specified in strings into generated C++, reaching a GPU only through the separate Brian2GeNN and Brian2CUDA projects rather than through Brian2 itself. GeNN and mlGeNN invert that dependency, generating code that runs natively on NVIDIA GPUs and positioning that speed as an alternative to neuromorphic hardware rather than a route onto it. CARLsim keeps Izhikevich-level biological detail while adding CUDA acceleration on top of it. What separates a run through any of these six from a deployment claim is the fact NEST's own documentation states plainly and that holds for the group as a whole, the output is a numerical simulation of network dynamics, not hardware execution.

The twelve deep-SNN libraries target the other end of the same problem, training or running a network at the scale a convolutional or transformer architecture requires, inside a PyTorch- or TensorFlow-like workflow rather than a biological point-neuron model. SpikingJelly, snnTorch, and Norse share this shape closely, a PyTorch-native neuron library plus, for SpikingJelly, its own ANN-to-SNN conversion tools; each documents an NIR export path toward another tool without a hardware target named on the page read, so a run through any of the three stops at simulation, E5. Lava and Lava-DL name a specific target instead, Intel's Loihi, but the pages read describe that route rather than exercise it, so the CPU/GPU run stays E5 and the Loihi route stays a documented E3; Lava-DL's own repository is additionally archived. The Nengo family breaks this pattern. Nengo and NengoDL optimize and train on CPU or GPU through TensorFlow, E5, but NengoLoihi distinguishes an emulator backend written in pure Python from direct execution on physical Loihi hardware through NxSDK, a path whose own documentation reports accuracy degraded by float-to-integer discretization and partially recovered by optimization, E2 rather than E5. BindsNET is the sparsest record in the group, a peer-reviewed description of the library with neither a runs-on nor a reaches fact stated on the page read. Rockpool and Sinabs each name a chip of their own, the Xylo family and DYNAP-SE2 for Rockpool, Speck for Sinabs, again as a documented NIR export tutorial rather than a measured run. SNN Toolbox converts a trained rate-based ANN and can target SpiNNaker, Loihi, or a software backend such as pyNN or Brian2, the same conversion-then-documented-route pattern repeated once more.

The seven chip backends and emulators sit one level below the general-purpose libraries above, each naming exactly one physical target and stating how a call reaches it, rather than offering a model library of its own. The Lava Loihi Simulator, covered by Lava's own records, reproduces Loihi's behavior on ordinary CPU rather than on the chip, a bit-accurate but pre-silicon model, E4. NxSDK is the weakest record in the table, its own documentation page unreachable after repeated attempts, so its Loihi-execution claim is carried secondhand through NengoLoihi's description of it rather than confirmed on NxSDK's own page, E3 and provisional. sPyNNaker runs directly and only on fabricated SpiNNaker silicon, and its cited paper states a measured per-core throughput figure, over 5,000 synaptic events per core, E1. hxtorch keeps the sharpest internal distinction in the group, genuine execution on BrainScaleS-2 hardware that the page treats as returning measured chip data, E2, held explicitly apart from a mock simulation mode, E5. Akida's MetaTF backend targets the event NPU word rather than the neuromorphic bucket, since Akida runs a converted, quantized network as sparse events without exposing a spiking-neuron model, and its own documentation describes energy-per-inference tracking on that hardware, E1. The Xylo Simulator trains and simulates on CPU toward eventual Xylo silicon, so its own run stays E5 even though the sub-mW figure it quotes describes the deployed chip rather than the simulator. samna is the lowest-level entry, an API that controls a physical Speck device directly rather than simulating anything, a documented route with no run reported on the page read, E3.

The four hardware-emulation entries sit pre-silicon, each estimating how an SNN-executing circuit would behave rather than reporting a measurement from a fabricated die within the facts read here, so every claim in the group tops out at the class shared with the Lava Loihi Simulator above, E4, a hardware model, an RTL synthesis, or a cycle-level simulation without fabricated silicon. APEX and NeuroFlex are each their own accelerator design with no external chip named, quoting a relative efficiency gain against a software or another accelerator baseline, up to 40% energy reduction for APEX and a 57 to 67% energy-delay-product reduction for NeuroFlex, rather than an absolute measured figure. SENECA instead reports an absolute per-operation energy figure, 2.8 pJ per synaptic operation, from a design synthesized to a named process node. The fourth entry, Valancius et al.'s FPGA emulation of TrueNorth, differs from the other three in kind rather than degree, it reimplements another chip's own five-component architecture on a Xilinx development board and checks its outputs against that chip's own reference simulator, functional verification rather than a novel design's efficiency claim.

Reading the catalog against a specific question rather than by name, a biological point-neuron question is answered by NEST or NEURON for a stock model library, or by Brian2 when the model itself is a custom differential equation, every route ending at E5. A deep-network conversion at scale is answered by SpikingJelly or SNN Toolbox when the target chip named in the Reaches column matters, and by snnTorch or Norse when it does not. A chip-faithful pre-silicon run points to the Lava Loihi Simulator for Loihi behavior, or to Valancius et al.'s FPGA emulation when the chip in question is TrueNorth specifically, both capped at E4 rather than a measurement of the named chip's own silicon. A cycle count or an energy-per-operation figure is answered only by the hardware-emulation group, SENECA's 2.8 pJ per synaptic operation being the one absolute figure the fact sheet carries rather than a relative gain, and every number in that column stays a synthesis-level estimate, never a measurement the catalog can promote past E4.

11.5 Physical Neuromorphic Silicon

Every entry in the catalog above, including the four hardware-emulation designs, still describes a program, a model of a circuit, or a synthesis-level estimate of one. Fabricated neuromorphic silicon is different in kind. What runs there is the circuit's own dynamics, not a program stepped by a separate processor reading instructions from memory, and the measurement boundary a claim about it must cross is the one the evidence classes already fix, physical execution or physical accuracy read from that die itself, not a proxy computed anywhere else. Section 13, Hardware Execution Models, takes up the families this bucket contains. Section 14, Complete Deployment Routes, takes up how a trained network actually reaches one of them.
