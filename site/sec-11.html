11 Complete Deployment Routes

Section 9.5 closed by distinguishing what a platform can do in principle from what a specific published chain from training framework to silicon has actually done. This section closes that gap for seven named candidate routes onto vendor silicon, six neuromorphic chips and Akida's event NPU, and, in Section 11.3, Routes to Conventional Targets, five onto conventional processors. The seven were chosen because each pairs a training or conversion framework already introduced in Section 10 with a hardware family already introduced in Section 5, and because each is a route a reader of this literature is likely to assume exists in something like its named form. Each route is traced edge by edge against this survey's generated route index, and a route absent from that index, however plausible its two named endpoints look together, is not treated as a route at all.

11.1 What Counts as a Named Route

A named route in this section is a chain of two or more edges, each typed by the schema Section 10.4 already applies, connecting a training or conversion framework to a physical or intended hardware target. A route's overall state is not asserted independently of its edges. It is read off the platform-capability record that names the specific chain of route identifiers behind it, exactly as Section 9.4 already distinguishes a field's capability status from a route's state. Six states are available for this purpose, exact, approximate, blocked, obsolete, unexercised, and physical, fixed once in this survey's evidence vocabulary and applied here without redefinition. A route can fail at its first edge, in which case every downstream edge is conditional on a hypothetical model that never reaches it, or it can succeed through every edge to a documented physical measurement. Both outcomes are reported in what follows with the same care, because a blocked route is exactly as informative to a practitioner choosing a deployment path as a physical one.

One naming caution applies before any route is traced. The plainest name for a candidate route is not always the exact chain the generated route index records. Where this survey's evidence traces a different, more specific intermediate node than the name a reader might expect, this section states the discrepancy explicitly rather than silently substituting the expected name for the audited one.

11.2 Physical Routes

Four of the seven candidate routes onto vendor silicon, six neuromorphic chips and Akida's event NPU, reach a physical target under this survey's audited evidence, and a fifth reaches physical hardware for a different execution mode than the one its name suggests. Two further chains that Figure 25 draws, Lava-DL's SLAYER to Loihi 2 and hxtorch to BrainScaleS-2, are traced here as well, because the figure renders only what this section traces.

SNN Toolbox → NxTF → Loihi 1. SNN Toolbox's Keras, PyTorch, Caffe, and Lasagne-ingested, percentile-normalized, reset-by-subtraction IF conversion bridges into NxTF's Keras-derived compiler through a dedicated interface module, and NxTF compiles the result down through NxSDK's register-level NxCore API onto physical Loihi 1 silicon . Every edge in this chain is E1. The result is, in this survey's audited evidence, the clearest published rate-coded conversion result on physical neuromorphic silicon, an 8.52 percent CIFAR-10 error on a 413,000-neuron, 3-million-parameter MobileNet on 861 cores across 7 chips . The route's threshold-and-reset field is emulated rather than native, recovered only through the two-compartment workaround Section 10.4 already traces. That workaround is an optional mode of the bridge rather than its default, the interface falls back to hard reset when no mode is set, and the paper does not state which mode the 8.52 percent run used, so the measurement certifies the chain and not the subtractive reset on it . NxTF's own authors list a native soft-reset primitive as a desired future hardware feature specifically to remove that cost . This chain is also a closed one. NxTF and NxSDK were never ported to Loihi 2, and Intel's own 2022 Neuromorphic Research Community guidance redirects members to Lava for that generation instead . This survey's platform-capability record accordingly assigns the route state physical to this chain specifically for Loihi 1.

SNN Toolbox → sPyNNaker → SpiNNaker 1. The audited chain is more specific than a name built around the generic PyNN interface would suggest. SNN Toolbox's own SpiNNaker output module builds PyNN-based convolutional and dense layers from a parsed model and drives sPyNNaker, PyNN's SpiNNaker-specific backend, directly, rather than passing through the generic, backend-agnostic PyNN interface as an intermediate stage in its own right . sPyNNaker's own SpiNNTools stage then partitions the graph, places it, compresses routing tables to the chip's router budget, and signals synchronized execution start across physical SpiNNaker 1 silicon . An earlier, independent deep-belief-network conversion on a single physical SpiNNaker 1 chip reports 95.01 percent MNIST accuracy at 0.3 watts and roughly 20 milliseconds latency, all measured on silicon, and a later result credits SNN Toolbox's own conversion methodology at 98.20 percent MNIST accuracy on a larger SpiNNaker-103 machine, though the exact code path is not confirmed to be this specific module in every source consulted . As Section 5.6.3 already states, this route's threshold-and-reset field is unsupported for the audited default configuration. The chain runs PyNN's IF_curr_exp cell, the current-based LIF Section 3.3 files under CUBA-LIF, with a hard reset to zero on every spike, because sPyNNaker's built-in cell types use PyNN-conventional reset semantics and reset by subtraction requires a bespoke C neuron model outside the audited chain . The 0.4 seconds per sample the 98.20 percent result reports is wall-clock time for 15 milliseconds of simulated activity, on a machine that by default matches simulated time to real time. This survey's evidence also locates no CIFAR-10-scale or larger convolutional network run on physical SpiNNaker silicon through any documented path; every physical result located is digit-recognition scale or a small number of convolutional layers, a scale limit distinct from the route's own state.

Sinabs → DynapcnnNetwork → samna → Speck. Sinabs's from_torch.from_model call replaces ReLU activations with subtractive-reset integrate-and-fire layers by default, and its DynapcnnNetwork mapper restructures the result into convolution, spike, and pooling blocks, quantizing weights to 8 bits and membrane state to 16 bits . samna then applies the resulting configuration object to physical Speck silicon and streams event data in real time . Multiple physical results confirm this chain, a dynamic-SNN DVS-Gesture result reporting 81.0 percent accuracy at 9.5 milliwatts for a fixed-rate configuration against 90.0 percent accuracy at 3.8 milliwatts for a dynamic configuration on the same chip, and an official NIR-to-Speck tutorial reporting 90.00 percent on-chip accuracy on N-MNIST . The gesture network was trained directly on a GPU with surrogate gradients and then deployed, not converted; the one classical-conversion measurement on Speck in this survey's evidence is the 86.17 percent N-MNIST run of the Speck ASIC paper, the same paper that measures a single event through nine 3x3 convolution-and-pooling layers, stride 1 and padding 1, in 3.36 microseconds from input pad to output pad on Speck1 . Both measured runs streamed events from an external source and bypassed the on-die sensor, so the sensor is documented on this route and not exercised by the numbers cited here. The Speck neuron is integrate-and-fire by default, with an optional linear leak driven by a slow clock . Unlike the two routes above, this chain's threshold-and-reset field is native rather than emulated, because Speck's own hardware default, return_to_zero=False, already implements subtraction and Sinabs's own default reset function matches it without a workaround . The route still transforms other fields on the way, average pooling becomes sum pooling because spikes cannot be meaningfully averaged, a linear layer becomes a 1x1 convolution, and Speck imposes one uniform threshold per spiking layer rather than a per-neuron threshold, so native status for one field does not extend to the graph-operators or precision fields on the same chain.

Rockpool → Xylo. A trained Rockpool computational graph passes to a mapper that extracts a hardware specification and checks it against Xylo's fixed CuBa-LIF architecture through a design-rule check, and a quantization stage then fixes weights to 8 bits and thresholds to 16 bits before XyloSamna or XyloMonitor deploys the result to physical Xylo Audio 2 silicon . A published micro-power keyword-spotting benchmark on the physical XyloAudio 2 development kit, with on-board current monitors sampled at 1280 hertz, is this chain's E1 result . That benchmark reports idle, active, and dynamic power measured on the kit, 216 to 217, 468 to 514, and 251 to 298 microwatts, and a deployed quantized accuracy of 95.31 percent; the network was trained directly with surrogate gradients, its readout is the membrane potential in training and any-spike detection at test rather than a decoded rate, and the measured run encoded audio with a simulation of the device's front end and streamed the events from the host in accelerated time, so the microphone path is documented on this route and not exercised by the cited numbers . Subtractive reset is preserved as a fixed hardware property on this route rather than a software switch, matching the native threshold-and-reset status Section 9.5's comparison table already records for Xylo. The route also departs from the single-spike-per-timestep assumption most conversion theory relies on, because Xylo's core supports up to 31 events per neuron per step, a capability this survey's audited evidence does not find exploited by the specific keyword-spotting benchmark cited here.

Lava-DL SLAYER → NetX → Lava → Loihi 2. This chain is not one of the seven candidate routes, and it is a direct-training route rather than a conversion one, but Figure 25 draws it and it is traced here for that reason. A network trained in Lava-DL with SLAYER's surrogate gradient exports to the NetX HDF5 format, which Lava loads as a process graph and runs on Loihi 2 under a hardware run configuration . The measured workload is PilotNet, a steering-angle regression from dashboard video frames, built as a sigma-delta network that exchanges graded spikes rather than as a rate-coded one. Shrestha et al. report it on an Oheo Gulch single-chip Loihi 2 system, trained with Lava-DL 0.4.0 and run on Lava 0.8.0, at 0.09 millijoules per inference without input and output, 1.21 milliseconds of latency, and about 7,400 samples per second (E1 neuromorphic) . A sigma-delta ReLU unit has no membrane potential and no reset, so the threshold-and-reset field this section tracks on every other chain does not apply here; the E1 attaches to that sigma-delta workload, and the CUBA-record edge from NetX into a Lava LIF process keeps the E3 Table 26 assigns it. A PilotNet variant built from LIF blocks appears in the vendor's tutorial material, not in a peer-reviewed measurement. Two cautions frame the result. Direct training reached Loihi 2 silicon here where rate-coded conversion did not, but conversion as such has, an ANN-to-sigma-delta conversion measured on a 16-chip Loihi 2 board . And Intel archived the Lava, Lava-DL, and NetX repositories on 13 May 2026, so the chain is frozen in the state that archival captured .

hxtorch → BrainScaleS-2, a physical route to a different target than its name suggests. This chain is not one of the seven originally listed candidate routes, but it belongs in this subsection as a caution against a mistake this survey's own audited evidence makes concrete rather than hypothetical. hxtorch's perceptron primitives execute non-spiking analog matrix multiplication on BrainScaleS-2 in its hagen mode, and a physical measurement exists, latency and energy for a mobile electrocardiogram application measured on the physical system . This measurement applies to the non-spiking analog execution mode alone. It must not be read as a measurement of hxtorch.snn's spiking, leaky-integrate-and-fire execution mode, a distinct route with its own separate, E2, hardware-in-the-loop evidence already developed in Section 7.3 and Section 5.6.4 . A route can be physical and still not be the route a name suggests it is.

11.3 Routes to Conventional Targets

Every route this subsection traces ends on a conventional processor rather than on neuromorphic silicon, and each is a named route by Section 11.1's own definition, two or more typed edges from a framework to a physical hardware target.

SpikingJelly → PyTorch CUDA backend → GPU. SpikingJelly's neuron and encoding modules are ordinary PyTorch modules that PyTorch's CUDA backend runs as kernel launches on the physical GPU, and in the training benchmark its LIF layers additionally ran as SpikingJelly's own CuPy-compiled CUDA kernels. Fang et al. measure a Spiking ResNet-18 of LIF neurons at framework defaults, hard reset to zero, on random 3x224x224 input at batch size 16 with mixed precision on an NVIDIA A100 GPU, reaching up to 11x the training speed of Norse and 8x that of snnTorch at T = 32 (E1 GPU) . The paper's inference run at T = 128 times the same LIF network to stand in for converted-network use; no converted network was run. The route is physical; a spike is only a nonzero tensor value on the same dense schedule as every other, so sparsity pays nothing.

mlGeNN → GeNN CUDA code generation → GPU. mlGeNN's converted VGG-16, integrate-and-fire neurons with a hard reset to zero under data-based threshold normalization, compiles through GeNN's code generator into CUDA C++ that runs on the physical GPU. Turner et al. measure it as 2.5x faster than BindsNET on a 12 GB NVIDIA Titan V GPU (E1 GPU) . Rate-based conversion was evaluated on CIFAR-10 only; the paper's ImageNet models, a VGG-16 and a ResNet-34, are few-spike conversions. The route is physical, but, as Section 10.1, The Framework Boundary, states, it is GPU-simulation evidence about the classical correspondence at scale, not about a neuromorphic target.

NEST → NEST simulation kernel → CPU. A PyNN-described point-neuron model is handed to NEST's own C++ kernel, which integrates it as an MPI- and OpenMP-parallelized program on physical CPU cores. Van Albada et al. measure its runtime and energy against physical SpiNNaker on a 32-node Xeon cluster running NEST 2.8, and a separate performance report measures a later NEST 5g development build, running the hpc_benchmark balanced random network from a native script under weak scaling rather than the PyNN-described microcircuit, to 82,944 K computer nodes (E1 CPU) . The route is physical; either run measures CPU-cluster throughput, not a dedicated spiking substrate, and the PyNN stage of the route covers only the first.

A custom TTFS conversion pipeline → Cortex-M deployment → Cortex-M4 MCU. This chain departs from the SNN-Toolbox-to-CMSIS-NN pattern a reader might expect; the retrieved text names CMSIS-NN only as a comparator framework, never as a stage, and a paper built around an analog comparator chip instead states a custom CNN-to-SNN pipeline, using time-to-first-spike among other encodings it does not name and a firing-rate control it does, deployed on an ARM Cortex-M4 MCU. The paper says neither firmware nor compiled, and leaves open whether the digital SNN was software-simulated or hardware-accelerated on the part. Measured with an external power analyzer under the MLPerf Tiny protocol, the converted SNN uses 48 microjoules and about 6 milliseconds per inference at 87.9 percent accuracy on the physical Cortex-M4, against 60 microjoules at 89.5 percent for its 8-bit CNN counterpart (E1 MCU) . That E1 carries a source-quality caveat. The paper does not state which task or dataset produced those values, its method section is titled as a proposed evaluation framework, its table holds round values without the confidence intervals that section promises, its latency reads 6.00 milliseconds in one place and 6.1 in another, and it states neither a neuron model nor a timestep count. The route is physical for this unnamed-toolchain chain only, not evidence that CMSIS-NN or SNN Toolbox reach a Cortex-M4 target.

FeNN → Vivado RTL synthesis → RISC-V accelerator. FeNN's custom RISC-V vector instruction set is specified as SystemVerilog RTL and synthesized by Vivado 2023.2 into a soft core on a Kria KV260 FPGA; the SNN itself is RISC-V assembly that a host-side C++ just-in-time assembler on the board's Cortex-A53 emits and loads into the core's memory. Aizaz et al. report a single FeNN core running an adaptive LIF recurrent classifier of Spiking Heidelberg Digits, whose input is cochlea-model spike trains rather than sampled audio, twice as fast as a Jetson Orin Nano at half the energy, where the energy is whole-board mains energy of the KV260 against the Jetson with both under desktop Linux, and faster than the Loihi time the paper takes from a prior study of a similar model rather than measures itself; the successor FeNN-DMA paper measures throughput for a larger network on the physical single-core SoC from on-chip counters (E1 RISC-V) . The route is physical. FeNN-DMA's 0.53 W figure is a vectorless Vivado power estimate, not a board reading; its 175 MHz clock and its lookup-table and flip-flop counts are Vivado implementation results, and its throughput is measured on the board.

11.4 Approximate and Transformed Routes

QuantizeML → CNN2SNN → Akida. QuantizeML's uniform, symmetric integer quantization output is consumed directly by CNN2SNN's convert() call, which maps the result to Akida's .fbz runtime format; QuantizeML has already folded batch normalization into the preceding layer's weights and bias, and CNN2SNN stores that bias, plus a half-step rounding offset, as a static threshold computed once at conversion time, so that an Akida 1.0 layer's output is the accumulated integer divided by an activation step . This is a documented, first-party BrainChip pipeline order, and the Akida runtime executes the resulting model on physical AKD1000, AKD1500, or Akida Pico silicon, an E1 chain for the Akida 1.0 generation . This survey nonetheless places the route in this subsection rather than treating it as an unqualified physical instance of rate-coded conversion, because the same evidence that establishes it as physical also establishes the total collapse of the multi-timestep rate-coded correspondence of Section 8 to a single feedforward pass with no reset by subtraction, a collapse this survey traces as an algorithmic matter in Section 8.3.6. The route is scoped to BrainChip's standard converted-CNN execution and does not establish the semantics of every sequential, recurrent, or otherwise undocumented Akida mode. For the Akida 2.0 generation specifically, this survey's platform-capability record states the route state as unexercised rather than physical, because the same software chain establishes a generic Akida target without demonstrating a measured run on Akida 2.0 FPGA or production silicon specifically . Reaching physical silicon and preserving the contract fields a reader might assume a rate-coded conversion route preserves are, in this one chain, two separate and partially conflicting claims.

11.5 Blocked and Unexercised Routes

Two of the seven originally listed candidate routes are blocked at their first edge, and neither reaches the hardware target its name promises.

SpikingJelly → Lava → Loihi 2. The network SpikingJelly's rate-coding recipe emits, a classical ReLU-to-IF conversion with maximum or percentile threshold calibration whose default neuron carries subtractive reset, v_reset=None, is rejected by its own lava_exchange module before it ever reaches Lava-DL's NetX schema, exactly as Section 3.5 already establishes and Table 26 (rows 1 and 2) types edge by edge. The rejection is layered. The exporter accepts only a flat list, tuple, or nn.Sequential of synapse-and-neuron pairs, while the recipe returns a traced graph module with voltage-scaling modules on either side of each neuron, so the converted network fails on shape before any neuron is inspected; a neuron that does reach the check trips the reset error; and a layer that passes both is dispatched only if it is a linear, convolution, average-pooling, or flatten layer, so a VGG max-pooling layer fails in the dispatch, while a ResNet skip connection never gets that far because the graph cannot be passed at all. SpikingJelly's QCFS-based recipe fares no better, since its activation-aware neuron is not an IFNode subclass and the exporter refuses it with a NotImplementedError. This is a blocked route at the first edge for the default, published conversion output, not an unexercised or merely undocumented one; the exporter fails with a stated, deliberate error rather than silently miscompiling or simply lacking an example. A separately configured hard-zero model can enter the restricted downstream chain, lava_exchange to NetX's HDF5 schema to the Lava runtime, provided it is also rebuilt as a flat nn.Sequential without the recipe's scaling modules, but that is a different model than the one the classical conversion recipe this survey has developed throughout Section 8 actually produces, and no official example exercising that alternative, hard-zero-configured chain end to end to physical Loihi 2 was located in this survey's audited evidence . The downstream edges this alternative chain would use, lava_exchange to NetX and NetX to Loihi 2, are independently recorded as E3, documented but unexercised for this specific source contract, and the entire Lava, Lava-DL, and NetX stack's 2026 archival by Intel (Section 10.6) freezes whatever state each downstream edge held at the point of archival.

SpikingJelly → NIR → available backends. The same default reset configuration blocks this second candidate route at the same first edge for a related but distinct reason. SpikingJelly's nir_exchange module traces the model graph but raises NotImplementedError, with the message that NIR does not distinguish soft reset, whenever v_reset is not set, because NIR 1.0.8's IF, LIF, and CubaLIF primitives, six neuron models among the seventeen primitives the format defines, specify a fixed-value reset and carry no field that distinguishes reset by subtraction from reset to a value . The format itself rejects nothing. NIR's own paper names subtractive reset as a valid discretization of the same primitives and exports a subtractive-reset network through them, and the snnTorch and Sinabs exporters send such neurons through NIR as IF or LIF with a zero reset value, leaving the choice to the importer . The block is therefore SpikingJelly's exporter policy, a deliberate and conservative refusal to export what the specification cannot distinguish, in the terms Section 10.3 already uses, rather than a bug in the exporter or a limit the format enforces. Once this first edge is blocked, the phrase available backends in the candidate route's name cannot be evaluated for SpikingJelly's own converted output, because there is no NIR graph for those backends to receive. NIR's fan-out to other frameworks remains real for other sources that do produce a representable graph, Sinabs to NIR is approximate and E2-evidenced through a physical Speck tutorial, and NIR to an official Lava or Lava-DL example is approximate through a documented but narrower zero-reset transformation, but neither of these is the SpikingJelly-authored chain the candidate route names, and NIR's own onward route to SpiNNaker 2 is independently blocked for the identical reset reason, at the identical first edge, before py-spinnaker2's importer is ever reached .

A further, more specific route belongs in this subsection precisely because its absence is evidentially informative rather than an oversight. This survey located no documented route, inside the public Lava or NetX toolchains or outside them, for implementing subtractive reset through custom Loihi 2 microcode. Loihi 2's neuron engine is documented as fully microcode-programmable, which makes impossibility an unwarranted inference from this absence . This survey accordingly records the edge as undocumented, a distinct route state from blocked, obsolete, or unexercised, and one that leaves the question open for a future audit rather than closing it in either direction.

11.6 Choosing a Route

The seven routes onto vendor silicon traced in this section, six neuromorphic chips and Akida's event NPU, and the five onto conventional processors in Section 11.3, Routes to Conventional Targets, do not rank neatly from best to worst, because the field each one preserves, transforms, or fails to reach at all is not the same field from route to route. A practitioner whose deployment claim depends specifically on reset by subtraction executing without a resource-doubling workaround has, in this survey's audited evidence, exactly three physical options, native subtraction on Speck through Sinabs, native subtraction on Xylo through Rockpool, or emulated subtraction on Loihi 1 through NxTF at the cost of a second compartment per neuron; SpiNNaker's two generations and the QuantizeML-to-Akida chain fail this specific requirement even though three of those four chains are otherwise physical. A practitioner whose claim depends only on reaching some physical target with a rate-coded, multi-step, feedforward convolutional network, without a further requirement on reset semantics, has a wider physical option set, but should read the QuantizeML-to-Akida route's own transformation of the timestep and reset fields as a warning against assuming that physical execution certifies the specific correspondence Section 8 derives. A practitioner drawn to SpikingJelly for its PyTorch-native training convenience should read this section's two blocked routes as a concrete cost of that convenience at the export boundary, not a permanent one, since a hard-zero-configured model rebuilt as a flat Sequential can enter the restricted Lava-DL chain that a subtractive-reset model cannot, but a cost that changes what the exported network computes relative to the one actually trained.
