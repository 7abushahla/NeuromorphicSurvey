13 Deployment Evidence and Findings

This section inventories the deployment evidence located during this survey's research passes, organized by the workload each case addresses rather than by the vendor or chip it targets. A platform inventory belongs to Section 5 and a route trace belongs to Section 11; this section asks a different question, what was actually run, on what input, and measured how. For every case, this survey records input provenance, the learning route that produced the deployed weights, direct training or ANN-to-SNN conversion in the sense Section 6 fixes, the neural code carried at inference, the portion of the pipeline that executes on the neuromorphic device itself rather than on a host, the comparator baseline, the measurement boundary from Section 12.2, what the source states about reproducibility, and the claim-level evidence class from Section 1.3's E1 through E5 scheme. Direct-training deployments already carry a dedicated treatment in Section 7, including the on-chip-learning capability survey of that section's Table 13, and several cases below cross-reference that treatment rather than repeating it; conversion deployments receive the same workload-level treatment here for the first time. Both routes appear side by side in every subsection, because the workload, not the route, is the organizing axis.

13.1 Vision Workloads

Section 8 already noted a gap this subsection returns to. None of the finite-time-error mechanisms and none of the low-latency conversion interventions surveyed in Section 8 report execution on physical neuromorphic silicon in their own papers; every case in this subsection that reaches silicon under a rate code does so through a separate, later engineering paper applying a documented toolchain to a classical or QCFS-lineage correspondence, not through the algorithm paper that first proposed the correspondence. The NxTF compiler of Rueckauer et al. illustrates the pattern from two directions within one paper. It carries a four-layer convolutional network trained conventionally and converted through SNN Toolbox's rate-coded, reset-by-subtraction pipeline to physical Loihi 1 for MNIST and, separately, an off-the-shelf MobileNet converted the same way for CIFAR-10, and it also carries a SLAYER-trained convolutional network for N-MNIST and DVS Gestures on the identical physical chip, a direct-training result rather than a converted one. Massa et al. deploy a gesture-recognition network to physical Loihi 1 through the same SNN Toolbox and NxSDK route, but their pipeline first accumulates DVS events into frames before conversion, so the reported accuracy and per-frame latency answer a question about a frame-accumulated input, not about native event input, a distinction Section 12.1's first row already flags. Patiño-Saucedo et al. carry the same two-route pattern onto SpiNNaker, converting a LeNet-style network through SNN Toolbox for MNIST and separately training a network directly by spatio-temporal backpropagation (STBP) for N-MNIST on the same 48-chip SpiNNaker-103 machine, predating the QCFS lineage by construction. Chen, Gao, and Fu's Cerebron, Panchapakesan, Fang, and Li's SyncNN, and Stromatias et al.'s spiking deep belief network extend the same rate-coded, converted-network pattern to physical FPGAs and to SpiNNaker respectively, each reporting accuracy alongside latency, throughput, or power measured on real boards rather than synthesis estimates. Li et al.'s FireFly v2 accelerator reports the highest clock frequency among the FPGA accelerators surveyed here, though its own record does not disclose whether the evaluated networks were converted or directly trained. Read together, this cluster is the clearest physical evidence in the audited corpus that a rate-coded, IF-neuron convolutional network, trained by ordinary means and converted through a documented toolchain, reaches physical silicon, and every instance of it that does so is an engineering paper built on top of an already-published correspondence rather than the correspondence's own proposing paper.

A second cluster abandons the rate code at the point of conversion itself, already established as a code-family and intervention-family case in Sections 4 and 8. Lenz, Orchard, and Sheik's Quartz converts to a time-to-first-spike code and reaches physical Loihi 1 for MNIST and CIFAR-10, an accuracy-energy trade against rate-coded baselines rather than an unconditional improvement (Section 4.3). Brehove et al.'s Sigma-Delta Neural Network conversion targets Loihi 2's native graded-spike channel for a frame-based workload, and Adaptive Fission's population-coding transformation reports, inside its own hardware table, unmodified QCFS and SRP baseline configurations measured on the same physical accelerator, the one documented instance in this survey's audited evidence in which an unmodified rate-coded QCFS-lineage output has been measured on physical silicon, achieved by a different paper's non-rate-coded contribution rather than by QCFS's or SRP's own authors. Section 13 returns to what this pattern implies about the two learning populations; the point recorded here is only that abandoning the rate code, in every confirmed vision case in this subsection, is what carried a low-latency conversion method to physical silicon.

A third cluster processes event-native vision directly, without a frame-accumulation stage, and is dominated by direct training. Amir et al.'s TrueNorth gesture-recognition system reads a live DVS128 camera feed straight through to a decision with no host-side frame reconstruction, the strongest sensor-to-decision case in this subsection. Yao et al.'s Speck-based dynamic-computing chip and Richter et al.'s Speck1, already introduced in Section 7 for their surrogate-gradient training route, both carry directly trained networks to physical event-native silicon, and Richter et al.'s own head-to-head comparison of BPTT training against an ANN-to-SNN converted baseline on the identical chip is the clearest same-chip route comparison this survey found for a vision workload. The HiAER-Spike and CRI project converts a SpikingJelly-trained network to physical FPGA-based neuromorphic hardware, one of a small number of SpikingJelly-trained networks in the audited evidence that reach any physical chip, cross-referenced from Section 7. Du et al.'s Temporal Flexibility deploys a single weight set trained with a mixed-timestep augmentation to a physical Speck2e device, reporting on-chip accuracy alongside a measured simulator-to-chip difference, an E2 neuromorphic result already introduced in Section 4 for its timestep-free, event-driven execution rather than a discrete per-layer schedule. Esser et al.'s EEDN trains a network directly under TrueNorth's own hardware constraints, binary neurons and trinary synapses via constrained backpropagation, mapping natively onto the chip rather than converting a separately trained ANN, and reports accuracy, throughput, and power measured on physical silicon across several frame-based vision benchmarks. uBrain's fabricated 40-nanometer ASIC is explicitly designed to ease ANN-to-SNN conversion, reporting power and energy per classification on a radar hand-gesture dataset and on a converted MNIST network from the same fabricated die, and Frenkel et al.'s ODIN reports on-chip local plasticity for a small MNIST network at measured energy per synaptic operation, also on fabricated silicon. For the FPGA accelerators and the single-board neuromorphic systems below whose papers report the programmed device measured on its development board without stating what share of the measurement a driving host contributed, Table 29 assigns the board-level boundary by convention, and a reader who needs the host share should consult the cited paper directly.

13.2 Audio and Temporal Workloads

Mészáros et al.'s EventProp pipeline trains a leaky integrate-and-fire network with learned synaptic delays through an exact spike-time gradient and deploys it to a physical single-chip Loihi 2 for keyword spotting on the Spiking Heidelberg Digits and Spiking Speech Commands benchmarks, already introduced in Section 7 for its training mechanism; the workload-relevant addition here is that both benchmarks are genuinely temporal classification tasks, not frame-accumulated vision relabeled as audio. Bos and Muir's Xylo Audio 2 deployment for the Aloha keyword-spotting benchmark follows a commercial, direct-training-first pipeline through Rockpool, reporting accuracy, dynamic power, and dynamic energy per inference on physical, purpose-built silicon compared against GPU, CPU, and other embedded baselines. Carpegna, Savino, and Di Carlo's Spiker+ framework, already noted in Section 13.1 for its configurable reset, evaluates the same generated accelerator on the Spiking Heidelberg Dataset and on AudioMNIST alongside its MNIST result, reporting accuracy, latency, and power from physical FPGA synthesis and deployment. Frenkel and Indiveri's ReckOn is a single fabricated processor evaluated across three different workloads, DVS Gestures, Spiking Heidelberg Digits, and a synthetic rodent-navigation task, using an eligibility-propagation rule for on-chip learning and reporting accuracy and energy per synaptic operation from five fabricated dies; its Spiking Heidelberg Digits result is the case recorded here, and its other two workloads recur in Table 30. Ortone et al.'s tactile-localization system belongs in this subsection rather than in vision because its represented variable is a continuously varying touch signal rather than a static image, and it is a hand-designed circuit, not a trained network in the direct-training or conversion sense; a Bragg-grating-based e-skin drives a population of DYNAP-SE analog neurons for tactile localization, reporting localization error against a software baseline alongside resting and median inference power measured on physical, mixed-signal silicon. Biosignal decoding, electromyography-based gesture classification and electroencephalography-based seizure prediction, is documented in Section 7 (Section 7.2) at the same physical evidence class; those cases fall outside the measurement-index population this subsection draws its workload-level fields from, so they are referenced rather than re-tabulated here.

Every case in this subsection is either directly trained or hand-designed with local plasticity; none is an ANN-to-SNN conversion. This is a narrower population than vision's, and this survey does not read the absence of a converted audio or temporal case here as evidence that no such conversion has ever been deployed, only that this survey's audited corpus did not locate one.

13.3 Control and Robotics

Robotics is the workload domain in which this survey's audited evidence is most heavily direct-trained or hand-designed, and in which a single case most often combines several evidence-relevant properties at once, closed-loop actuation, a non-rate code, and a comparison against a conventional embedded baseline. Arfa et al. combine hardware-aware fine-tuning with eight-bit quantization to deploy directly trained spiking Q-networks, reinforcement-learning policies for CartPole and Acrobot, to physical SpiNNaker2, already introduced in Section 7 for its quantization mechanism; the workload here is closed-loop control rather than classification, and the policy's action at each tick is itself the deployed output. Tang et al.'s mapless-navigation policy, introduced in Section 6 as a hybrid method that applies a Loihi-specific integer weight-rescaling step after gradient-based training, is measured on physical Loihi hardware at a reported reduction in energy per inference relative to a conventional deep-reinforcement-learning baseline on an embedded GPU. A separate, industrial peg-in-hole force-control policy, also introduced in Section 7, trains a spiking reinforcement-learning policy end to end in simulation and ports it to both physical Loihi 1 and Loihi 2 for a KUKA robotic arm, reporting latency and dynamic energy against named CPU and GPU comparators on the same insertion task. Cheng and Nikolić's goalkeeper robot, already placed in Section 7's hand-designed local-plasticity cluster, maps a live DVS camera stream through SpiNNaker to a servomotor in a closed loop disconnected from any host PC, reporting response latency, interception accuracy against balls moving up to one meter per second, and maximum system power, the clearest sensor-to-decision robotics case in the audited evidence. Tang et al.'s head-direction SLAM circuit, from the same hand-designed cluster, runs on physical Loihi and reports dynamic power roughly two orders of magnitude below a GMapping baseline on a conventional CPU at comparable localization accuracy. Pei et al.'s Tianjic chip demonstrates a hybrid ANN and SNN architecture in a single physical chip driving a real-time robotics demonstration spanning object detection, tracking, voice recognition, obstacle avoidance, and balance control, reporting throughput and power-efficiency ratios against GPU baselines from a hardware-in-the-loop demonstration rather than a benchmark table. CLP-SNN, already discussed in Section 7's on-chip-learning survey (Table 13), performs online continual learning directly on physical Loihi 2 for a few-shot robotic-vision task, reporting latency and energy against a Jetson Orin Nano baseline; it sits at the boundary between vision and robotics because its workload is object recognition, but its deployment claim is about on-chip adaptation for a robotic platform rather than a fixed classifier.

Every closed-loop case in this cluster, the goalkeeper, the SLAM circuit, and Tianjic's demonstration, reports either a hand-designed circuit or a hybrid architecture rather than a network converted from a pretrained ANN by the classical or QCFS-lineage correspondence Section 8 formalizes. The reinforcement-learning cases, Arfa et al., Tang et al.'s navigation policy, and the force-control policy, are directly trained by gradient descent. No case in this subsection is a rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion of the kind Section 13.1 documents for vision, a gap this survey states rather than explains, since a causal account of why the two learning branches concentrate differently across workloads is not something the audited evidence itself supports.

13.4 Scientific and Industrial Workloads

This survey's audited evidence for scientific and industrial sensing outside vision, audio, and closed-loop control is thin, two confirmed cases, and this subsection states that population size honestly rather than padding it with cases that belong more naturally elsewhere. Imam and Cleland deploy a hand-designed olfactory-bulb-model circuit that learns odor identity online through one-shot local plasticity on physical Loihi silicon, with no backpropagation and no ANN-to-SNN conversion step anywhere in the pipeline, reporting latency and energy per sniff against a wind-tunnel chemosensor dataset. This survey records a caveat the audited evidence itself carries. A 2023 replication study by Dennler, van Schaik, and Schmuker found that the underlying chemosensor dataset suffers from sensor drift and a non-randomized acquisition protocol, and that a simple hash-table baseline matches or exceeds the circuit's reported accuracy once the data is split properly. The hardware latency and energy measurement itself is not invalidated by this finding, but the algorithmic and scientific claim attached to it is substantially undercut, and this survey reports the measurement at E1 neuromorphic while flagging the accuracy claim as unresolved rather than folding the two together into one verdict. Andrei et al. extend Few-Spikes conversion to complex-valued layers for an unfolded multidimensional harmonic-retrieval network, a signal-processing workload rather than a classification benchmark, and map the result to a physical single-chip SpiNNaker2 board, reporting board power against a Jetson Xavier baseline for a serialized temporal Few-Spikes code rather than a spike-count rate estimate, already introduced in Section 8 for its non-rate-coded conversion.

This survey's own research passes did not locate a physically or near-physically deployed case of industrial anomaly detection, large-scale neural simulation informing a deployment claim, or an emerging sequence or transformer-style SNN workload with a matching measurement-index record. Their absence here is a statement about what this survey's audited corpus contains, not a claim that such deployments do not exist.

13.6 Population Counts

Section 1 asked whether a recent low-timestep ANN-to-SNN conversion method's own deployment route reaches named physical silicon with measurement, or whether deployment instead proceeds through an older pipeline, a directly trained model, or a platform-native code. Section 13.5 closed by naming this section as the place that question is answered, with counts, a falsifier, and counterexamples rather than an assumed verdict. This section does that under an explicit counting policy stated before every number, and it keeps a demonstrated finding, a count or a verified claim, separate from a causal or sociological reading of that finding. Sections 13.6 through 13.9 report only what the audited evidence demonstrates. Section 13.10 states, and marks as such, the interpretations that evidence does not itself adjudicate.

Every count in this subsection is computed from data/evidence-papers.json, the audited paper population underlying this survey's conversion-track evidence, current to 17 September 2026; the falsifier search of Section 8.12 keeps its own cutoff of 16 September 2026. Each of its 90 records carries a population field with three possible values, algorithm, deployment, or both, and a family field naming the method cluster the record belongs to. A paper carrying claims on two targets is two records, one per target, under the rule Section 1.3, How Claims Are Graded, states for a claim graded on two targets, which is why the record count exceeds the paper count of 89 by one. The counting rule is the field value itself, tallied by population and cross-tabulated by family, with no manual reclassification. Population and evidence class are independent axes by construction, exactly as Section 1.3, How Claims Are Graded, already establishes for individual claims generally, so a record tagged algorithm can still carry hardware evidence up to E3 or E4 without being tagged deployment, and a handful of the algorithm-tagged records in this corpus do exactly that.

An exclusive count partitions the 90 records into three disjoint classes. Algorithm-only is 43 records that propose a method without the proposing paper itself reporting that method's execution on named hardware. Deployment-only is 40 records that execute an existing or hardware-oriented design rather than introducing a new one. Both is 7 records that introduce a method and report that same method's own physical hardware execution. An inclusive count instead asks two separate questions of the same corpus. How many records propose an algorithm at all? Adding the 7 both-tagged records to the 43 algorithm-only records gives 50. How many records report a hardware measurement at all? Adding the same 7 to the 40 deployment-only records gives 47. Neither count is more authoritative than the other; they answer different questions, and this survey states both rather than collapsing them into a single headline figure.

The eight families are the ones this survey's Sections 5, 7, 8 and 13 already organize the primary literature around, classical conversion, low-latency conversion, mixed-timestep and early-exit interventions, population coding, direct training, hardware-accelerator implementations, and workload applications.

13.7 The Rate-Coding Falsifier and Its Counterexamples

The rate-coding falsifier, that no audited source both proposes a recent, low-timestep, rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion algorithm and reports that same algorithm measured on named physical silicon (Section 8.12), is this subsection's starting point. What follows reads that finding directly off Table 31 and widens it with three verified counterexamples.

Table 31's low-latency-conversion row makes the finding concrete. Of its 17 records, 13 are algorithm-only, every one of them rate, burst, or signed-rate coded, and every one E5, software simulation with no chip named in the experiments section. One is tagged deployment rather than both, a time-to-first-spike conversion pipeline benchmarked on a physical ARM Cortex-M4 MCU, not rate-coded and not a paper's own report of an algorithm it proposes. The remaining 3 are tagged both, meaning the proposing paper itself reports E1 physical hardware execution, and all 3 abandon rate coding at the point of conversion to do so. Quartz converts to a time-to-first-spike code and reaches physical Loihi 1. Brehove et al. convert to Loihi 2's native graded sigma-delta channel. Andrei et al. extend Few-Spikes conversion to a serialized temporal code on physical SpiNNaker2. Zero of the 17 records in this row are both rate-coded and E1 in their own paper. This is exactly the gap the falsifier names, read directly off Table 31 rather than asserted separately from it.

Three verified counterexamples widen this picture without weakening the narrow claim above, and each answers one of three separate questions this survey does not collapse into one. Did the proposing paper deploy its own contribution? Adaptive Fission's own hardware table includes unmodified no-Fission QCFS and SRP baseline rows measured on a physical Lynxi HP201 accelerator, an E1 result neither the QCFS nor the SRP paper itself reported. Rate-coded QCFS-lineage networks have therefore reached silicon, achieved by a later, different paper's population-coding contribution rather than by QCFS's or SRP's own authors. Is a rate-coded, low-timestep SNN reaching silicon confined to conversion at all? Arfa et al. report physical SpiNNaker2 latency, power, and energy for rate-coded spiking Q-networks run for 10 or 20 ticks, a genuine rate-coded, low-timestep result on named silicon that falls outside the falsifier's scope because the method is direct Q-learning with hardware-aware fine-tuning, not ANN-to-SNN conversion. Does a vendor's own product label settle the coding question? BrainChip's Akida deployments are physical and are called SNNs by the vendor, yet its standard conversion route collapses to a single feedforward pass with no timestep window and no reset by subtraction (Section 8.11). Akida's physical results therefore do not satisfy the falsifier's requirement that hidden activations retain a binary spike-count or firing-rate representation over a finite window, independent of what the runtime is named.

This falsifier's scope excludes direct-training methods, application papers that reuse an existing converter, classical conversion deployments audited under Section 8's earlier correspondence, non-silicon RTL or cycle-level synthesis, and later third-party baseline deployments of an existing method. A future paper that introduces a new low-timestep conversion algorithm whose hidden activations retain a rate or spike-count representation, and that itself reports at least one on-device measurement on named physical neuromorphic silicon, weakens this finding. None of the sources audited here does so.

13.8 Semantic Findings on Reset, Operators, and Time

Reset semantics are the field this survey has traced in the greatest cross-toolchain depth, and the corpus-level pattern is asymmetric. Of the eleven typed edges Table 26 applies the input, output, preservation, transformation, rejection, and evidence schema to, two reject the source model outright, four transform subtractive reset into a different rule, one is undocumented, one concerns the sample boundary rather than the reset rule, and only two preserve subtractive reset, one only through the two-compartment emulation NxTF requires on Loihi 1 and the other natively through Sinabs's route to Speck. A third preserving row, Lava-DL NetX HDF5 to the Lava LIF process, preserves a hard-zero reset instead. At platform level, Table 24's nine audited platforms span four of the six capability statuses this survey defines for the threshold-and-reset field, native on Speck, Xylo, and BrainScaleS-2, emulated on Loihi 1, transformed on both Akida generations, unsupported on the audited routes to Loihi 2 and both SpiNNaker generations. No audited platform in Table 24 recovers subtractive reset through a native, single-compartment hardware primitive on a chip whose own default behavior is reset-to-zero.

Graph operators show the opposite pattern, uniform rather than spread. Every one of the nine platforms in Table 24 carries a transformed status for graph operators and supported operations, meaning no platform accepts an arbitrary computation graph without a mapper restructuring, splitting, or rejecting unsupported topology first. Encoder and decoder status is equally uniform, host-assisted at all nine platforms, confirming at corpus scale that no audited platform performs both input encoding and output decoding entirely on-chip.

Time model findings come from a different axis than either field above. Section 4.1 and Audit A distinguish four mechanisms that this survey's evidence keeps structurally separate, a static per-layer horizon set before inference, a per-input early-exit horizon set during inference, asynchronous within-core computation coexisting with a chip-wide barrier, and timestep-free event-driven execution with no discrete horizon at all. Among PASCAL, QAC, MT-SNN, and Temporal Flexibility, the four sources this survey audited for static heterogeneous per-layer scheduling, none demonstrates that schedule executing on physical or even cycle-simulated hardware; PASCAL's and APEX's own RTL synthesis evidence covers only a uniform-timestep neuron overhead check, not the mixed-horizon schedule itself. Barrier synchronization coexisting with asynchronous within-core computation is independently confirmed for Loihi and Tianjic-lineage systems, and this coexistence does not, on its own, establish that different layers hold independent discrete time coordinates, the non-equivalence Section 4.1 already states. Timestep-free execution is the one mechanism in this group with a confirmed physical result, Temporal Flexibility's deployment to a physical Speck2e device, and that result is event-driven and horizon-free at the point of execution, not a demonstration of the static mixed-horizon schedule the other three sources study only in software.

13.10 Evidence and Interpretation

Sections 13.6 through 13.9 report counts, verified claims, route states, and sourced facts. Nothing above this point assigns a cause to any of those findings. The paragraphs below state candidate explanations explicitly as interpretation, not as an extension of the evidence.

Why zero of the 17 low-latency-conversion records in Table 31 pair rate coding with the proposing paper's own physical hardware measurement admits several candidate explanations that the audited evidence does not adjudicate between. One reading is architectural, that a multi-step, binary, count-based representation is a genuinely poor match for the barrier-synchronized or fully asynchronous execution families Section 5 compares, so groups that abandon rate coding are responding to a real hardware constraint. A second reading is a matter of engineering cost, that building a hardware-specific pipeline for one platform is a separate, substantial effort from the software contribution a conversion paper is otherwise built around, so most conversion papers simply stop before that step regardless of the code's hardware suitability. A third reading is about publication incentives, that a venue rewarding a simulated accuracy curve across many timestep values gives an author little additional credit for the months of engineering a physical deployment would cost. A fourth reading is about access, that the platforms with the clearest documented capability, Loihi 2 and the archived Lava stack among them, are also the platforms gated behind community membership or now frozen without a successor toolchain, which would depress physical results independent of any property of the code itself. This survey's audited evidence is consistent with all four readings and does not distinguish among them.

Section 13.5 records a parallel, workload-level version of the same open question. Vision carries every confirmed rate-coded and non-rate-coded conversion case in the audited application evidence, while audio, temporal sensing, control, robotics, and the two scientific and industrial cases skew toward direct training and hand-designed circuits. Whether that skew reflects a property of the workloads themselves, a property of which research communities publish hardware results, or a property of which correspondence a given code admits for a given signal class is a question this survey's evidence does not answer, and it is restated here as an open interpretation rather than resolved by restating it.

One reading this survey does reject as unsupported by its own evidence is a strong impossibility claim, that rate-coded low-timestep conversion cannot reach silicon. Table 31 and Table 32 already falsify that stronger reading directly, through the Adaptive Fission baseline rows and through Akida's physical, if semantically transformed, route. The narrower, demonstrated finding is that no audited paper has yet closed the loop between proposing such a method and measuring that same method on hardware. Section 14 takes each of Section 13's demonstrated gaps, a failed contract edge, a blocked route, an unsupported or undocumented platform cell, and turns it into a stated research problem rather than repeating this section's findings as unresolved questions.
