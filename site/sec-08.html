8 ANN-to-SNN Conversion

Section 6 introduced conversion as the second branch of the fork, training a conventional network with ordinary backpropagation and only afterward re-expressing it in spikes. This section makes that re-expression precise. It fixes one derivation, built directly on the staged update and reset term of Section 3 and the firing fraction and horizon vocabulary of Section 4, and one end-to-end pipeline that every later comparison in this survey refers back to rather than re-deriving. The pipeline has five stages, named once here and used identically for the remainder of the article. Normalization removes network elements that do not fit the staged update of Section 3.2 as they stand, chiefly batch normalization, max pooling, and unbalanced residual branches. Parameter mapping chooses the integrate-and-fire weights, thresholds, and biases that make the mapped network's firing fractions track the trained network's activations. Encoding places the network's input onto the mapped network's first layer. Temporal execution runs the staged update for a finite horizon \(T\). Readout decodes a class score from the final layer. Sections 8.6 through 8.13 later locate specific error mechanisms and specific interventions at these same five named stages, so a claim there that names "the encoding stage" or "the normalization stage" refers to exactly the stage fixed here. Conversion changes the representation a trained network computes with and the semantics its units execute, and it does not make the network biologically realistic, since the converted unit remains an integrate-and-fire threshold at the efficient end of the model menu of Section 3.1.

8.1 Purpose and Architectural Assumptions

Conversion exists because training a spiking network directly, through the surrogate-gradient machinery of Section 7, remains harder to scale and slower to converge than training a conventional ReLU network with backpropagation and the mature tooling built around it. Conversion instead asks a narrower question. Given a network already trained to convergence with ordinary methods, can its computation be re-expressed exactly, or with a bounded and characterized error, using only integrate-and-fire units, binary events, and the reset rule fixed in Section 3.5. The earliest instances of this question were asked of shallow or fully connected networks and hand-built spiking equivalents , and Stromatias et al. answer a related version of it for a deep belief network mapped onto SpiNNaker before the convolutional, ReLU-specific pipeline this section derives was formalized. The derivation below is specific to that later, ReLU-specific pipeline.

The derivation and the pipeline built on it hold only under a stated set of architectural assumptions, and the failure modes catalogued in Section 8.5 are exactly the consequences of violating one of them. First, every nonlinearity the source network applies between affine layers is a ReLU, so every target activation is non-negative and, on the calibration data the mapping is built from, bounded by a finite per-layer value. Second, every connection is feedforward with zero delay between layers, \(d_{ij}^l=0\) in the notation of Section 3.2, deferring configurable synaptic delay to the platform-specific treatment in Section 5. Third, batch normalization, where present, uses fixed running statistics rather than batch statistics computed at inference time, so that it is a fixed affine map rather than a data-dependent one. Fourth, pooling and residual connections are restricted to the forms Section 8.3 shows fit the derivation, or are rewritten into those forms before mapping. Fifth, the per-step synaptic drive into every unit is bounded, so that the membrane potential defined in Section 3.2 stays within a fixed range independent of the horizon \(T\); Section 8.2 uses this bound directly, and Section 8.5 returns to what happens when it is violated.

8.2 The ReLU-to-Firing-Fraction Correspondence

Specialize the staged update of Section 3.2 to the IF instance fixed in Section 3.3, with the feedforward, zero-delay synaptic stage \(q_i^l[t]=\sum_j w_{ij}^l z_j^{l-1}[t]\) and the fixed threshold \(\theta_i^l\), and apply reset-by-subtraction from Section 3.5. Writing the three stages as one recurrence for the post-reset state, \[ u_i^l[t] = u_i^l[t-1] + q_i^l[t] + b_i^l - z_i^l[t]\,\theta_i^l , \] and summing both sides over \(t=1,\ldots,T\) telescopes the \(u_i^l[t-1]\) terms and leaves \[ u_i^l[T]-u_i^l[0] = \sum_{t=1}^{T} q_i^l[t] + T b_i^l - \theta_i^l \sum_{t=1}^{T} z_i^l[t] = \sum_{t=1}^{T} q_i^l[t] + T b_i^l - \theta_i^l N_i^l(T), \] using the finite-horizon spike count \(N_i^l(T)\) fixed in Section 4.2. Because \(q_i^l[t]\) is linear in the presynaptic events, \(\sum_{t=1}^{T} q_i^l[t] = \sum_j w_{ij}^l N_j^{l-1}(T) = T\sum_j w_{ij}^l \rho_j^{l-1}(T)\), where \(\rho_j^{l-1}(T)=N_j^{l-1}(T)/T\) is the discrete firing fraction of Section 4.2. Substituting this and dividing by \(T\theta_i^l\) gives \[ \rho_i^l(T) = \frac{1}{\theta_i^l}\left(\sum_j w_{ij}^l \rho_j^{l-1}(T) + b_i^l\right) - \epsilon_i^l(T), \qquad \epsilon_i^l(T) := \frac{u_i^l[T]-u_i^l[0]}{T\,\theta_i^l}. \]

This identity is exact for every finite \(T\ge 1\). It is not an approximation, and nothing in its derivation has yet invoked a limit; \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) is defined so that the identity holds by construction, and the entire content of the correspondence is in how \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) behaves. Under the bounded-drive assumption of Section 8.1, standard arguments in the conversion literature bound the membrane excursion \(|u_i^l[T]-u_i^l[0]|\) by a constant independent of \(T\) whenever the neuron's asymptotic firing fraction sits strictly inside \((0,1)\), the regime away from the ReLU's own zero and saturation edges, which gives \(\epsilon_i^l(T) = O(1/T)\), the discretization error \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\) of the notation , . At the two edges the identity still holds exactly but for a different reason. A unit whose net drive stays negative for the whole horizon never crosses threshold, so \(\rho_i^l(T)=0\) at every \(T\), matching \(\mathrm{ReLU}\) at zero without the residual needing to shrink at all; a unit whose drive would require firing on every step saturates at \(\rho_i^l(T)=1\) for the same reason. Section 8.5 turns this per-neuron identity into a statement about the whole network as \(T\to\infty\), and Section 8 names the regime where the bound on \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) is not yet small at the \(T\) actually used as one of its finite-time error mechanisms.

8.3 Encoding, Readout, Parameter Mapping, and Architecture Preparation

The identity above already fixes what happens at every hidden layer once its input is a spike train with a well-defined firing fraction. The first and last layers are boundary cases, because the network's actual input is not itself the output of an IF neuron and its actual output is read before any further layer would consume it as spikes.

At the input, the classical pipeline uses direct or analog current injection rather than a Poisson process, treating each input value as a constant per-pixel current \(q_i^1[t]=\sum_j w_{ij}^1 x_j\) held fixed for the whole horizon, where \(x_j\) is the corresponding entry of the trained network's own input tensor. This is a stronger claim than merely being one option among the code family of Section 4.3, because it changes which error term dominates at the first layer. Constant injection makes \(q_i^1[t]\) exactly time-invariant, so the bound on \(\epsilon_i^1(T)\) from Section 8.2 applies with no additional variance term. A Poisson-rate encoding of the same input instead adds the code's own stochastic variance, which Section 4.3 gives as scaling with \(1/\sqrt{T}\) against the deterministic count's \(1/T\), a quadratically worse rate for the same horizon. Rueckauer et al. report this difference directly on a controlled ablation at matched \(T\), in which switching the first layer from Poisson-rate input to direct analog injection reduces the same converted network's classification error on CIFAR-10, with every other setting held fixed E5, a software-simulation result rather than a hardware measurement. This is why essentially all classical and later conversion work encodes its input this way, and why the derivation in Section 8.2 is written for a deterministic drive.

At the output, the classical pipeline does not require the final layer to spike at all. Setting the final layer's threshold high enough that it never fires, or equivalently never applying the threshold and reset stages to it, turns \(\mathcal{M}^L\) into a pure accumulator, and summing its recurrence over \(T\) steps the same way as in Section 8.2, but with no reset term to track, gives \(u_i^L[T] - u_i^L[0] = \sum_{t=1}^{T} q_i^L[t] + T b_i^L\), with the initial potential retained exactly as Section 8.2 retains it. This section adopts the convention that the readout accumulator starts from a zero initial membrane potential, \(u_i^L[0]=0\). Under that convention, dividing by \(T\) gives \(u_i^L[T]/T = \sum_j w_{ij}^L \rho_j^{L-1}(T) + b_i^L\) exactly, with no residual \(\epsilon\) term at all; a nonzero initial value would instead carry the extra term \(u_i^L[0]/T\) into the right-hand side, a term that vanishes as \(T\) grows but is not itself zero at any finite \(T\). This is the class-accumulator decoder named among the options in Section 4.2, and it is why the final layer's decoded score is not subject to the same finite-count error that a spiking hidden layer carries. The deployment consequence is that only the hidden layers between input and output pay the discretization cost derived in Section 8.2; the choice to decode logits from an unreset accumulator rather than from a spike count is a design choice available because the final layer's output never needs to be re-encoded as an event train for a further layer to consume.

Section 8.2 leaves the weights, thresholds, and biases of the mapped network unspecified beyond linearity; it holds for any values, which means it does not by itself tell a practitioner what to enter into \(w_{ij}^l\), \(\theta_i^l\), or \(b_i^l\). Parameter mapping is the separate question of which values make the mapped network's decoded activations reproduce the trained network's own pre-activations rather than an arbitrary rescaling of them.

Fix a per-layer calibration constant \(\lambda^l\), the scale symbol of the notation, chosen from the trained network's own activation statistics on a calibration set, either the observed maximum or a more outlier-robust percentile of it ; Sengupta et al.'s SPIKE-NORM instead estimates the same constant online, from spikes produced by a short simulation of the partially mapped network rather than from the source network's own activation statistics directly. Define the decoded value at layer \(l\) as \(\hat a_i^l(T):=\lambda^l \rho_i^l(T)\), read directly from a hidden layer's firing fraction, or, at the final layer, from the unreset accumulator of Section 8.3. Setting the mapped network's threshold to the calibration constant, \(\theta_i^l=\lambda^l\), and its weight to the trained weight rescaled by the previous layer's constant, \(w_{ij}^l=\lambda^{l-1}\tilde w_{ij}^l\), with the bias carried over unchanged, \(b_i^l=\tilde b_i^l\), turns the identity of Section 8.2 into \[ \hat a_i^l(T) = \sum_j \tilde w_{ij}^l\, \hat a_j^{l-1}(T) + \tilde b_i^l - \lambda^l \epsilon_i^l(T), \] which is the trained network's own affine pre-activation, evaluated at the previous layer's decoded value rather than its true activation, minus the same bounded residual carried forward from Section 8.2. Different papers place the ratio \(\lambda^{l-1}/\lambda^l\) on the weight, on the threshold, or on an explicit rescaling of the activation itself; these are algebraically equivalent packagings of the same normalization, and a reader comparing two papers' stated weights or thresholds directly, without checking which packaging each uses, will compare numbers that are not on the same footing.

Because the firing fraction \(\rho_i^l(T)\) is bounded in \([0,1]\) by construction, a spike count cannot fall below zero or exceed the horizon, the decoded value \(\hat a_i^l(T)=\lambda^l\rho_i^l(T)\) is confined to \([0,\lambda^l]\) regardless of how large or negative the right-hand side of the equation above becomes. This is the origin of the clipping this literature repeatedly reports, and it is a structural consequence of representing a value as a bounded count, not an artifact of a particular calibration choice. A calibration constant set too small clips activations the source network actually produces; one set too large wastes the achievable \(1/T\) resolution over a range the activations never use. Choosing \(\lambda^l\) well is exactly the estimation problem the parameter-mapping methods above solve, and refining that estimate under a fixed, small \(T\) is the subject of the low-latency interventions Section 8 covers; this section fixes only the mapping's structure, not its optimization.

Three components of a trained convolutional network do not fit the staged update of Section 3.2 as trained, and each must be rewritten before parameter mapping applies. Table 14 collects the three together with the mechanism each preparation step preserves.

Batch normalization in evaluation mode is a fixed per-channel affine map built from running statistics, \(\hat y = \gamma(y-\mu)/\sqrt{\sigma^2+\delta}+\beta\), which composes algebraically with the affine map of the convolution or linear layer immediately before it, \[ w' = w\cdot\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\delta}}, \qquad b' = b\cdot\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\delta}} + \beta - \frac{\gamma\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\delta}}, \] folding the two layers into one affine layer with weight \(w'\) and bias \(b'\) ahead of the ReLU. A batch-normalization layer computed from batch statistics at inference time is not this fixed map and does not fold this way, which is why the assumption in Section 8.1 restricts to the evaluation-mode case.

Average pooling is a fixed linear operator on its input window, so it commutes exactly with the substitution in Section 8.2's derivation; forwarding an averaged spike count rather than a single binary event changes the payload carried on that connection from binary to graded, in the sense of Section 3.3, but introduces no new error term. Max pooling is not linear in its inputs, and no exact analog of the firing-fraction identity holds for it; an instantaneous "any input fired" gating rule approximates the operation but can overestimate the pooled unit's rate relative to the single highest-rate input channel, which is why Rueckauer et al. document this approximation explicitly and why the great majority of classical and low-latency conversion pipelines replace max pooling with average pooling before mapping rather than attempting the approximation.

A residual or skip connection sums two branches that were calibrated independently, an identity path and a residual branch, each with its own \(\lambda^l\). Summing their raw spike trains at the merge point implicitly assumes both branches are expressed on the same scale; when they are not, the merged drive entering the shared post-add threshold no longer corresponds to the sum the trained network actually computed. Sengupta et al.'s extension of the pipeline to VGG and residual architectures handles this by rescaling both branches to a shared calibration constant before the merge, which is the architectural preparation this survey groups under normalization for residual networks specifically.

8.4 Walkthrough

Assembling Section 8.3 in execution order rather than exposition order gives the pipeline named in the opening paragraph, normalization, parameter mapping, encoding, temporal execution, readout, applied here to one trained network end to end. Normalization first rewrites the trained graph, folding every batch-normalization layer into its preceding affine layer, replacing any max-pooling layer with average pooling, and rescaling every residual branch to a shared calibration constant, leaving a graph of affine layers, ReLU nonlinearities, and linear pooling. Parameter mapping then estimates a calibration constant \(\lambda^l\) for every layer from a held-out calibration batch and sets each layer's integrate-and-fire threshold and rescaled weight from Section 8.3's identity, carrying every bias over unchanged. Encoding injects the network's actual input tensor as a constant per-pixel current into the first layer for the entire horizon, as fixed in Section 8.3, rather than sampling any stochastic process. Temporal execution then runs the staged update of Section 3.2 for \(T\) discrete steps at every hidden layer, with reset-by-subtraction at every threshold crossing, producing a firing fraction \(\rho_i^l(T)\) at every hidden unit that Section 8.2 already characterizes. Readout accumulates the final layer's membrane potential without ever resetting it and divides by \(T\), giving the class scores from which the argmax decision is taken.

\(T\) here is the algorithmic horizon fixed in Section 4.1, a count of discrete update steps, not a wall-clock duration and not a proxy for it. A smaller \(T\) shortens the sum each unit accumulates and enlarges the bound on \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) from Section 8.2; it does not by itself establish a shorter inference latency on any particular target, because what one step of temporal execution costs in wall-clock time depends on the execution model that Sections 4.1 and 5 treat separately from the step count itself.

8.5 Asymptotic Equivalence and Where the Derivation Does Not Apply

Composing the identity of Section 8.3 across the \(L_{\mathrm{net}}\) layers of the mapped network gives, for the output layer, a sum of the finite-\(T\) residuals \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) accumulated at every hidden layer the output depends on, each weighted by the path of trained weights connecting that layer to the output. Two statements follow from this composition, and this survey states them separately rather than as one claim, because only one of them is exact at every finite \(T\) actually used.

The asymptotic statement is a limit. As \(T\to\infty\), with the calibration constants, weights, and thresholds of Section 8.3 held fixed, every per-layer residual \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) in the interior regime of Section 8.2 shrinks toward zero, and the network's decoded output converges to the trained network's own forward pass evaluated through a clipped ReLU, \(\mathrm{clip}(\cdot,0,\lambda^l)\), at every layer. This is the content of Rueckauer et al.'s convergence result for the reset-by-subtraction rule, and Ding et al. state and prove a version of the same layer-by-layer firing-rate correspondence, matching a magnitude between a source network and a converted network, for general feedforward and residual architectures. Both results are asymptotic; neither author claims the limit is reached at any finite \(T\), and this survey makes the same restriction explicit rather than leaving it implicit.

The finite-time statement is what Section 8.2's identity gives directly, and it does not vanish at the horizons any deployed system actually runs. Each hidden layer carries a residual bounded on the order of \(1/T\) in the interior regime, exact at the two clipped edges, and these residuals do not stay confined to the layer where they arise. A hidden layer's own residual enters the next layer's synaptic drive as part of \(\rho_j^{l-1}(T)\), so a shallow network's error at some finite \(T\) is not simply the single largest per-layer term but a composition of per-layer terms along every path from input to output, weighted by the trained weights on that path. This composition has no universal closed form or growth law derivable from Section 8.2 alone, an instance of the prohibition, in the notation's terms, against assuming additivity or independence of named error mechanisms without a specific cited derivation. Section 8 is where this survey names and separately bounds the individual mechanisms, clipping, discretization, and the mismatch between a reset-to-zero and reset-by-subtraction trajectory, that this compounded finite-\(T\) residual decomposes into, rather than treating "the conversion error" as one undifferentiated quantity.

Neither statement licenses substituting one algorithmic quantity for another. Convergence as \(T\to\infty\) is convergence in the horizon defined in Section 4.1, not in wall-clock time; a claim that a smaller \(T\) achieves lower latency requires the separate, target-specific argument that Section 5 supplies for a named execution model, not an appeal to this section's asymptotic result.

Each architectural assumption fixed in Section 8.1 has a corresponding failure case, and naming them here closes the loop the assumptions opened rather than leaving them as unenforced fine print.

A source network that uses a negative-valued or non-monotonic activation, rather than a ReLU, has no non-negative target for \(\rho_i^l(T)\in[0,1]\) to converge toward, since the derivation in Section 8.2 relies on the decoded value \(\hat a_i^l(T)=\lambda^l\rho_i^l(T)\) being confined to \([0,\lambda^l]\) by construction. Representing a signed target requires a signed or dual-channel emission scheme distinct from the single non-negative event this section assumes, an intervention Section 8 covers rather than a case this derivation extends to on its own.

A source network with recurrent connections, attention, or normalization layers computed from live batch or sequence statistics breaks the feedforward, fixed-weight linearity that Section 8.2's substitution \(\sum_{t=1}^T q_i^l[t]=T\sum_j w_{ij}^l \rho_j^{l-1}(T)\) depends on; if \(w_{ij}^l\) or the set of contributing units varies with \(t\) or with the input itself, no analogous closed-form telescoping identity has been established by the sources this section relies on.

A per-step synaptic drive that is not bounded independent of \(T\), violating the fifth assumption of Section 8.1, leaves the membrane excursion \(|u_i^l[T]-u_i^l[0]|\) unbounded in \(T\) as well, so the \(O(1/T)\) rate for \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) no longer follows; a sustained, strongly negative drive without a floor on the membrane potential is the clearest instance, and it is a modeling and implementation detail this section flags rather than resolves.

Rank order coding, defined among the code families of Section 4.3, lies outside this correspondence for a reason traceable directly to Section 8.2's own construction rather than to a general property of non-rate codes. The derivation needs a magnitude per neuron, a spike count \(N_i^l(T)\) that can be normalized into a firing fraction; a rank-order channel carries no such magnitude, because each neuron fires at most once per wave and the encoded content is that neuron's rank among the population, not any count attached to it in isolation . There is accordingly no \(\rho_i^l(T)\) to define for a single rank-order unit, and every conversion proof this survey locates, including Rueckauer et al.'s and Ding et al.'s , is stated over a matched magnitude rather than a matched ordering, consistent with Section 4.3's statement that this is why the code sits outside the rate-matching correspondence.

These are the assumptions Section 8 traces as the entry points of finite-time conversion error, clipping from a mis-calibrated \(\lambda^l\), discretization from the bound on \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\), and the reset-rule mismatches Section 3.5 already anticipates, and they are the targets Section 8 organizes its interventions around, most of which relax exactly one of the assumptions fixed here while leaving the rest of this section's pipeline unchanged.

8.6 A General Finite-Time Error Model

Section 8 derives the correspondence between a ReLU-activated ANN and a converted SNN under an asymptotic assumption, an inference horizon \(T\to\infty\) at which the discrete firing fraction \(\rho_i^l(T)\) defined in Section 4 exactly reproduces the corresponding activation. Every claim in this article that describes a converted network running with a finite, deliberately small \(T\) is a claim about what that asymptotic derivation leaves out. This section names each thing it leaves out, states where in the pipeline it enters, whether it is a fixed structural consequence of the architecture or a random consequence of the input encoding, and how it interacts with the reset semantics fixed in Section 3. Section 8 organizes its intervention methods by which of these named mechanisms they target; this section exists so that organization has fixed referents.

Write the finite-window deviation of a converted neuron's discrete firing fraction from its asymptotic target as \[ \delta_i^l(T) \;=\; \rho_i^l(T) \;-\; \lim_{T'\to\infty}\rho_i^l(T'), \] evaluated at the layer index \(l\), unit index \(i\), and closed window \([1,T]\) fixed by Section 4's definition of \(N_i^l(T)\) and \(\rho_i^l(T)\). This survey organizes \(\delta_i^l(T)\) into four named contributions using the symbols \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\), \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\), \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{reset}}\), and \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{time}}\) reserved for this purpose, \[ \delta_i^l(T) \;\approx\; \epsilon_{\mathrm{clip},i}^l \;+\; \epsilon_{\mathrm{disc},i}^l(T) \;+\; \epsilon_{\mathrm{reset},i}^l[T] \;+\; \epsilon_{\mathrm{time},i}^l[T]. \] The approximate sign is deliberate. This is an organizational device for exposition, not a derived identity. The literature proves an additive bound for a narrower claim than the one written above, decomposing the error of a specific converted activation function into a clipping term and a discretization term under a stated reset rule and a stated finite window ; that proof does not cover the timing term introduced in Section 8.7, and no source reviewed for this survey proves that all four terms are independent, additive, or governed by one growth law across architectures and reset rules. Treat the equation as a labeling scheme for the rest of this section, not as a formula to evaluate.

Where does \(\delta_i^l(T)\) come from mechanically. The classical pipeline traced in Section 8 runs five named stages in order, normalization, parameter mapping, encoding, temporal execution, and readout. Each of the four error terms above enters at one of these stages, and a layer's total deviation is whatever the temporal execution stage has accumulated by the time it reaches that layer's own contribution. Section 8.7 locates \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\) and \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\) at parameter mapping and at the counting operation itself. Section 8.7 locates \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{reset}}\) at the reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\) fixed in Section 3.2. Section 8.7 separates a dispersion component of \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{reset}}\), called unevenness error, from \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{time}}\), a structurally distinct term tied to propagation across depth rather than to reset. Section 8.8 and Section 8.7 then trace how normalization, pooling, residual paths, depth, encoding, and readout change how much of each term a given layer inherits.

Where in this pipeline finite-time error visibly enters, and how it can grow, shrink, or interact as a signal passes through the stages a real converted network uses, batch normalization folding, average or max pooling, a residual summation, and a non-spiking readout, is a structural question rather than a numeric one; no single measured trace generalizes across architectures, reset rules, and horizons. 8.7 The Error Sources

Clipping error, \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\), is a mechanism of the parameter mapping stage, not of the neuron dynamics that follow it. Before conversion, that stage fixes a firing threshold \(\theta_i^l\), or equivalently an activation scale \(\lambda^l\) in the sense Section 8 assigns that symbol, so that the neuron's finite range of achievable spike counts stands in for the ANN activation's range. Any input drive that would have produced an ANN activation above the value that scale represents is truncated at the maximum achievable count, exactly as a hard upper clip truncates a real-valued activation. The semantic effect is a value-dependent, one-sided bias, present only for inputs at or above the chosen scale, that does not shrink as the simulation horizon \(T\) grows, because it is a property of the chosen scale rather than of the counting resolution. The trade-off this creates is between two threshold-setting failure modes on opposite sides, a scale set too low clips frequently and biases every affected unit downward, while a scale set too high wastes achievable count levels on activation values the network rarely produces, which is itself a discretization cost. Early parameter mapping methods, the weight and threshold balancing procedures of the first conversion papers, exist specifically to choose this scale from a calibration pass over training data, an unresolved tension the field returns to repeatedly rather than a solved constant . This is a mechanism claim about where and why the bias appears, not a measured accuracy result, and it is unconditional on the reset rule in Section 3.5, since a spike count cannot exceed the finite window \(T\) regardless of which reset stage is chosen.

Discretization error, \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\), is a mechanism of the counting operation itself, present even for an input drive that a correctly scaled threshold would not clip. Section 4 establishes that a fixed threshold restricts the achievable values of \(N_i^l(T)\) to the integers from zero to \(T\), so the achievable firing fractions \(\rho_i^l(T)\) form a uniform grid spaced at intervals of \(1/T\) . Whatever real-valued activation the ANN side would produce, the SNN side can only represent the nearest point on that grid, a flooring operation whose worst-case magnitude is bounded by the grid spacing itself and therefore shrinks as \(T\) grows. The semantic effect is a bounded, deterministic rounding error rather than the unbounded, value-dependent bias clipping produces. The trade-off is direct, halving the grid spacing means doubling \(T\), so discretization error is the term that most directly ties accuracy to simulation length, and it is the term every low-latency conversion method in Section 8's activation-shaping family is built to compress without simply enlarging \(T\) . The deployment consequence is that clipping and discretization are separable mechanisms that a single calibration step cannot both fix at once, choosing a scale narrow enough to keep the achievable grid fine costs clipped range, and choosing a scale wide enough to avoid clipping costs grid resolution at the same fixed \(T\); a formal treatment of this trade-off as a joint optimization over both quantities exists and is itself an inference claim about the shape of that trade-off, not a hardware measurement .

Reset error, \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{reset}}\), is a mechanism of the reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\) that Section 3.5 already makes load-bearing for the asymptotic derivation. Recall the reset term \(r_i^l[t]=\tilde{u}_i^l[t]-\theta_i^l[t]\), the residual charge left over the instant a neuron crosses threshold. Section 3.5 shows that reset-to-zero discards \(r_i^l[t]\) outright and reset-by-subtraction retains it for the membrane stage to integrate again at the next step, and that only the second rule removes a persistent, input-dependent bias as \(T\to\infty\) . Reset error is what remains at any finite \(T\) even under reset-by-subtraction, because the window closes before every unit of residual charge has necessarily been converted into an output spike. At the final step \(t=T\), whatever membrane potential \(u_i^l[T]\) sits below threshold is charge the neuron accepted but never spent, and it is discarded by the readout the moment the window ends regardless of which reset rule governed the steps inside that window. The semantic effect is a mean-level bias in \(N_i^l(T)\) that depends on the neuron's own charge history over the closed window, not a random fluctuation that a resimulation would erase.

Naming this quantity residual membrane potential rather than folding it silently into discretization error matters because its size is not fixed by the grid spacing alone. A neuron's leftover charge at cutoff depends on where in its own integration cycle the window happened to end, which depends in turn on every upstream layer's own finite-time behavior, so the bias compounds with depth rather than staying at one layer's fixed grid resolution. This is exactly the failure mode a line of work formalizes for deep and residual architectures under uniform, hard reset, terming it a residual membrane potential problem and reporting worse degradation as network depth increases . A closely related line explicitly targets a converted network's initial membrane condition, \(u_i^l[0]\), as the free parameter that determines how much residual charge a finite window can accumulate before it starts producing this bias, and a separate line targets the same bias by explicitly modeling and subtracting the expected residual term at conversion time . A third diagnostic line observes that the resulting output spikes are not merely fewer or more numerous than the target but offset from the value the discretization grid alone would predict, naming this an offset-spike phenomenon rather than treating it as ordinary discretization noise . All four are mechanism and inference claims about where this bias originates and how it scales with depth, not hardware measurements; none of the source papers audited for this survey report execution on physical neuromorphic silicon, a gap Section 13 returns to for the low-latency conversion literature generally. The deployment consequence is that reset error cannot be fixed by lengthening \(T\) alone in a deep network, because added depth adds new residual-charge accumulation points faster than a longer window drains any single one of them.

Unevenness error and timing error are not the same mechanism, and this survey does not use them interchangeably. Unevenness error is a dispersion of residual charge across units that share a layer and a target rate. Timing error is a structural propagation delay tied to network depth. A network can suffer from one without the other.

Unevenness error is the part of \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{reset}}\) that a single-neuron, mean-value account of residual charge misses. Two units with the same target firing fraction \(\rho_i^l(T)\) over a shared window need not carry the same instantaneous input history inside that window, because the individual arrival times of their presynaptic events, set by whatever unevenness of upstream firing already exists, differ even when the two windows' totals agree. Since the reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\) and the membrane stage \(\mathcal{M}^l\) are stateful rather than simple accumulators, two input trains with an identical mean rate but a different within-window arrival pattern leave different amounts of residual charge \(r_i^l[t]\) at the window's end. This is the mechanism the Quantization Clip-Floor-Shift (QCFS) line of analysis isolates as a third, separate contribution to finite-time error beyond clipping and discretization, one that a value-independent scale correction and a value-independent count-resolution improvement do not reach . The semantic effect is that two neurons converted from the same ANN activation value can carry different amounts of finite-time error depending on where in the network they sit and what firing pattern their inputs happen to produce, so unevenness error is heterogeneous across a layer rather than a single per-layer constant. The deployment consequence is that a per-layer average error estimate, the kind a calibration pass over a validation set naturally produces, can understate the worst-affected units in that same layer.

Timing error, \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{time}}\), is a different mechanism located at the temporal execution stage rather than at the reset stage. Under the layer-synchronous update order Section 3.2 fixes, a unit at layer \(l\) cannot register an event fired at layer \(l-1\), step \(t\), before step \(t+d_{ij}^l\), where \(d_{ij}^l\) is the synaptic delay Section 3.4 reserves that symbol for, at minimum one step when no explicit delay is modeled. A signal entering the network at step 1 therefore cannot influence a unit at depth \(l\) before step \(l\), so the deepest layers of a network with many layers spend part of any short window simply waiting for the first wave of events to arrive, a fixed structural cost that a wider threshold scale or a finer discretization grid cannot remove. This cost is why depth interacts with a small \(T\) even when clipping, discretization, and reset error are all individually small, a constraint the literature on very deep converted networks documents directly by needing larger simulation windows and dedicated reset treatments specifically as convolutional depth increases . Where timing error is deliberately made unequal across layers, by assigning a different simulation length to each layer rather than treating it as an unavoidable propagation cost, the resulting per-layer realignment is a Section 8 intervention rather than a Section 8 mechanism, and this section stops at naming the underlying cost without describing that remedy.

Sections 8.7 and 8.8 treat every mechanism as deterministic given a fixed input trajectory, which is the correct treatment for the direct or analog encoding convention, injecting a real-valued input as a constant current rather than as a Poisson process, that Section 8's own walkthrough and the great majority of the low-latency conversion literature adopt at the encoding stage . Under that convention, rerunning the same input through the same converted network with the same finite \(T\) reproduces the same \(\delta_i^l(T)\) exactly, because nothing in the encoder \(\mathcal{E}\) is random. Every bound and every mechanism named above is a deterministic-encoding claim in this sense, a fixed function of the input and the finite window, not a distribution over repeated runs.

A Poisson-rate encoder changes this at the source rather than downstream. Section 4.3 already establishes that deterministic count coding's quantization error scales as \(1/T\) while stochastic Poisson coding's variance instead scales as \(1/\sqrt{T}\) . The mechanism reason is that a Poisson encoder makes the input event train \(z_i^{0}[t]\) itself a random variable with the correct value only in expectation, so every downstream quantity built from it, \(N_i^l(T)\), \(\rho_i^l(T)\), and every error term in Table 16, becomes a random variable rather than a deterministic function of the input, and its spread does not close as fast as a deterministic encoder's bias does for the same increase in \(T\). The semantic effect is that a stochastic-encoding result reported at a given \(T\) describes a distribution over repeated draws, and a single reported accuracy number at that \(T\) is one sample from that distribution rather than a bound. The trade-off is why deterministic direct encoding, not Poisson encoding, is the near-universal convention in the low-latency conversion literature specifically, quadratically more steps are needed under Poisson encoding to match a deterministic encoder's precision at the same \(T\) , and the deployment consequence is that a stochastic-encoding and a deterministic-encoding result at the same nominal \(T\) are not directly comparable claims about accuracy at that horizon.

The decoder side carries a parallel, non-equivalent distinction. Choosing which of \(\mathcal{D}\)'s readout options, count, rate, first-spike time, membrane state, or a class accumulator over several output units, to use does not by itself introduce or remove stochastic variance; that property is fixed at the encoder. What the decoder choice changes is which of the deterministic terms in Table 16 the readout is exposed to, a count or rate readout adds one more instance of \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\), as Section 8.8 already states, while a membrane-state readout instead exposes whatever residual and unevenness error sits in that final layer's pre-threshold potential without the count-related bounding property Table 16 gives \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\). Conflating a decoder's exposure to deterministic finite-window error with the encoder's stochastic variance is exactly the confusion this subsection exists to prevent, the two are governed by different mechanisms, enter the pipeline at opposite ends, and do not trade off against each other by adjusting \(T\) alone.

8.8 Accumulation and the Design Problem

The four mechanisms above are defined for a single unit and a single layer. What a specific architecture experiences is how many times, and in what combination, each unit's output is passed through another unit that adds its own contribution. Section 8's normalization stage assumes a specific set of transformations, batch normalization folded into the preceding weight and bias, average pooling, and an explicit prescription for residual summation, precisely because these are the transformations the finite-time account below needs to hold as claimed.

Batch normalization folding is exact in the asymptotic derivation, the folded scale and bias reproduce the trained network's normalized activation as \(T\to\infty\). At finite \(T\) this exactness does not carry over unchanged. The folded bias becomes an added constant current that the membrane stage integrates over the same closed window as every other input, so it is clipped by the same threshold, discretized by the same grid, and left with the same residual charge at cutoff as any other contribution to \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]\). Folding a normalization layer into the weight and bias removes a separate computational stage, it does not remove that stage's finite-time error, and treating a folded network as automatically equivalent to its unfolded ANN counterpart at low \(T\) is an inference this survey does not make without a cited derivation covering the folded case specifically.

Pooling divides along exactly the line Section 4's linearity properties predict. Average pooling is a linear combination of its inputs, so it commutes with the spike-count readout the same way it commutes with a real-valued sum, and a pooled unit's finite-time error is a direct, unweighted composition of its inputs' own \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\), \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\), and \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{reset}}\) terms rather than a new error source. Max pooling is not linear in spike counts, the maximum of several finite, integer-valued counts over a shared window is not obtainable from a weighted sum of those counts by any fixed set of synaptic weights, so a converted network built with max pooling needs a separate approximation of the pooling operation itself, and that approximation is a fifth, architecture-specific error source outside the four named above, one this survey does not attempt to characterize in general because its magnitude is a property of the specific approximation chosen rather than of the pooling operation itself . The deployment consequence is that Section 8's architecture-preparation assumption of average pooling is not a stylistic simplification, a network trained with max pooling either needs retraining with average pooling before the rest of this section's error account applies to it, or it carries an additional, separately characterized error term that this taxonomy does not cover.

Residual summation compounds the timing term specifically. A residual block sums an identity shortcut path against a transformed path that has passed through additional convolutional, normalization, and activation stages, so the two operands entering that summation have unequal depth measured from the network's input, and by the timing mechanism in Section 8.7, unequal depth means unequal minimum propagation delay before either operand's events can meaningfully reflect the input driving them. The two operands also carry unequal accumulated clipping, discretization, and residual error, since one has passed through strictly fewer stages than the other. Two documented lines of work address exactly this combination, one by tracking how conversion accuracy degrades specifically as convolutional depth and residual connections are added , and one by introducing a reset treatment aimed at the resulting residual-charge accumulation in deep and residual architectures specifically . Depth is therefore not merely a multiplier on a single-layer error rate, each additional layer contributes its own instance of all four mechanisms and its own minimum propagation delay, and a residual architecture adds the further complication of summing two branches whose accumulated error and delay are not equal to begin with.

The readout stage inherits whatever the deepest layer before it has accumulated rather than adding a fixed increment of its own. A spike-count or firing-fraction readout applies one more instance of \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\) on top of everything upstream. Reading the pre-threshold membrane potential of a non-spiking final layer directly, a decoder choice Section 4.2 already names among \(\mathcal{D}\)'s options, removes that one final discretization stage, since the readout no longer counts events at all, but it does not remove or correct any clipping, discretization, reset, unevenness, or timing error already embedded in the real-valued input that final layer receives from the layer below it . The deployment consequence is that a non-spiking readout is a bound on where error can still be introduced, not a correction of error already present.

No single mechanism in this section is the barrier to low-latency conversion. Clipping, discretization, residual membrane potential, unevenness, and timing are five structurally distinct terms, entering at different pipeline stages, with different growth behavior in \(T\), compounded further by normalization, pooling, residual paths, and depth. A method that only targets one of these terms leaves the others in place.

This section has deliberately stopped short of describing how any of these mechanisms is corrected. That is Section 8's question, and Section 8 organizes its answer by which of the mechanisms named here a given intervention targets, rather than by chronology or by author. The design problem Section 8 answers is not simply how to reduce \(T\). A method that reduces the discretization grid's spacing at a given \(T\) without addressing residual membrane potential or timing error leaves those terms exactly as large as before, and a smaller \(T\) obtained this way is a claim about the number of algorithmic steps a network requires, not, on its own, a claim about wall-clock inference latency \(\tau_{\mathrm{inf}}\), a distinction Section 4.1 already makes and this survey does not relax for the interventions that follow. The problem Section 8 must solve is therefore which mechanism, or combination of mechanisms, a given intervention can address without silently reintroducing another, at a stated finite \(T\), under a stated reset rule, for a stated architecture.

8.9 Activation Shaping and Potential Calibration

Section 8 traces five error mechanisms to the pipeline stage where each one enters, clipping and discretization error, residual and reset error, unevenness and timing error, architecture-dependent error, and encoding and readout error. Each mechanism marks a point where a finite, discrete simulation departs from an assumption Section 8's classical derivation holds fixed. This section organizes the methods that intervene against those errors by the point in the pipeline where each intervention acts, not by publication venue or year. Activation shaping intervenes before conversion, on the ANN's own training objective. Potential and reset calibration and neuron interventions intervene on the neuron itself, changing what Section 3 holds fixed as the reset rule or the neuron-model family for the specific case of a classically converted network. Temporal allocation and adaptive inference intervene on the schedule, replacing Section 8's single global horizon with one that varies by layer or by input. Alternative-code conversion and hardware-aware conversion intervene on the correspondence itself, replacing rate matching with a mapping onto a different code or a specific target platform. Hybrid fine-tuning and emerging architectures intervene after conversion or across the boundary between the two learning branches introduced in Section 6. Evidence class and hardware status are stated per method below, never assumed from the group a method belongs to.

A rate-coded IF neuron under reset-by-subtraction accumulates a spike count that is, by construction, a uniform \(T\)-level quantizer, as Section 4.3 establishes. With a fixed threshold, the achievable output levels over a \(T\)-step window are the integers from zero to \(T\), spaced at intervals of \(1/T\). Classical conversion, described in Section 8, trains the ANN independent of this grid and imposes it only afterward, which is the mechanism Section 8's clipping-and-discretization error attributes error to. Activation shaping intervenes earlier, training the ANN's own activation function to anticipate the grid before conversion happens. This is an ordinary quantization-aware training step applied to the ANN, not a hardware intervention, and it should be read as such rather than as a claim about the SNN's own execution. Bu, Fang, Ding, Dai, Yu, and Huang's QCFS activation replaces the ANN's ReLU with a clip-floor-shift nonlinearity trained to anticipate the target IF neuron's step response, reporting 93.96 percent CIFAR-10 accuracy at \(T=4\) and further results from \(T=8\) up to 512 and beyond, all in GPU simulation. Read this way, the clip-floor-shift function is an activation fake-quantizer of the kind used throughout conventional quantization-aware training. This survey's claim index does not carry a verified claim establishing that the two constructions are bit-identical, so that correspondence is stated here as an interpretive reading of the published mechanism, not as a verified finding.

Three later papers keep the same intervention point and vary what the trained nonlinearity looks like. Yang, Yang, Zhang, Dou, Shen, and Zhao's CS-QCFS gives each channel its own per-channel, softplus-shaped, guaranteed-positive threshold, reporting a \(T=1\) headline accuracy on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100. Li, Ma, and Furber's QFFS makes the quantization framing explicit rather than implicit, training with a learned step size quantization (LSQ)-style quantization-aware quantizer before conversion and stating an explicit analog-coded input convention, reaching \(T=4\) on CIFAR-10 and \(T=8\) or 10 on ImageNet. Jiang, Anumasa, De Masi, Xiong, and Gu's SlipReLU generalizes the activation to a weighted-ReLU-plus-step form with a shift term and reports a claimed one-step, \(T=1\), conversion, with further results at \(T=2\) and \(T=4\). All three, like QCFS itself, report GPU simulation accuracy only; none names a target chip inside an experiments section, and all four papers in this paragraph and the one above are E5.

Datta, Liu, Diffenderfer, Kailkhura, and Beerel's When SNN Meets ANN keeps the same intervention point, a modified IF neuron that postpones firing and reset until after input-current accumulation together with a batch-normalization bias adjustment, reporting a \(T=4\) headline on ImageNet. Its own appendix, titled Deployment of Proposed SNN on Loihi, is the clearest instance in this literature of a documented-looking route that stops short of an exercised one. The appendix reports a PyTorch-versus-Lava-DL accuracy comparison, which is E5 evidence for the proposed method, and separately asserts a Lava-DL-to-Loihi path without naming a Loihi generation, a physical board, or a device measurement, which keeps that route at E3 neuromorphic rather than a deployment claim . Section 8.12 returns to this paper as the negative case against which genuinely hardware-aware conversions are compared.

None of the four activation-shaping papers above reports its own hardware execution. A later, unrelated paper does place one of them on physical silicon. Adaptive Fission's hardware table includes an unmodified, no-Fission QCFS configuration measured on a physical Lynxi HP201 accelerator, reporting per-epoch time and system energy from board sensors, an E1 neuromorphic result for a baseline the QCFS paper itself never deployed . Section 8.9 records the parallel case for SRP.

A second family keeps the classical IF neuron and the rate code intact and instead changes what happens to membrane potential at the start of inference and at reset, intervening directly against the residual-and-reset error Section 8 attributes to reset-by-subtraction's carried-over charge and to an implicit zero initial condition. Bu, Ding, Yu, and Huang's Optimized Potential Initialization (OPI) sets a non-zero initial membrane potential, one half of threshold, for IF neurons under reset-by-subtraction, targeting a horizon of 32 steps or fewer with further results reported from 8 up to 512 and beyond. Hao, Bu, Ding, Huang, and Yu's Residual Membrane Potential method (SRP) is built directly on the QCFS activation and adds a second, calibration-only pass that reads out the residual membrane potential left after ordinary inference, reducing the reported headline to as few as 4 steps, with 8, 16, and 32-step alternatives. Both papers report GPU simulation accuracy only and are E5.

As with QCFS in Section 8.9, a later hardware paper places SRP's own unmodified baseline configuration on physical silicon. Adaptive Fission's hardware table includes a no-Fission SRP row measured for per-epoch time and system energy on a physical Lynxi HP201 accelerator, an E1 neuromorphic result the SRP paper itself did not report, using board sensors that closed interfaces prevent from separating into core-only figures . Read together, the QCFS and SRP rows in that table establish that rate-coded QCFS-lineage networks have reached silicon, while also confirming that neither proposing paper reported that deployment itself, a distinction this survey preserves rather than collapsing into a single verdict about the lineage.

8.10 Neuron Interventions and Temporal Allocation

A third family leaves the ANN's activation function and the reset rule largely alone and instead redesigns the IF neuron itself, changing the neuron-model assumption Section 3 holds fixed for the specific case of a classically converted network and intervening against Section 8's clipping-and-discretization error by changing what a single step's spike budget can represent. Li and Zeng's burst-spike IF neuron lifts the one-spike-per-step ceiling ordinary rate coding assumes, letting a neuron emit several unit spikes within one step when its residual post-threshold potential remains large, paired with a pooling layer the paper calls LIPooling, and reaches sub-100-step headlines with ablations extending to 32 steps and beyond; the burst-coding row in Section 4.3's Table 9 already fixes the code this neuron implements. Wang, Zhang, Chen, and Qu's Signed Neuron with Memory (SNM) permits a compensating negative spike alongside neuron-wise normalization, targeting headlines at or below 128 steps with tables extending to 1024. Hu, Zheng, Jiang, and Pan's Fast-SNN converts a quantized ANN into a signed IF neuron that resets by adding the threshold on a negative spike, fine-tuned layer by layer, at \(T\) of 3, 7, or 15 across classification, detection, and segmentation workloads. Liu, Zhao, Chen, Wang, and Jiang's SpikeConverter introduces an inverse-leaky IF neuron with a temporal-separation scheme, reducing the reported headline to 16 steps. All four report GPU simulation accuracy only and are E5. Fast-SNN cites Loihi's documented support for signed spikes as design justification for its own signed-IF neuron; the paper reports no execution on that or any other chip, and the citation should be read as motivation for a design choice, not as a deployment claim .

Section 4.1 already fixes the vocabulary a mixed-timestep paper needs to be read correctly. A static per-layer horizon \(T_l\) is a different control variable from wall-clock latency, and the average or \(T_{\mathrm{eff}}\) each paper reports is a derived layer statistic, not a measured duration. What that discussion leaves for this section is each method's target and its hardware evidence. PASCAL, QAC, and MT-SNN intervene against Section 8's unevenness-and-timing error by discarding Section 8's assumption of one global \(T\) shared by every layer, assigning instead a layer-specific quantization level and therefore a layer-specific unrolled timestep count, reconciled between adjacent layers by an explicit temporal-alignment step . The accuracy results for all three are E5, software simulation reported against an averaged \(T_{\mathrm{eff}}\) rather than a measured duration. A separate reproducibility finding narrows how PASCAL's own headline should be read. The published ResNet-18/CIFAR-10 \(T_{\mathrm{eff}} = 3.14\) is reproducible from the released script only when its 17 listed per-layer horizons are silently truncated to the 10 energy coefficients the script's zip() call actually consumes, a mapping neither the paper nor the code documents.

Two later accelerator papers do synthesize hardware for the neuron this family introduces, though neither synthesizes the mixed-horizon schedule itself. Ramesh and Srinivasan's own PASCAL paper separately reports an RTL synthesis and cycle-accurate simulation of its PASC-IF neuron at one uniform \(T=4\) design point, in modified LoAS and SparTen accelerator models synthesized with Synopsys Design Compiler at 560 MHz in a 40-nanometer library, an E4 simulator result that tests only the neuron's overhead relative to a standard IF neuron and does not exercise Adaptive Layerwise mixed-horizon execution. Venkatesh, Radhakrishnan, Rakshit, and Srinivasan's APEX integrates the same PASC-IF neuron natively into the LoAS accelerator's dataflow as a fully combinational circuit, synthesized across a \(T \in \{4, 8, 16\}\) and INT4/INT8 design-space sweep at 400 MHz in 40-nanometer CMOS, reporting 69.23 millijoules per inference at \(T=8\) against a 181.76-millijoule LoAS baseline at \(T=32\), also E4 simulator rather than a fabricated chip. QAC's own hardware-efficiency appendix, by contrast, gives only a qualitative discussion, warning that a pipelined multicore accelerator can stall while a downstream layer waits for an upstream layer to finish a different number of steps; this survey's claim index keeps that discussion below the documented-toolchain-path bar it uses for E3 rather than promoting a feasibility argument to deployment evidence . MT-SNN, in both its withdrawn ICLR manuscript and its later accepted Frontiers abstract, which names a revised alignment mechanism the two records should not be read as documenting identically, contains no compiler, RTL, simulator, or physical run at all, and both versions remain E5 . Manjunath, Ramesh, and Srinivasan's NeuroFlex extends the same PASC-IF equivalence to a column-level granularity rather than a per-layer one; Section 8.13 discusses it as an architectural intervention rather than a temporal one.

8.11 Adaptive Inference and Alternative-Code Conversion

SEENN and Dynamic Confidence intervene against the same finite-window error along a different axis than Section 8.10's static per-layer allocation. Rather than assigning a fixed \(T_l\) to each layer in advance, both vary a single, whole-network terminal horizon \(T_{\mathrm{stop}}(x)\) per input, stopping every layer together once a runtime criterion is satisfied, exactly the distinction Section 4.1 already establishes between per-input early exit and static per-layer allocation. Li, Geller, Kim, and Panda's SEENN offers a confidence-threshold variant, SEENN-I, and a reinforcement-learned policy variant, SEENN-II, built on a QCFS or temporal efficient training (TET) base network, reaching an average of 1.08 timesteps on CIFAR-10 for SEENN-II against full baselines of up to \(T=6\). Li, Jones, and Furber's Dynamic Confidence decodes a confidence signal from the SNN's output over time and applies a Pareto-calibrated threshold on top of a QCFS or QFFS backbone, reducing an average from baselines of \(T=4\) or \(T=14\) to roughly 1.28 to 4.71 timesteps depending on configuration. Both report GPU latency and a spike-count-based energy proxy rather than a chip measurement, and both are E5. Neither paper treats the hardware question as settled. SEENN's latency figure is explicitly measured on an NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPU, and Dynamic Confidence's own text states directly that the real power saving from running on neuromorphic hardware is unknown to its authors . Because every layer in both methods still advances on one shared running step count, a pipelined multi-core accelerator could in principle cut a uniform per-layer \(T\) short once its confidence criterion is met, without the stall failure mode Section 8.10 describes for a genuinely heterogeneous per-layer schedule; no source examined for this section demonstrates that behavior on a physical part.

The interventions in Sections 8.9 through 8.11 all keep Section 8's rate-matching correspondence and change what feeds it. A separate family instead changes the correspondence itself, replacing the encoder, channel, and decoder Section 4.2 defines with a different code entirely, intervening against Section 8's encoding-and-readout error by changing the code the encoder and decoder use rather than the statistics carried through a fixed rate code. Section 4.3 already fixes what each of these codes represents, how its encoder and decoder work, and what it costs in steps, neurons, or payload width; this section records only where each conversion algorithm intervenes and what evidence supports the result it reports.

Lenz, Orchard, and Sheik's Quartz converts to a time-to-first-spike code using counterweight and rectifier synapses rather than the dynamic-threshold machinery earlier TTFS conversions required, and because each neuron fires at most once, the conversion needs no soft-reset mechanism at all, unlike every rate-coded method in Sections 8.9 through 8.11. Brehove, Tumpa, Kyubwa, Menon, and Narayanan convert a quantized ANN to a Sigma-Delta Neural Network built on Loihi 2's native, up to 24-bit signed graded-spike payload, transmitting activation deltas rather than a spike count. Jiang, Chen, Li, Liu, Gao, Zhang, and Fang's Adaptive Fission applies a population-coding transformation after training, on top of an already converted or directly trained network, trading additional temporal integration for a group of binary sub-neurons carrying unequal fixed weights, which Section 4.3 records as a spatial axis orthogonal to rate and temporal coding rather than a fifth primitive. All three abandon rate coding at the point of conversion itself, rather than adjusting the activation function, the neuron, or the timestep schedule around a rate code, which distinguishes them structurally from every method in Sections 8.9 through 8.11. Section 8.12 records the hardware evidence for all three together, since in every confirmed case in the audited corpus, abandoning rate coding for a platform-native code is also what carries a conversion method to physical silicon.

Rank order coding is the code family for which no conversion theorem exists among the sources reviewed for this survey, for the reason Section 4.3 develops in full. Every conversion proof surveyed matches a per-layer magnitude, and an ordinal object drawn from \(N!\) permutations has no natural target for that convergence argument. BrainChip's Akida carries a genuine rank-order-coding lineage at its input-encoding stage, but the standard QuantizeML-to-CNN2SNN route nearly all published Akida results use collapses to a single-pass, order-agnostic threshold comparison inside hidden layers . The collapse is total rather than partial. There is no user-exposed multi-timestep simulation horizon, so the mapped network evaluates in a single feedforward pass; no membrane potential carries across timesteps; and there is no reset by subtraction, only a static threshold computed once at conversion time. Rate coding of an activation as a spike count over a horizon \(T\) is replaced outright by a step-wise quantized ReLU evaluated once, a substitution two independent physical-hardware studies confirm, Lunghi et al. and Ziegler et al. , and a reading of the toolkit's own source code, which this survey records but has not independently verified, supports the same conclusion . That converted route is therefore neither a new rate-coded low-timestep conversion algorithm nor an order-sensitive one, and this survey does not count it toward either family's evidence base. Section 11.4 traces this same transformation as a named deployment route, with its pipeline mechanics and its Akida-generation-specific route state. MorphIC's same-chip, same-task comparison of rate and rank-order coding, and the resulting tenfold measured energy difference at a 1.9-percentage-point accuracy cost, is developed fully in Section 4.3 and is not repeated here .

8.12 Hardware-Aware Conversion and Hybrid Fine-Tuning

Through the falsifier search this survey conducted into 16 September 2026, no audited source both introduces a recent, low-timestep, spike-count or firing-rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion algorithm and reports that same proposed algorithm measured on named physical neuromorphic silicon . This is a bounded search result over a stated population of paper-level conversion algorithms; it is not a claim that rate-coded conversion cannot reach silicon, and it says nothing about direct training, classical conversion, or later application papers that reuse an existing converter. Every confirmed instance in the audited corpus of a paper that both proposes a conversion method and reports its own physical-silicon measurement abandons rate coding for a code native to the target platform, which is why this section and Section 8.11 describe the same four methods from two directions.

Quartz's own comparison is head-to-head against a rate-coded NxTF and SNN Toolbox baseline on the same Nahuku 32 Loihi 1 board using NxSDK 1.0.0, reporting 0.77 percent MNIST error against a 0.73 percent ANN baseline, and separately a considerably higher 25.14 percent CIFAR-10 error than the rate-coded NxTF result of 8.52 percent on the same dataset, which the paper itself frames as an energy-accuracy trade rather than an unconditional improvement . Brehove et al. measure their conversion on an in-house 16-chip Loihi 2 VPX board running nxkernel 0.3.0 with modified microcode, reporting chip-probe latency, throughput, power, energy per frame, and energy-delay product (EDP) against a Jetson Xavier baseline whose whole system-on-chip (SoC) power is not boundary-identical to Loihi's chip-side measurement. Adaptive Fission's population-coding conversion is deployed and measured on a physical Lynxi HP201 accelerator, reporting on-chip memory, per-epoch time, and system energy from board sensors that closed interfaces prevent from being separated into core-only figures . Andrei, Drăguțoiu, Béna, Akl, Li, Lohrmann, Mönich, and Boche extend Few-Spikes conversion to complex-valued layers for an unfolded multidimensional harmonic-retrieval network and map the result to a physical single-chip SpiNNaker2 board, reporting board power against a Jetson Xavier baseline; the underlying representation is a serialized temporal Few-Spikes code, not a spike-count rate estimate. All four reach E1 neuromorphic.

APEX and NeuroFlex, discussed for their temporal and architectural contributions in Sections 8.10 and 8.13, synthesize RTL for the PASC-IF neuron rather than fabricating silicon, an E4 simulator hardware model rather than a measured chip in either case . Datta et al.'s asserted Lava-DL-to-Loihi route, by contrast, names no board, generation, or measurement and remains E3 neuromorphic, the clearest example in this section of a documented-looking route that stops short of an exercised one. Read across this section, a hardware-aware conversion's evidence class tracks how specifically it names a generation, a toolchain version, and a measurement boundary, not how confidently its introduction or appendix invokes a chip's name.

A third strategy neither reshapes the ANN's training objective, as Section 8.9 does, nor redesigns the neuron, as Sections 8.9 and 8.10 do, but instead corrects a network's activation statistics after conversion, calibrating against Section 8's residual-and-reset error and Section 8's clipping-and-discretization error that an earlier conversion step already introduced, without altering the underlying correspondence theorem. Li, Deng, Dong, Gong, and Gu's calibration method adjusts bias, weight, and initial-membrane-potential statistics on a network already converted at up to 256 steps, explicitly keeping the first layer's input continuous rather than binary because generating binary spikes there degrades accuracy, and computes its energy from Rathi and Roy's published per-operation constants rather than from a measured value. Hao, Ding, Bu, Huang, and Yu's Calibrating Offset Spikes (COS) iteratively detects and corrects the offset between an SNN's spike output and the value its source ANN activation would produce, reaching a headline six-step configuration, four calibration steps plus two inference steps. Wang, Fang, Cao, Ren, and Xu's Adaptive Calibration (AdaFire) introduces a training-free calibration procedure paired with an adaptive-firing burst neuron, reaching a \(T=8\) headline against \(T=32\) competitors at equal energy, with its energy figures again computed from an appendix-stated theoretical method rather than measured. All three report GPU simulation only and are E5; where a chip is named at all in these three papers, it is cited as motivation for a design or calibration choice, never as an executed deployment.

A second, structurally different hybrid pattern fine-tunes across the branch boundary itself rather than statistically. One record in this survey's route index describes a hybrid SNN-ANN network, conventional PyTorch or TensorFlow layers alongside SLAYER- or Lava-trained spiking layers, mapped through NengoLoihi to physical Loihi 1 hardware, with power, core count, and spike-propagation-delay latency measured on-chip . This record is retrieved from structured metadata rather than the paper's full text and carries a provisional verification status in this survey's source registry; its reported E1 neuromorphic measurement should be read with that limitation attached until the underlying paper is independently checked against its full text. Section 8.13 returns to the architecture this method builds, a network that is neither wholly ANN nor wholly SNN.

8.13 Emerging Architectures

Every method in Sections 8.9 through 8.12 keeps one assumption of Section 8's architecture preparation intact, that a network is prepared once, as a whole, into a wholly artificial or a wholly spiking form before it runs. Two records in the audited corpus intervene on that partition itself, executing part of a network as an ANN and part as an SNN within a single inference, which targets Section 8's architecture-dependent error at the level of the network's structure rather than at a layer's activation, neuron, or timestep count. Manjunath, Ramesh, and Srinivasan's NeuroFlex extends PASCAL's PASC-IF neuron and its underlying integer-exact equivalence proof from a layer-wide granularity to a column-level one, in which individual output columns within a single layer independently execute in ANN mode or PASC-IF SNN mode, chosen offline by a profiling-driven scheduler tied to each column's own QCFS quantization level. Because the equivalence proof it inherits depends only on local, per-neuron integer arithmetic, the paper argues it survives being applied column by column rather than layer by layer, and reports 98.6 to 99.3 percent processing-element-array utilization on VGG-16, ResNet-34, GoogLeNet, and BERT against 71.0 to 83.9 percent for coarser, layer-wise hybrid scheduling, synthesized in RTL at 560 MHz in a 40-nanometer library rather than fabricated, an E4 simulator result .

The hybrid SNN-ANN network introduced in Section 8.12 makes the same kind of partition at a coarser, layer-wide granularity instead of a column-wide one, and is the one record in this group reporting execution on physical rather than synthesized hardware, subject to the provisional status Section 8.12 already attaches to it . Both records are early enough, and few enough, that this survey states their existence as a design direction rather than as a demonstrated deployment pattern. Section 14 returns to algorithm-hardware co-design as an open research problem, and the column-level and layer-level granularities distinguished here are exactly the design axis that discussion resumes.

Nine intervention points, organized this way, change different pieces of the classical pipeline Section 8 derives and target different mechanisms in the error taxonomy Section 8 fixes. Every one of them still reports its result against an implicit contract, namely which fields of the network (code, precision, reset, time model, target hardware) a paper's own comparison actually holds fixed, and which it silently lets vary. Nothing in Sections 6 through 8 has forced that contract to be written down. An activation-shaping paper's hardware citation is motivation and not a run; a temporal-allocation paper's averaged timestep is a layer statistic and not a latency; a hardware-aware paper's asserted route is a documented intention and not, by itself, a measurement. Section 9 makes the contract explicit, fixing the eleven fields a converted or a directly trained network must state before its deployment claims can be compared against another paper's at all.
