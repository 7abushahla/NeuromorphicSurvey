8 ANN-to-SNN Conversion

Section 6 introduced conversion as the second branch of the fork, training a conventional network with ordinary backpropagation and only afterward re-expressing it in spikes. Training a spiking network directly remains harder to scale and slower to converge than training a conventional network with backpropagation and its mature tooling, which is why conversion trains first and re-expresses second rather than training from scratch. Conversion changes the representation a trained network computes with and the semantics its units execute; it does not make the network biologically realistic, since the converted unit remains an integrate-and-fire threshold at the efficient end of the model menu of Section 3.1. Most conversion methods share one idea, that a spiking unit's firing activity over a finite simulation horizon can approximate the continuous activation of the unit it replaces. This section makes that idea precise, fixing one derivation, built directly on the staged update and reset term of Section 3 and the firing fraction and horizon vocabulary of Section 4, and one end-to-end pipeline that every later comparison in this survey refers back to rather than re-deriving. The pipeline has five stages. Normalization removes network elements that do not fit the staged update of Section 3.2 as they stand, chiefly batch normalization, max pooling, and unbalanced residual branches. Parameter mapping chooses the integrate-and-fire weights, thresholds, and biases that make the mapped network's firing fractions track the trained network's activations. Encoding places the network's input onto the mapped network's first layer. Temporal execution runs the staged update for a finite horizon \(T\). Readout decodes a class score from the final layer. The section has four parts, the classical derivation, the error sources a finite horizon exposes, the low-latency methods that intervene against them, and the methods that convert with a target in view.

8.1 Classical Conversion

The first ANN-to-SNN conversion framework was proposed by Pérez-Carrasco et al. , who mapped ANNs with sigmoid activations to SNNs composed of LIF neurons by appropriately scaling synaptic weights, establishing the feasibility of parameter transfer from ANNs to spiking dynamics. A major breakthrough was introduced by Cao et al. , who showed that ReLU-based ANNs can be converted to SNNs of IF neurons under rate coding. The core insight is that, for nonnegative activations, an IF neuron's spike count (or firing rate) over a time window can approximate the corresponding ANN activation. Figure 25 draws this correspondence for one unit, the ReLU unit beside its integrate-and-fire twin, and plots how the twin's firing fraction closes on the activation, in units of the scale, as the window grows. Owing to the widespread adoption of ReLU in modern deep learning, most subsequent conversion methods follow this rate-based IF conversion framework, the one this part derives.

Diehl et al. and Rueckauer et al. gave this framework its data-based weight and threshold normalization, calibrating the mapping from a network's own activation statistics rather than from a fixed constant, and Sengupta et al.'s SPIKE-NORM extended the same normalization to VGG and residual architectures by estimating the calibration constant online from a short simulation of the partially mapped network. Rueckauer et al. also fixed reset-by-subtraction as the reset rule the framework needs, the rule that keeps the residual charge a hard reset discards. A later line makes the threshold itself adaptive during learning rather than fixed by a calibration pass, self-driven adaptive threshold plasticity for low-latency, sparse spiking computation . This part fixes the architectural assumptions a trained network must satisfy (Section 8.1.1), the correspondence between a ReLU activation and a firing fraction (Section 8.1.2), the encoding, readout, parameter mapping, and architecture preparation that carry that correspondence from input to output (Section 8.1.3, Section 8.1.4, and Section 8.1.5), a walkthrough of the five stages on one network (Section 8.1.6), and the asymptotic statement together with the cases the derivation does not cover (Section 8.1.7 and Section 8.1.8).

8.1.1 Purpose and Architectural Assumptions

Conversion asks a narrower question than direct training does. Given a network already trained to convergence with ordinary methods, and the surrogate-gradient machinery of Section 7 set aside, can its computation be re-expressed exactly, or with a bounded and characterized error, using only integrate-and-fire units, binary events, and the reset rule fixed in Section 3.5. The earliest instances of this question were asked of shallow or fully connected networks and hand-built spiking equivalents , and Stromatias et al. answer a related version of it for a deep belief network mapped onto SpiNNaker before the convolutional, ReLU-specific pipeline this section derives was formalized. The derivation below is specific to that later, ReLU-specific pipeline.

The derivation and the pipeline built on it hold only under a stated set of architectural assumptions, and the failure modes cataloged in Section 8.1.8 are exactly the consequences of violating one of them. First, every nonlinearity the source network applies between affine layers is a ReLU, so every target activation is non-negative and, on the calibration data the mapping is built from, bounded by a finite per-layer value. Second, every connection is feedforward with zero delay between layers, \(d_{ij}^l=0\) in the notation of Section 3.2, deferring configurable synaptic delay to the platform-specific treatment in Section 5. Third, batch normalization, where present, uses fixed running statistics rather than batch statistics computed at inference time, so that it is a fixed affine map rather than a data-dependent one. Fourth, pooling and residual connections are restricted to the forms Section 8.1.5 shows fit the derivation, or are rewritten into those forms before mapping. Fifth, the per-step synaptic drive into every unit is bounded, so that the membrane potential defined in Section 3.2 stays within a fixed range independent of the horizon \(T\); Section 8.1.2 uses this bound directly, and Section 8.1.7 returns to what happens when it is violated.

8.1.2 The ReLU-to-Firing-Fraction Correspondence

Specialize the staged update of Section 3.2 to the IF instance fixed in Section 3.3, with the feedforward, zero-delay synaptic stage \(q_i^l[t]=\sum_j w_{ij}^l z_j^{l-1}[t]\) and the fixed threshold \(\theta_i^l\), and apply reset-by-subtraction from Section 3.5. Writing the three stages as one recurrence for the post-reset state, \begin{equation}\label{eq:if-recurrence} u_i^l[t] = u_i^l[t-1] + q_i^l[t] + b_i^l - z_i^l[t]\,\theta_i^l , \end{equation} and summing both sides over \(t=1,\ldots,T\) telescopes the \(u_i^l[t-1]\) terms and leaves \begin{equation}\label{eq:telescoped} u_i^l[T]-u_i^l[0] = \sum_{t=1}^{T} q_i^l[t] + T b_i^l - \theta_i^l \sum_{t=1}^{T} z_i^l[t] = \sum_{t=1}^{T} q_i^l[t] + T b_i^l - \theta_i^l N_i^l(T), \end{equation} using the finite-horizon spike count \(N_i^l(T)\) fixed in Section 4.2. Because \(q_i^l[t]\) is linear in the presynaptic events, the summed current is the weighted sum of the presynaptic spike counts. \begin{equation}\label{eq:summed-current} \sum_{t=1}^{T} q_i^l[t] = \sum_j w_{ij}^l N_j^{l-1}(T) = T\sum_j w_{ij}^l \rho_j^{l-1}(T), \end{equation} where \(\rho_j^{l-1}(T)=N_j^{l-1}(T)/T\) is the discrete firing fraction of Section 4.2. Substituting this and dividing by \(T\theta_i^l\) gives \begin{equation}\label{eq:rate-identity} \rho_i^l(T) = \frac{1}{\theta_i^l}\left(\sum_j w_{ij}^l \rho_j^{l-1}(T) + b_i^l\right) - \epsilon_i^l(T), \qquad \epsilon_i^l(T) := \frac{u_i^l[T]-u_i^l[0]}{T\,\theta_i^l}. \end{equation}

This identity is exact for every finite \(T\ge 1\). It is not an approximation, and nothing in its derivation has yet invoked a limit; \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) is defined so that the identity holds by construction, and the entire content of the correspondence is in how \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) behaves. Under the bounded-drive assumption of Section 8.1.1, standard arguments in the conversion literature bound the membrane excursion \(|u_i^l[T]-u_i^l[0]|\) by a constant independent of \(T\) whenever the neuron's asymptotic firing fraction sits strictly inside \((0,1)\), the regime away from the ReLU's own zero and saturation edges, which gives \(\epsilon_i^l(T) = O(1/T)\), the discretization error \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\) of the notation , . At the two edges the identity still holds exactly but for a different reason. A unit whose net drive stays negative for the whole horizon never crosses threshold, so \(\rho_i^l(T)=0\) at every \(T\), matching \(\mathrm{ReLU}\) at zero without the residual needing to shrink at all; a unit whose drive would require firing on every step saturates at \(\rho_i^l(T)=1\) for the same reason. Section 8.1.7 turns this per-neuron identity into a statement about the whole network as \(T\to\infty\), and Section 8.2 names the regime where the bound on \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) is not yet small at the \(T\) actually used as one of its finite-time error mechanisms.

8.1.3 Input Encoding and Output Readout

The identity above already fixes what happens at every hidden layer once its input is a spike train with a well-defined firing fraction. The first and last layers are boundary cases, because the network's actual input is not itself the output of an IF neuron and its actual output is read before any further layer would consume it as spikes.

At the input, the classical pipeline uses direct or analog current injection rather than a Poisson process, treating each input value as a constant per-pixel current \(q_i^1[t]=\sum_j w_{ij}^1 x_j\) held fixed for the whole horizon, where \(x_j\) is the corresponding entry of the trained network's own input tensor. Constant injection makes \(q_i^1[t]\) exactly time-invariant, so the bound on \(\epsilon_i^1(T)\) from Section 8.1.2 applies with no additional variance term, unlike a Poisson-rate encoding of the same input, whose own stochastic variance scales a quadratically worse \(1/\sqrt{T}\) against the deterministic count's \(1/T\) (Section 4.3). Rueckauer et al. report this difference directly on a controlled ablation at matched \(T\), in which switching the first layer from Poisson-rate input to direct analog injection reduces the same converted network's classification error on CIFAR-10, with every other setting held fixed E5, a software-simulation result rather than a hardware measurement, which is why the derivation here is written for a deterministic drive.

At the output, the classical pipeline does not require the final layer to spike at all. Setting the final layer's threshold high enough that it never fires, or equivalently never applying the threshold and reset stages to it, turns \(\mathcal{M}^L\) into a pure accumulator, and summing its recurrence over \(T\) steps the same way as in Section 8.1.2, but with no reset term to track, gives the accumulated potential directly. \begin{equation}\label{eq:readout-accumulator} u_i^L[T] - u_i^L[0] = \sum_{t=1}^{T} q_i^L[t] + T b_i^L, \end{equation} with the initial potential retained exactly as Section 8.1.2 retains it. This section adopts the convention that the readout accumulator starts from a zero initial membrane potential, \(u_i^L[0]=0\). Under that convention, dividing by \(T\) gives \(u_i^L[T]/T = \sum_j w_{ij}^L \rho_j^{L-1}(T) + b_i^L\) exactly, with no residual \(\epsilon\) term at all; a nonzero initial value would instead carry the extra term \(u_i^L[0]/T\) into the right-hand side, a term that vanishes as \(T\) grows but is not itself zero at any finite \(T\). This is the class-accumulator decoder named among the options in Section 4.2, and it is why the final layer's decoded score is not subject to the same finite-count error that a spiking hidden layer carries. The deployment consequence is that only the hidden layers between input and output pay the discretization cost derived in Section 8.1.2; the choice to decode logits from an unreset accumulator rather than from a spike count is a design choice available because the final layer's output never needs to be re-encoded as an event train for a further layer to consume.

8.1.4 Parameter Mapping

The identity of Section 8.1.2 leaves the weights, thresholds, and biases of the mapped network unspecified beyond linearity; it holds for any values, which means it does not by itself tell a practitioner what to enter into \(w_{ij}^l\), \(\theta_i^l\), or \(b_i^l\). Parameter mapping is the separate question of which values make the mapped network's decoded activations reproduce the trained network's own pre-activations rather than an arbitrary rescaling of them.

Fix a per-layer calibration constant \(\lambda^l\), the scale symbol of the notation, chosen from the trained network's own activation statistics on a calibration set, either the observed maximum or a more outlier-robust percentile of it ; Sengupta et al.'s SPIKE-NORM instead estimates the same constant online, from spikes produced by a short simulation of the partially mapped network rather than from the source network's own activation statistics directly. Define the decoded value at layer \(l\) as \(\hat a_i^l(T):=\lambda^l \rho_i^l(T)\), read directly from a hidden layer's firing fraction, or, at the final layer, from the unreset accumulator of the readout above. Setting the mapped network's threshold to the calibration constant, \(\theta_i^l=\lambda^l\), and its weight to the trained weight rescaled by the previous layer's constant, \(w_{ij}^l=\lambda^{l-1}\tilde w_{ij}^l\), with the bias carried over unchanged, \(b_i^l=\tilde b_i^l\), turns the identity of Section 8.1.2 into \begin{equation}\label{eq:conversion-identity} \hat a_i^l(T) = \sum_j \tilde w_{ij}^l\, \hat a_j^{l-1}(T) + \tilde b_i^l - \lambda^l \epsilon_i^l(T), \end{equation} which is the trained network's own affine pre-activation, evaluated at the previous layer's decoded value rather than its true activation, minus the same bounded residual carried forward from Section 8.1.2. Different papers place the ratio \(\lambda^{l-1}/\lambda^l\) on the weight, on the threshold, or on an explicit rescaling of the activation itself; these are algebraically equivalent packagings of the same normalization, and a reader comparing two papers' stated weights or thresholds directly, without checking which packaging each uses, will compare numbers that are not on the same footing.

Because the firing fraction \(\rho_i^l(T)\) is bounded in \([0,1]\) by construction, a spike count cannot fall below zero or exceed the horizon, the decoded value \(\hat a_i^l(T)=\lambda^l\rho_i^l(T)\) is confined to \([0,\lambda^l]\) regardless of how large or negative the right-hand side of the equation above becomes. This bounded-count origin of clipping is a structural consequence of representing a value as a bounded count, not an artifact of a particular calibration choice; Section 8.2.2 works out the resulting trade-off against discretization error. Choosing \(\lambda^l\) well is exactly the estimation problem the parameter-mapping methods above solve, and refining that estimate under a fixed, small \(T\) is the subject of the low-latency interventions Section 8.3 and Section 8.4 cover; this part fixes only the mapping's structure, not its optimization.

8.1.5 Architecture Preparation

Three components of a trained convolutional network do not fit the staged update of Section 3.2 as trained, and each must be rewritten before parameter mapping applies. Table 15 collects the three together with the mechanism each preparation step preserves.

Batch normalization in evaluation mode is a fixed per-channel affine map built from running statistics. \begin{equation}\label{eq:bn-affine} \hat y = \gamma\,\frac{y-\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\delta}}+\beta, \end{equation} which composes algebraically with the affine map of the convolution or linear layer immediately before it, \begin{equation}\label{eq:bn-fold} w' = w\cdot\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\delta}}, \qquad b' = b\cdot\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\delta}} + \beta - \frac{\gamma\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\delta}}, \end{equation} folding the two layers into one affine layer with weight \(w'\) and bias \(b'\) ahead of the ReLU. A batch-normalization layer computed from batch statistics at inference time is not this fixed map and does not fold this way, which is why the assumption in Section 8.1.1 restricts to the evaluation-mode case.

Average pooling is a fixed linear operator on its input window, so it commutes exactly with the substitution in Section 8.1.2's derivation; forwarding an averaged spike count rather than a single binary event changes the payload carried on that connection from binary to graded, in the sense of Section 3.3, but introduces no new error term. Max pooling is not linear in its inputs, and no exact analog of the firing-fraction identity holds for it; an instantaneous "any input fired" gating rule approximates the operation but can overestimate the pooled unit's rate relative to the single highest-rate input channel, which is why Rueckauer et al. document this approximation explicitly and why the great majority of classical and low-latency conversion pipelines replace max pooling with average pooling before mapping rather than attempting the approximation.

A residual or skip connection sums two branches that were calibrated independently, an identity path and a residual branch, each with its own \(\lambda^l\). Summing their raw spike trains at the merge point implicitly assumes both branches are expressed on the same scale; when they are not, the merged drive entering the shared post-add threshold no longer corresponds to the sum the trained network actually computed. Sengupta et al.'s extension of the pipeline to VGG and residual architectures handles this by rescaling both branches to a shared calibration constant before the merge, which is the architectural preparation this survey groups under normalization for residual networks specifically.

8.1.6 Walkthrough

Assembling Sections 8.1.3 through 8.1.5 in execution order rather than exposition order gives the pipeline named in the opening paragraph, normalization, parameter mapping, encoding, temporal execution, readout, applied here to one trained network end to end. Normalization first rewrites the trained graph, folding every batch-normalization layer into its preceding affine layer, replacing any max-pooling layer with average pooling, and rescaling every residual branch to a shared calibration constant, leaving a graph of affine layers, ReLU nonlinearities, and linear pooling. Parameter mapping then estimates a calibration constant \(\lambda^l\) for every layer from a held-out calibration batch and sets each layer's integrate-and-fire threshold and rescaled weight from Section 8.1.4's identity, carrying every bias over unchanged. Encoding injects the network's actual input tensor as a constant per-pixel current into the first layer for the entire horizon, as fixed in Section 8.1.3, rather than sampling any stochastic process. Temporal execution then runs the staged update of Section 3.2 for \(T\) discrete steps at every hidden layer, with reset-by-subtraction at every threshold crossing, producing a firing fraction \(\rho_i^l(T)\) at every hidden unit that Section 8.1.2 already characterizes. Readout accumulates the final layer's membrane potential without ever resetting it and divides by \(T\), giving the class scores from which the argmax decision is taken.

\(T\) here is the algorithmic horizon fixed in Section 4.1, a count of discrete update steps, not a wall-clock duration and not a proxy for it. A smaller \(T\) shortens the sum each unit accumulates and enlarges the bound on \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) from Section 8.1.2; it does not by itself establish a shorter inference latency on any particular target, because what one step of temporal execution costs in wall-clock time depends on the execution model that Sections 4.1 and 5 treat separately from the step count itself.

8.1.7 Asymptotic Equivalence and Finite-Time Behavior

Composing the identity of Section 8.1.4 across the \(L_{\mathrm{net}}\) layers of the mapped network gives, for the output layer, a sum of the finite-\(T\) residuals \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) accumulated at every hidden layer the output depends on, each weighted by the path of trained weights connecting that layer to the output. Two statements follow from this composition, and this survey states them separately rather than as one claim, because only one of them is exact at every finite \(T\) actually used.

The asymptotic statement is a limit. As \(T\to\infty\), with the calibration constants, weights, and thresholds of Section 8.1.4 held fixed, every per-layer residual \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) in the interior regime of Section 8.1.2 shrinks toward zero, and the network's decoded output converges to the trained network's own forward pass evaluated through a clipped ReLU, \(\mathrm{clip}(\cdot,0,\lambda^l)\), at every layer. This is the content of Rueckauer et al.'s convergence result for the reset-by-subtraction rule, and Ding et al. state and prove a version of the same layer-by-layer firing-rate correspondence, matching a magnitude between a source network and a converted network, for general feedforward and residual architectures. Both results are asymptotic; neither author claims the limit is reached at any finite \(T\), and this survey makes the same restriction explicit rather than leaving it implicit.

The finite-time statement is what Section 8.1.2's identity gives directly, and it does not vanish at the horizons any deployed system actually runs. Each hidden layer carries a residual bounded on the order of \(1/T\) in the interior regime, the envelope the right panel of Figure 25 plots for one unit, and exact at the two clipped edges, and these residuals do not stay confined to the layer where they arise. A hidden layer's own residual enters the next layer's synaptic drive as part of \(\rho_j^{l-1}(T)\), so a shallow network's error at some finite \(T\) is not simply the single largest per-layer term but a composition of per-layer terms along every path from input to output, weighted by the trained weights on that path. This composition has no universal closed form or growth law derivable from Section 8.1.2 alone, an instance of the prohibition, in the notation's terms, against assuming additivity or independence of named error mechanisms without a specific cited derivation. Section 8.2 is where this survey names and separately bounds the individual mechanisms, clipping, discretization, and the mismatch between a reset-to-zero and reset-by-subtraction trajectory, that this compounded finite-\(T\) residual decomposes into, rather than treating "the conversion error" as one undifferentiated quantity.

Neither statement licenses substituting one algorithmic quantity for another. Convergence as \(T\to\infty\) is convergence in the horizon defined in Section 4.1, not in wall-clock time; a claim that a smaller \(T\) achieves lower latency requires the separate, target-specific argument that Section 5 supplies for a named execution model, not an appeal to this section's asymptotic result.

8.1.8 Where the Derivation Does Not Apply

Each architectural assumption fixed in Section 8.1.1 has a corresponding failure case, and naming them here closes the loop the assumptions opened rather than leaving them as unenforced fine print.

A source network that uses a negative-valued or non-monotonic activation, rather than a ReLU, has no non-negative target for \(\rho_i^l(T)\in[0,1]\) to converge toward, since the derivation in Section 8.1.2 relies on the decoded value \(\hat a_i^l(T)=\lambda^l\rho_i^l(T)\) being confined to \([0,\lambda^l]\) by construction. Representing a signed target requires a signed or dual-channel emission scheme distinct from the single non-negative event this section assumes, an intervention Section 8.3.3 covers rather than a case this derivation extends to on its own.

A source network with recurrent connections, attention, or normalization layers computed from live batch or sequence statistics breaks the feedforward, fixed-weight linearity that Section 8.1.2's substitution \(\sum_{t=1}^T q_i^l[t]=T\sum_j w_{ij}^l \rho_j^{l-1}(T)\) depends on; if \(w_{ij}^l\) or the set of contributing units varies with \(t\) or with the input itself, no analogous closed-form telescoping identity has been established by the sources this section relies on.

A per-step synaptic drive that is not bounded independent of \(T\), violating the fifth assumption of Section 8.1.1, leaves the membrane excursion \(|u_i^l[T]-u_i^l[0]|\) unbounded in \(T\) as well, so the \(O(1/T)\) rate for \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) no longer follows; a sustained, strongly negative drive without a floor on the membrane potential is the clearest instance, and it is a modeling and implementation detail this section flags rather than resolves.

Rank order coding, defined among the code families of Section 4.3, lies outside this correspondence for a reason traceable directly to Section 8.1.2's own construction rather than to a general property of non-rate codes. The derivation needs a magnitude per neuron, a spike count \(N_i^l(T)\) that can be normalized into a firing fraction; a rank-order channel carries no such magnitude, because each neuron fires at most once per wave and the encoded content is that neuron's rank among the population, not any count attached to it in isolation . There is accordingly no \(\rho_i^l(T)\) to define for a single rank-order unit, and every conversion proof this survey locates, including Rueckauer et al.'s and Ding et al.'s , is stated over a matched magnitude rather than a matched ordering, consistent with Section 4.3's statement that this is why the code sits outside the rate-matching correspondence.

These are the assumptions Section 8.2 traces as the entry points of finite-time conversion error, and the targets Section 8.3 and Section 8.4 organize their interventions around, most of which relax exactly one of them while leaving the rest of this section's pipeline unchanged.

8.2 Conversion Error Sources

Rate coding, read at a finite horizon rather than in the limit Section 8.1.7 takes, is a quantization process before it is anything else. Over a horizon of \(T\) steps, a soft-reset IF neuron's firing rate can only take one of \(T+1\) values, \(0, 1/T, 2/T,\dots,1\), so the map from a neuron's normalized input to its firing fraction \(\rho_i^l(T)\) is a uniform quantizer with three cases, zero for a non-positive input, the input scaled by \(T\) and rounded to the nearest integer in between, and one at or above the point where the neuron would fire on every step, \begin{equation}\label{eq:rate-quantizer} \rho_i^l(T) \approx \begin{cases} 0, & z_i^l/\theta_i^l \le 0,\\[2pt] \dfrac{1}{T}\,\mathrm{round}\!\left(\dfrac{z_i^l}{\theta_i^l}\,T\right), & 0 < z_i^l/\theta_i^l < 1, & \textcolor{#D63A2F}{\text{quantization error}}\\[6pt] 1, & z_i^l/\theta_i^l \ge 1, & \textcolor{#D63A2F}{\text{clipping error}} \end{cases} \end{equation} where \(z_i^l\) is the same aggregate synaptic drive Section 8.1.2's identity already carries, and the normalized input is this drive divided by the neuron's own threshold \(\theta_i^l\), the same ratio PseudoSNN's own analysis normalizes by. Two of this quantizer's three cases already account for two of the mechanisms named below, the middle case for discretization error, and the last case for clipping error, both fixed properties of the quantizer itself rather than of any particular spike train . Everything else Section 8.2.1 onward traces, residual and reset error, unevenness and timing error, their accumulation with depth, and the encoding and readout choices that make each term deterministic or stochastic, exists because a real, finite spike train is not the ideal time average this quantizer describes, which Section 8.2.1 onward derives. Section 8.3.1 writes this same map as the ANN's activation function, \(\eqref{eq:qcfs}\).

Section 8.1 derives the correspondence between a ReLU-activated ANN and a converted SNN under an asymptotic assumption, an inference horizon \(T\to\infty\) at which the discrete firing fraction \(\rho_i^l(T)\) defined in Section 4 exactly reproduces the corresponding activation. Every claim in this article that describes a converted network running with a finite, deliberately small \(T\) is a claim about what that asymptotic derivation leaves out. This part names four such mechanisms, states where in the pipeline each enters, whether it is a fixed structural consequence of the architecture or a random consequence of the input encoding, and how it interacts with the reset semantics fixed in Section 3. Section 8.3 and Section 8.4 organize their intervention methods by which of these four mechanisms they target.

8.2.1 A General Finite-Time Error Model

Write the finite-window deviation of a converted neuron's discrete firing fraction from its asymptotic target as \begin{equation}\label{eq:rate-deviation} \delta_i^l(T) \;=\; \rho_i^l(T) \;-\; \lim_{T'\to\infty}\rho_i^l(T'), \end{equation} evaluated at the layer index \(l\), unit index \(i\), and closed window \([1,T]\) fixed by Section 4's definition of \(N_i^l(T)\) and \(\rho_i^l(T)\). This survey organizes \(\delta_i^l(T)\) into four named contributions using the symbols \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\), \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\), \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{reset}}\), and \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{time}}\) reserved for this purpose, \begin{equation}\label{eq:error-decomposition} \delta_i^l(T) \;\approx\; \epsilon_{\mathrm{clip},i}^l \;+\; \epsilon_{\mathrm{disc},i}^l(T) \;+\; \epsilon_{\mathrm{reset},i}^l[T] \;+\; \epsilon_{\mathrm{time},i}^l[T]. \end{equation} The approximate sign is deliberate. This is an organizational device for exposition, not a derived identity. The literature proves an additive bound for a narrower claim than the one written above, decomposing the error of a specific converted activation function into a clipping term and a discretization term under a stated reset rule and a stated finite window ; that proof does not cover the timing term introduced in Section 8.2.4, and no source reviewed for this survey proves that all four terms are independent, additive, or governed by one growth law across architectures and reset rules. Treat the equation as a labeling scheme for the rest of this section, not as a formula to evaluate.

Each of these four terms enters the pipeline of Section 8.1.6 at a specific stage. \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\) and \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\) enter at parameter mapping and at the counting operation, \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{reset}}\) enters at the reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\) fixed in Section 3.2, and a dispersion component of \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{reset}}\), unevenness error, is kept distinct from \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{time}}\). Section 8.2.5 traces how normalization, pooling, residual paths, depth, and readout change how much of each term a given layer inherits, and Section 8.2.6 does the same for the input.

Where in this pipeline finite-time error enters, and how much of it a deeper layer inherits from the layers before it, is a structural question rather than a numeric one; no single measured trace generalizes across architectures, reset rules, and horizons. Figure 28 gives a conceptual answer.

8.2.2 Clipping and Discretization Error

Clipping error, \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\), is a mechanism of the parameter mapping stage, not of the neuron dynamics that follow it. Before conversion, that stage fixes a firing threshold \(\theta_i^l\), or equivalently an activation scale \(\lambda^l\) in the sense Section 8.1.4 assigns that symbol, so that the neuron's finite range of achievable spike counts stands in for the ANN activation's range. Any input drive that would have produced an ANN activation above the value that scale represents is truncated at the maximum achievable count, exactly as a hard upper clip truncates a real-valued activation. The semantic effect is a value-dependent, one-sided bias, present only for inputs at or above the chosen scale, that does not shrink as the simulation horizon \(T\) grows, because it is a property of the chosen scale rather than of the counting resolution. The trade-off this creates is between two threshold-setting failure modes on opposite sides, a scale set too low clips frequently and biases every affected unit downward, while a scale set too high wastes achievable count levels on activation values the network rarely produces, which is itself a discretization cost. Early parameter mapping methods, the weight and threshold balancing procedures of the first conversion papers, exist specifically to choose this scale from a calibration pass over training data, an unresolved tension the field returns to repeatedly rather than a solved constant . This is a mechanism claim about where and why the bias appears, not a measured accuracy result, and it is unconditional on the reset rule in Section 3.5, since a spike count cannot exceed the finite window \(T\) regardless of which reset stage is chosen.

Discretization error, \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\), is a mechanism of the counting operation itself, present even for an input drive that a correctly scaled threshold would not clip. Section 4 establishes that a fixed threshold restricts the achievable values of \(N_i^l(T)\) to the integers from zero to \(T\), so the achievable firing fractions \(\rho_i^l(T)\) form a uniform grid spaced at intervals of \(1/T\) . Whatever real-valued activation the ANN side would produce, the SNN side can only represent a point on that grid, a floor from an empty membrane or a round from a half-threshold initial potential (Section 8.3.1), whose worst-case magnitude is bounded by the grid spacing itself and therefore shrinks as \(T\) grows. The semantic effect is a bounded, deterministic rounding error rather than the unbounded, value-dependent bias clipping produces, the same quantity Section 8.2's quantizer view names quantization error elsewhere in the literature; this survey keeps the name discretization error throughout to avoid conflating a temporal spike-count grid with the spatial quantization grid of an ANN's own weights and activations, a distinction Section 8.3.1 makes precise. The trade-off is direct, halving the grid spacing means doubling \(T\), so discretization error is the term that most directly ties accuracy to simulation length, and it is the term every low-latency conversion method in Section 8.3.1's activation-shaping family is built to compress without simply enlarging \(T\) . The deployment consequence is that clipping and discretization are separable mechanisms that a single calibration step cannot both fix at once, choosing a scale narrow enough to keep the achievable grid fine costs clipped range, and choosing a scale wide enough to avoid clipping costs grid resolution at the same fixed \(T\); a formal treatment of this trade-off as a joint optimization over both quantities exists and is itself an inference claim about the shape of that trade-off, not a hardware measurement .

How that interaction plays out is a property of the activation distribution a layer's own trained weights produce, not of the threshold-setting rule alone. Low-bit quantization is known to depend on the distribution it meets, weights and activations being Gaussian-like by nature while uniform-like distributions quantize with less loss , and robust-quantization work ties a tensor's quantization sensitivity to its distribution shape through its kurtosis . Datta and Beerel find that earlier conversion strategies incorrectly assume the pre-activation values are uniformly distributed and correct it with a training method that captures the distributions, reaching two timesteps. A single calibration pass that fixes \(\lambda^l\) once, uniformly across a layer, therefore optimizes against whichever distribution the calibration set happens to show it, not against the trade-off in general.

8.2.3 Residual Membrane Potential and Reset Error

Reset error, \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{reset}}\), is a mechanism of the reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\) that Section 3.5 already makes load-bearing for the asymptotic derivation. Only reset-by-subtraction, which retains the residual charge \(r_i^l[t]\) a hard reset discards, removes the persistent input-dependent bias as \(T\to\infty\) (Section 3.5) . Reset error is what remains at any finite \(T\) even under reset-by-subtraction, because the window closes before every unit of residual charge has necessarily been converted into an output spike. At the final step \(t=T\), whatever membrane potential \(u_i^l[T]\) sits below threshold is charge the neuron accepted but never spent, and it is discarded by the readout the moment the window ends regardless of which reset rule governed the steps inside that window. The semantic effect is a mean-level bias in \(N_i^l(T)\) that depends on the neuron's own charge history over the closed window, not a random fluctuation that a resimulation would erase.

Naming this quantity residual membrane potential rather than folding it silently into discretization error matters because its size is not fixed by the grid spacing alone. A neuron's leftover charge at cutoff depends on where in its own integration cycle the window happened to end, which depends in turn on every upstream layer's own finite-time behavior, so the bias compounds with depth rather than staying at one layer's fixed grid resolution. This is exactly the failure mode a line of work formalizes for deep and residual architectures under uniform, hard reset, terming it a residual membrane potential problem and reporting worse degradation as network depth increases . A closely related line explicitly targets a converted network's initial membrane condition, \(u_i^l[0]\), as the free parameter that determines how much residual charge a finite window can accumulate before it starts producing this bias, and a separate line targets the same bias by explicitly modeling and subtracting the expected residual term at conversion time . A third diagnostic line observes that the resulting output spikes are not merely fewer or more numerous than the target but offset from the value the discretization grid alone would predict, naming this an offset-spike phenomenon rather than treating it as ordinary discretization noise . All four are mechanism and inference claims about where this bias originates and how it scales with depth, not hardware measurements, a gap Section 13 returns to for the low-latency conversion literature generally. The deployment consequence is that reset error cannot be fixed by lengthening \(T\) alone in a deep network, because added depth adds new residual-charge accumulation points faster than a longer window drains any single one of them.

8.2.4 Unevenness Error and Timing Error

Unevenness error and timing error are not the same mechanism, and this survey does not use them interchangeably. Unevenness error is a dispersion of residual charge across units that share a layer and a target rate. Timing error is a structural propagation delay tied to network depth. A network can suffer from one without the other.

Unevenness error is the part of \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{reset}}\) that a single-neuron, mean-value account of residual charge misses. Two units with the same target firing fraction \(\rho_i^l(T)\) over a shared window need not carry the same instantaneous input history inside that window, because the individual arrival times of their presynaptic events, set by whatever unevenness of upstream firing already exists, differ even when the two windows' totals agree. Since the reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\) and the membrane stage \(\mathcal{M}^l\) are stateful rather than simple accumulators, two input trains with an identical mean rate but a different within-window arrival pattern leave different amounts of residual charge \(r_i^l[t]\) at the window's end. This is the mechanism the Quantization Clip-Floor-Shift (QCFS) line of analysis isolates as the third term of its own three-term account, beyond clipping and discretization, one that a value-independent scale correction and a value-independent count-resolution improvement do not reach . The semantic effect is that two neurons converted from the same ANN activation value can carry different amounts of finite-time error depending on where in the network they sit and what firing pattern their inputs happen to produce, so unevenness error is heterogeneous across a layer rather than a single per-layer constant. Two lines of work target this dispersion directly rather than averaging over it, Yousefzadeh et al.'s signed neurons that fire a positive or a negative spike on crossing one threshold in either direction and Hu et al.'s Fast-SNN , which pairs a signed spike representation with a learned clipping range; Section 8.3.3 covers both as neuron-model interventions. The deployment consequence is that a per-layer average error estimate, the kind a calibration pass over a validation set naturally produces, can understate the worst-affected units in that same layer.

Timing error, \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{time}}\), is a different mechanism located at the temporal execution stage rather than at the reset stage. Under the layer-synchronous update order Section 3.2 fixes, a unit at layer \(l\) cannot register an event fired at layer \(l-1\), step \(t\), before step \(t+d_{ij}^l\), where \(d_{ij}^l\) is the synaptic delay Section 3.4 reserves that symbol for, at minimum one step when no explicit delay is modeled. A signal entering the network at step 1 therefore cannot influence a unit at depth \(l\) before step \(l\), so the deepest layers of a network with many layers spend part of any short window simply waiting for the first wave of events to arrive, a fixed structural cost that a wider threshold scale or a finer discretization grid cannot remove. This cost is why depth interacts with a small \(T\) even when clipping, discretization, and reset error are all individually small, a constraint the literature on very deep converted networks documents directly by needing larger simulation windows and dedicated reset treatments specifically as convolutional depth increases . Where timing error is deliberately made unequal across layers, by assigning a different simulation length to each layer rather than treating it as an unavoidable propagation cost, the resulting per-layer realignment is a Section 8.3.4 intervention rather than a Section 8.2 mechanism, and this section stops at naming the underlying cost without describing that remedy.

8.2.5 Architecture-Dependent Accumulation

The four mechanisms above are defined for a single unit and a single layer. What a specific architecture experiences is how many times, and in what combination, each unit's output is passed through another unit that adds its own contribution. Section 8.1's normalization stage assumes a specific set of transformations, batch normalization folded into the preceding weight and bias, average pooling, and an explicit prescription for residual summation, precisely because these are the transformations the finite-time account below needs to hold as claimed.

Batch normalization folding is exact only in the asymptotic derivation; at finite \(T\) the folded bias becomes an added constant current the membrane stage integrates over the same closed window as every other input, so it is clipped, discretized, and left with the same residual charge at cutoff as any other contribution to \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]\). Folding removes a separate computational stage, not that stage's finite-time error, and treating a folded network as automatically equivalent to its unfolded ANN counterpart at low \(T\) is an inference this survey does not make without a cited derivation covering the folded case specifically.

Pooling divides along exactly the line Section 4's linearity properties predict. Average pooling is linear, so a pooled unit's finite-time error is a direct, unweighted composition of its inputs' own \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\), \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\), and \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{reset}}\) terms rather than a new error source. Max pooling is not linear in spike counts, so a converted network built with it needs a separate approximation whose error this survey does not characterize in general, since its magnitude is a property of the specific approximation chosen rather than of the pooling operation itself . The deployment consequence is that Section 8.1.5's average-pooling assumption is not a stylistic simplification, a network trained with max pooling either needs retraining with average pooling first or carries an additional, separately characterized error term this taxonomy does not cover.

Residual summation compounds the timing term specifically, since an identity shortcut and a transformed path that has passed through additional layers have unequal depth from the network's input, and by Section 8.2.4's timing mechanism, unequal depth means unequal minimum propagation delay; the two operands also carry unequal accumulated clipping, discretization, and residual error, having passed through unequal numbers of stages. Sengupta et al. track how conversion accuracy degrades as convolutional depth and residual connections are added, and Han et al.'s residual membrane potential neuron (RMP-SNN) introduces a reset treatment aimed at the resulting residual-charge accumulation in deep and residual architectures specifically. Depth is therefore not a multiplier on a single-layer error rate, each additional layer contributes its own instance of all four mechanisms and its own minimum propagation delay, and a residual architecture sums two branches whose accumulated error and delay already differ.

The readout stage inherits whatever the deepest layer before it has accumulated rather than adding a fixed increment of its own. A spike-count or firing-fraction readout applies one more instance of \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\) on top of everything upstream. Reading the pre-threshold membrane potential of a non-spiking final layer directly, a decoder choice Section 4.2 already names among \(\mathcal{D}\)'s options, removes that one final discretization stage, since the readout no longer counts events at all, but it does not remove or correct any clipping, discretization, reset, unevenness, or timing error already embedded in the real-valued input that final layer receives from the layer below it . The deployment consequence is that a non-spiking readout is a bound on where error can still be introduced, not a correction of error already present.

8.2.6 Encoding and Readout Error

Sections 8.2.2 through 8.2.5 treat every mechanism as deterministic given a fixed input trajectory, which is the correct treatment for the direct or analog encoding convention, injecting a real-valued input as a constant current rather than as a Poisson process, that Section 8.1.6's own walkthrough and the great majority of the low-latency conversion literature adopt at the encoding stage . Under that convention, rerunning the same input through the same converted network with the same finite \(T\) reproduces the same \(\delta_i^l(T)\) exactly, because nothing in the encoder \(\mathcal{E}\) is random. Every bound and every mechanism named above is a deterministic-encoding claim in this sense, a fixed function of the input and the finite window, not a distribution over repeated runs.

A Poisson-rate encoder changes this at the source rather than downstream. Section 4.3 already establishes that deterministic count coding's quantization error scales as \(1/T\) while stochastic Poisson coding's variance instead scales as \(1/\sqrt{T}\) . The mechanism reason is that a Poisson encoder makes the input event train \(z_i^{0}[t]\) itself a random variable with the correct value only in expectation, so every downstream quantity built from it, \(N_i^l(T)\), \(\rho_i^l(T)\), and every error term in Table 17, becomes a random variable rather than a deterministic function of the input, and its spread does not close as fast as a deterministic encoder's bias does for the same increase in \(T\). The semantic effect is that a stochastic-encoding result reported at a given \(T\) describes a distribution over repeated draws, and a single reported accuracy number at that \(T\) is one sample from that distribution rather than a bound. The trade-off is why deterministic direct encoding, not Poisson encoding, is the near-universal convention in the low-latency conversion literature specifically, quadratically more steps are needed under Poisson encoding to match a deterministic encoder's precision at the same \(T\) , and the deployment consequence is that a stochastic-encoding and a deterministic-encoding result at the same nominal \(T\) are not directly comparable claims about accuracy at that horizon.

The decoder side carries a parallel, non-equivalent distinction. Choosing which of \(\mathcal{D}\)'s readout options, count, rate, first-spike time, membrane state, or a class accumulator over several output units, to use does not by itself introduce or remove stochastic variance; that property is fixed at the encoder. What the decoder choice changes is which of the deterministic terms in Table 17 the readout is exposed to, a count or rate readout adds one more instance of \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\), as Section 8.2.5 already states, while a membrane-state readout instead exposes whatever residual and unevenness error sits in that final layer's pre-threshold potential without the count-related bounding property Table 17 gives \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\). The two properties are governed by different mechanisms, enter the pipeline at opposite ends, and do not trade off against each other by adjusting \(T\) alone.

8.2.7 The Design Problem Finite-Time Error Creates

No single mechanism in this section is the barrier to low-latency conversion. Clipping, discretization, residual membrane potential (with its unevenness dispersion), and timing are four structurally distinct mechanisms, entering at different pipeline stages, with different growth behavior in \(T\), compounded further by normalization, pooling, residual paths, and depth. A method that only targets one of them leaves the others in place, which is the question Section 8.3 and Section 8.4 take up, organized by which mechanism a given intervention targets rather than by chronology or author.

The design problem these two sections answer is not simply how to reduce \(T\). A method that reduces the discretization grid's spacing at a given \(T\) without addressing residual membrane potential or timing error leaves those terms exactly as large as before, and a smaller \(T\) obtained this way is a claim about algorithmic step count, not wall-clock inference latency \(\tau_{\mathrm{inf}}\) (Section 4.1). The problem is therefore which mechanism, or combination of mechanisms, a given intervention can address without silently reintroducing another, at a stated finite \(T\), under a stated reset rule, for a stated architecture.

8.3 Low-Latency Conversion

Conceptually, most low-\(T\) conversion methods can be interpreted as targeted interventions on the classical "rate coding + (clipped) ReLU + IF" pipeline introduced by Cao et al. and derived in Section 8.1, aimed at the four finite-time error mechanisms Section 8.2 names. Broadly, these approaches fall into three categories, (i) modifying ANN nonlinearities to better align with discrete spike-based representations, (ii) altering spiking neuron dynamics to reduce systematic firing bias, or (iii) changing the spike encoding or representation to increase effective resolution without increasing \(T\). This part organizes the methods that follow by which of these three levers a method pulls, not by publication venue or year. The activation lever (i) acts before conversion, on the ANN's own training objective, so that the trained activations already sit on the levels a finite horizon can represent (Section 8.3.1). The neuron lever (ii) acts on the spiking neuron itself, either by calibrating its initial potential and reset against the residual error (Section 8.3.2) or by redesigning the neuron model that Section 3 holds fixed (Section 8.3.3). The encoding lever (iii) acts on how the network's values are carried in time, replacing the single global horizon of Section 8.1.7 with one that varies by layer (Section 8.3.4) or by input (Section 8.3.5), or replacing the rate code itself with a different code (Section 8.3.6). The methods that act with a specific target in view, hardware-aware conversion, hybrid fine-tuning, and the emerging architectures, follow in Section 8.4.

Low-latency methods also differ in the horizon they aim at, not only in which lever they pull. Most reach a few steps with one shared budget for every layer, QCFS and SRP at four steps, Error Compensation Learning at two, and the 2026 residual-membrane-potential-alignment method at two to eight. Some instead reach a single step by widening what one step can carry rather than shrinking the shared budget further, a per-channel threshold at \(T=1\) , signed multi-spikes emitted within one step , or a multi-threshold neuron proven equal to a multi-step IF neuron at \(T=1\) , spending the precision budget in the spike alphabet instead of in time. The methods of Section 8.3.4 spend that same budget unevenly across layers instead of uniformly across time or within one step. All three choices, a shared low \(T\), a wider single step, and a per-layer \(T_l\), are choices about where one precision budget goes, and each needs the execution model Section 8.4 and Section 11 ask for before it can be run rather than only simulated.

8.3.1 Activation Shaping

Classical conversion, described in Section 8.1, trains the ANN independent of the \(T+1\)-level grid of \(\eqref{eq:rate-quantizer}\) and imposes it only afterward, which is the mechanism to which Section 8.2.2 attributes clipping and discretization error. Conversion is, at bottom, a discretization problem with two coupled forms, spatial quantization of the ANN's own weights and activations and temporal quantization of the SNN's spike counts over a finite \(T\) , and the representational capacity of a converted network is constrained jointly by both. Zou et al.'s early median-quantization method trains the ANN with a quantized activation to minimize spike approximation error and converts it by either a temporal or a spatial rate-based method. Activation shaping intervenes on the mismatch this leaves, that a soft-reset neuron started from an empty membrane floors accumulated charge while ordinary ANN quantization rounds (\(\eqref{eq:rate-quantizer}\) writes the idealized map with a round, and the parameter choices that close this subsection make the neuron round too), training the activation function to anticipate the grid before conversion happens rather than imposing it afterward.

Bu, Fang, Ding, Dai, Yu, and Huang's QCFS activation replaces the ANN's ReLU with a clip-floor-shift nonlinearity trained to anticipate the target IF neuron's step response, \begin{equation}\label{eq:qcfs} a^l = \lambda^l\,\mathrm{clamp}\left(\frac{1}{L}\left\lfloor \frac{z^l L}{\lambda^l} + \varphi \right\rfloor,\ 0,\ 1\right), \end{equation} where \(z^l\) is the pre-activation input to layer \(l\), \(\lambda^l\) is the same trainable per-layer scale Section 8.1.4 calibrates, \(L\) is QCFS's own fixed quantization-level count, and \(\varphi\) is a shift term the paper sets to one half, so that \(\eqref{eq:qcfs}\) is a uniform quantizer with fixed, explicitly embedded levels. Setting \(\varphi=\tfrac12\) turns its floor into a round, \(\lfloor x + \tfrac12 \rfloor = \mathrm{round}(x)\), aligning the ANN-side discretization with the rounding convention ordinary quantization-aware training uses, a convention a neuron started from an empty membrane does not share, since it floors accumulated charge, and one that a half-threshold initial potential restores, \(\eqref{eq:fast-snn-params}\) below and OPI in Section 8.3.2. QCFS reports 93.96 percent CIFAR-10 accuracy at \(T=4\) and further results from \(T=8\) up to 512 and beyond, all in GPU simulation. Read this way, the clip-floor-shift function is an activation fake-quantizer of the kind used throughout conventional quantization-aware training.

Four later papers keep the same intervention point and vary what the trained nonlinearity looks like. Wang, Cao, Chen, Feng, and Wang's StepReLU and Jiang, Anumasa, De Masi, Xiong, and Gu's SlipReLU both replace ReLU with a piecewise nonlinearity built to remove conversion error, StepReLU a ReLU shifted by a per-layer constant set from that layer's firing threshold so that training with it adds no conversion error beyond a bias adjustment; SlipReLU generalizes the activation to a weighted-ReLU-plus-step form with a shift term, inside a unified optimization framework that balances task loss against conversion error directly, and reports a claimed one-step, \(T=1\), conversion, with further results at \(T=2\) and \(T=4\). Yang, Yang, Zhang, Dou, Shen, and Zhao's CS-QCFS gives each channel its own per-channel, softplus-shaped, guaranteed-positive threshold, reporting a \(T=1\) headline accuracy on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100. Li, Ma, and Furber's Quantization Framework for Fast Spiking Neural Networks (QFFS) makes the quantization framing explicit rather than implicit, training with a learned step size quantization (LSQ)-style quantization-aware quantizer before conversion and stating an explicit analog-coded input convention, reaching \(T=4\) on CIFAR-10 and \(T=8\) or 10 on ImageNet. All four, like QCFS itself, report GPU simulation accuracy only; none names a target chip inside an experiments section, and all five papers in this paragraph and the one above are E5.

None of the five activation-shaping papers above reports its own hardware execution. A later, unrelated paper does place one of them on physical silicon. Adaptive Fission's hardware table includes an unmodified, no-Fission QCFS configuration measured on a physical Lynxi HP201 accelerator, reporting per-epoch time and system energy from board sensors, an E1 neuromorphic result for a baseline the QCFS paper itself never deployed. The calibration paragraph below records the parallel case for SRP.

QCFS itself assumes activation mass is well matched to a uniform grid with a predetermined step size. Ramesh and Srinivasan's PASCAL and Adaptive Fission relax this assumption at different granularities, PASCAL assigning each layer its own quantization level from a statistic of that layer's activation histogram and Adaptive Fission splitting high-sensitivity neurons after training into groups with their own scales and thresholds. Two further papers relax it from a different direction, negative-valued activations. Zhang et al.'s Polarity Quantized Activation (PQA) preserves information from batch normalization that would otherwise be lost when negative values are truncated to a nonnegative grid, and PASCAL further extends QCFS to support inhibitory spikes and a layer-wise procedure that sets each layer's own quantization step size rather than one shared across the network, the same per-layer step size Section 8.3.4 later unrolls into a per-layer timestep count.

Hu et al.'s Fast-SNN closes this chain by making the correspondence between spatial and temporal quantization exact rather than qualitative. A \(b\)-bit unsigned integer representation provides \(2^b\) discrete states, and a rate-coded spiking neuron observed over \(T\) time steps emits \(N_i^l(T)\in\{0,1,\dots,T\}\) spikes, \(T+1\) discrete levels, giving the approximate correspondence \begin{equation}\label{eq:t-bits} T \approx 2^b - 1. \end{equation} Constructing an ANN with integer-valued activations is then equivalent to applying a uniform activation quantizer \begin{equation}\label{eq:spatial-quant} Q_i^l = \frac{\lambda^l}{2^b-1}\,\mathrm{clamp}\!\left(\mathrm{round}\!\left((2^b-1)\frac{z_i^l}{\lambda^l}\right),\ 0,\ 2^b-1\right), \end{equation} a form that also underlies QCFS-style activations , making explicit that low-bit ANN activations already occupy a finite, uniformly spaced set of representable levels. On the SNN side, the same firing fraction Section 4.2 already fixes, \begin{equation}\label{eq:firing-rate} \rho_i^l(T) = \frac{N_i^l(T)}{T}, \end{equation} is itself a discretized quantity, and Fast-SNN shows that, under the parameter choices \begin{equation}\label{eq:fast-snn-params} T = 2^b - 1, \qquad \theta_i^l = \lambda^l, \qquad u_i^l[0] = \theta_i^l/2, \end{equation} threshold set to the same scale Section 8.1.4 calibrates and initial membrane potential set to half of it, with a matching rescaling of the next layer's weights, the temporally quantized spike count becomes exactly equal to the spatially quantized ANN activation \(Q_i^l\) rather than merely analogous to it. Because SNN inference cost scales linearly with \(T\), reducing \(T\) is reducing activation resolution by the same equation that reducing bit-width would, and at \(T=1\) the network degenerates to a binary-activation network. Read across this subsection, the ANN's activation function has become a quantizer in its own right rather than a fixed nonlinearity conversion maps afterward.

8.3.2 Potential and Reset Calibration

A second family keeps the classical IF neuron and the rate code intact and instead changes what happens to membrane potential at the start of inference and at reset, intervening directly against the residual-and-reset error Section 8.2.3 attributes to reset-by-subtraction's carried-over charge and to an implicit zero initial condition. Bu, Ding, Yu, and Huang's Optimized Potential Initialization (OPI) sets a non-zero initial membrane potential, one half of threshold, for IF neurons under reset-by-subtraction, targeting a horizon of 32 steps or fewer with further results reported from 8 up to 512 and beyond. Hao, Bu, Ding, Huang, and Yu's SRP , a residual membrane potential method, is built directly on the QCFS activation and adds a second, calibration-only pass that reads out the residual membrane potential left after ordinary inference, reducing the reported headline to as few as 4 steps, with 8, 16, and 32-step alternatives. Both papers report GPU simulation accuracy only and are E5.

As with QCFS above, a later hardware paper places SRP's own unmodified baseline configuration on physical silicon. Adaptive Fission's hardware table includes a no-Fission SRP row measured for per-epoch time and system energy on a physical Lynxi HP201 accelerator, an E1 neuromorphic result the SRP paper itself did not report, using board sensors that closed interfaces prevent from separating into core-only figures. Read together, the QCFS and SRP rows in that table establish that rate-coded QCFS-lineage networks have reached silicon, while also confirming that neither proposing paper reported that deployment itself, a distinction this survey preserves rather than collapsing into a single verdict about the lineage.

Datta, Liu, Diffenderfer, Kailkhura, and Beerel's When SNN Meets ANN calibrates the same residual-and-reset mechanism from a different angle, a modified IF neuron built on a QCFS-style activation base that postpones firing and reset until after input-current accumulation, together with a batch-normalization bias adjustment, reporting a \(T=4\) headline on ImageNet. Its own appendix, titled Deployment of Proposed SNN on Loihi, is the clearest instance in this literature of a documented-looking route that stops short of an exercised one. The appendix reports a PyTorch-versus-Lava-DL accuracy comparison, which is E5 evidence for the proposed method, and separately asserts a Lava-DL-to-Loihi path without naming a Loihi generation, a physical board, or a device measurement, which keeps that route at E3 neuromorphic rather than a deployment claim . Section 8.4.1 returns to this paper as the negative case against which genuinely hardware-aware conversions are compared.

8.3.3 Neuron Interventions

A third family leaves the ANN's activation function and the reset rule largely alone and instead redesigns the IF neuron itself, changing the neuron-model assumption Section 3 holds fixed for the specific case of a classically converted network and intervening against Section 8.2.2's clipping and discretization error by changing what a single step's spike budget can represent. Li and Zeng's burst-spike IF neuron lifts the one-spike-per-step ceiling ordinary rate coding assumes, letting a neuron emit several unit spikes within one step when its residual post-threshold potential remains large, paired with a pooling layer the paper calls LIPooling, and reaches sub-100-step headlines with ablations extending to 32 steps and beyond; the burst-coding row in Section 4.3's Table 7 already fixes the code this neuron implements. Wang, Zhang, Chen, and Qu's Signed Neuron with Memory (SNM) permits a compensating negative spike alongside neuron-wise normalization, targeting headlines at or below 128 steps with tables extending to 1024. Signed and bidirectional variants recur elsewhere in this literature under different names, negative-spike formulations paired with a nonnegative baseline , Spiking-YOLO's channel-wise normalization and signed neuron with an imbalanced threshold for object-detection conversion, and the signed, symmetric-threshold neuron Section 8.2.4 already credits against unevenness error , each expanding the representable range beyond the single nonnegative spike count Section 8.1.1's first assumption fixes. Hu, Zheng, Jiang, and Pan's Fast-SNN converts a quantized ANN into a signed IF neuron that resets by adding the threshold on a negative spike, fine-tuned layer by layer, at \(T\) of 3, 7, or 15 across classification, detection, and segmentation workloads. Liu, Zhao, Chen, Wang, and Jiang's SpikeConverter introduces an inverse-leaky IF neuron with a temporal-separation scheme, reducing the reported headline to 16 steps. All four report GPU simulation accuracy only and are E5. Fast-SNN cites Loihi's documented support for signed spikes as design justification for its own signed-IF neuron; the paper reports no execution on that or any other chip, and the citation should be read as motivation for a design choice, not as a deployment claim.

A wider family of neuron redesigns targets the same clipping and discretization error from other angles, without each having been independently audited for this survey's evidence classes. Gao et al.'s Ca-LIF pairs a quantization-aware training framework with a calcium-gated bipolar leaky integrate-and-fire neuron. Zhang et al.'s polarity multi-spike neuron pairs an augmented IF neuron that emits multiple polarity spikes within one timestep with the Polarity Quantized Activation of Section 8.3.1, the two tied by the conditions under which the paper's conversion error vanishes. Oh and Lee prove that the IF neuron's dynamics approximate the subgradient method and replace it with a sign-gradient-descent neuron, SignGD, that approximates nonlinearities beyond ReLU. Chen et al.'s Scale-and-Fire neuron formalizes a temporal-to-spatial equivalence that replaces a multi-timestep IF neuron with a single-timestep, multi-threshold one, reporting 88.8 percent top-1 accuracy on ImageNet-1K at \(T=1\). Shen et al.'s SpikePack targets the information lost in spike transmission through a leaky neuron's temporal dependencies with a neuron of constant time and space complexity in \(T\). Chen, Song, and Sun's LAS , a loss-less ANN-SNN conversion for fully spike-driven large language models, handles extreme activation ranges with an outlier-aware threshold neuron and nonlinear operations with a hierarchically gated neuron, a domain outside the CNN scope of the rest of this subsection. None of these six names a target chip in the record this survey has read for them.

8.3.4 Mixed and Heterogeneous Timesteps

A uniform-latency SNN gives every layer the same simulation horizon \(T\), large or small, and a hetero-latency SNN gives layer \(l\) its own \(T_l\), the contrast Zhang, Sun, Li, Deng, and Yao's PseudoSNN draws. PseudoSNN's related-work paragraph sorts the ways out of one global horizon into three, a static heterogeneous latency designed or learned per layer, this subsection's subject, a dynamic sample-wise latency, Section 8.3.5's subject, and training for robustness across horizons, which Temporal Flexibility represents below. A uniform low horizon gives every layer the same budget regardless of how much resolution that layer's own activation distribution needs, the same argument mixed-precision quantization makes for a spatial bit-width. Under the correspondence \(\eqref{eq:t-bits}\) already fixes, which QAC states as the equivalence of activation bit-width and timestep count and MT-SNN as a theorem, \(T \approx 2^{n}\), a per-layer horizon is a per-layer activation bit-width, \(T_l = 2^{b_l} - 1\), so a mixed-timestep network is an activation mixed-precision quantized network read in time. The allocators that decide \(b_l\) in mixed-precision quantization carry over directly, Hessian sensitivity , activation-only per-layer search , and bit-width allocation formulated as an integer linear program from a global information-flow sensitivity score . The mixed-precision problem, its sensitivity metrics, and its allocators are surveyed for microcontroller deployment in the authors' earlier quantization survey, so a reader of both surveys sees one problem . PASCAL's per-layer sensitivity metric is exactly this allocator, run inside a QCFS pipeline rather than an ANN quantizer. One qualification comes from an MSc thesis rather than a peer-reviewed record. Steennis studies the bit-depth-to-timestep relation directly and finds that the bound \(T = 2^b - 1\) can overestimate the horizon a layer actually needs for accuracy.

Figure 30 draws the contrast, one global horizon fanned from a single node to every layer against a horizon held beside each layer gap, after PseudoSNN's Figure 1 .

Nallathambi, Sen, Raghunathan, and Chandrachoodan's layerwise disaggregated SNN (LD-SNN) is the earliest per-layer timestep allocation in this survey's record, independently optimizing the timestep count for each layer and reporting, at abstract level and ungraded here, 1.25 to 3.45 times faster inference across four hardware platforms. PASCAL , QAC , and MT-SNN take the conversion route to a per-layer \(T_l\), intervening against Section 8.2.4's unevenness and timing error. PASCAL builds a QCFS-based, sensitivity-assigned per-layer resolution onto its own precise ANN-SNN conversion (PASC) integrate-and-fire formulation, the neuron APEX later names PASC-IF, reporting an energy-weighted mean \(T_{\mathrm{eff}} = 3.14\) rather than a latency, a mean the released script reproduces only when its 17 listed per-layer horizons are silently truncated to the 10 energy coefficients its own zip() call consumes. QAC maps a learned precision to per-layer steps and reconciles adjacent layers by averaging the source layer's full time axis and replicating that average over the target layer's steps, reporting 2.76 as an unweighted mean. MT-SNN , from the same group, averages over \(T_l\) and replicates that average over \(T_{l+1}\), a real-valued tensor whenever \(T_l>1\); the withdrawn manuscript reports GPU wall-clock validation only, and the accepted abstract establishes no run at all. All three reach E5 in software; PASCAL's and APEX's RTL synthesis of the PASC-IF neuron reach E4 simulator at a uniform \(T\), and QAC's own hardware-efficiency appendix keeps its pipelined-accelerator stall warning, a downstream layer waiting on an upstream layer's longer horizon, below the E3 bar this survey applies to a documented toolchain path rather than a feasibility argument. A large-language-model branch outside this subsection's CNN scope takes the same lever, SpikeMLLM compressing per-modality timesteps from \(T = L - 1\) to \(T = \log_2 L - 1\) with a temporally compressed LIF neuron and reporting a dedicated RTL accelerator and NSLLM proposing a spike-based large-language-model framework the audited record does not detail further.

The same object is reached by the other route, training a spiking network directly rather than converting one, the branch Section 7 describes generally. Ding, Zuo, Jing, He, and Xiao's Shrinking SNN (SSNN) divides a network into stages of decreasing horizon length and trains a temporal transformer to carry a stage's integrated activity into the next stage's shorter window. Du, Wu, Deng, and Gu's Temporal Flexibility mixes a vector of per-stage horizons during training and infers at a single horizon afterward; its physical Speck2e run is timestep-free at execution, not a demonstration of a static per-layer schedule, exactly the reading Section 13.8 already gives it, an E2 neuromorphic result for that deployment of the trained weights rather than for the mixed-horizon training schedule itself. Yao, Hu, Zhou, Liu, Li, Wang, and Cheng's Adaptive Bit Allocation learns per-layer horizons jointly with weight and spike bit-widths and squeezes time where the horizon changes between layers. PseudoSNN itself learns per-layer horizons through a quantization-inspired training proxy and reshapes time between layers by averaging then broadcasting. PseudoSNN's statement that a soft-reset neuron's time-averaged output over \(T\) steps is a uniform quantization onto the \(T+1\) levels of \(\eqref{eq:rate-quantizer}\), and that its noise proxy emulates the clipping and quantization errors of spike generation, is what makes it the bridge back to the map Section 8.2 opens with.

SSNN , Adaptive Bit Allocation , and PseudoSNN report software-simulation accuracy only for their own per-layer horizon schedule and are E5 on that count. Conversion and direct training reach the same object from opposite directions. Both inherit the same unresolved problem, a per-layer temporal budget and what crosses the boundary between two layers running at different horizons.

What crosses that boundary is a design choice each method states for itself. SSNN's temporal transformer redistributes a stage's integrated activity across the next stage's steps rather than passing it unchanged. QAC's , MT-SNN's , PseudoSNN's , and Adaptive Bit Allocation's averaging step passes a real-valued quantity rather than a binary spike whenever the source horizon exceeds one. A count-then-regenerate alternative keeps the interface binary instead, spending the target horizon's steps to re-emit the count as a spike train. PASCAL's own Adaptive Layerwise mode is the conversion-route instance, accumulating a layer's spike tensor over the preceding layer's steps, correcting it with inhibitory spikes, and re-emitting it over the next layer's horizon (Algorithm 1). NeuroFlex realizes this inside one processing element at column granularity and a uniform horizon, an architectural rather than a temporal intervention Section 8.4.3 discusses in full; APEX realizes it per layer, also at a uniform horizon; and SpikingJelly 2.0 ships the same primitive as library code, SpikeCountToBinary and TemporalBinSum, for multi-step integer-valued networks, no more than its changelog entry states.

The mean \(T_l\) that QAC , MT-SNN , PseudoSNN , and Adaptive Bit Allocation report, and PASCAL's own energy-weighted mean, are layer statistics rather than durations, the distinction Section 4.1 already states for exactly these headline numbers. A mixed schedule instead costs either the sum of every per-layer horizon plus per-layer overhead when layers execute one after another, or the largest single horizon plus pipeline fill and drain when they run pipelined, the layer-by-layer and timestep-by-timestep execution classes ELSA , an elastic SNN inference architecture, distinguishes for SNN accelerators before adding its own pipelined dataflow. The Loihi 2 runtime model prices a step by the slowest of several per-step terms plus an explicit barrier term, and Koopman et al. show that a synchronization barrier between layers limits how much latency and energy an asynchronous schedule can recover. A platform's internal asynchrony does not give its layers independent time coordinates (Section 4.1), and a barrier-synchronized platform advances every layer at one global step (Section 5.6.1). Section 13.8 and Section 14.2 already find that no audited source executes a static heterogeneous per-layer schedule on physical or cycle-simulated hardware.

8.3.5 Adaptive Inference

SEENN and Dynamic Confidence intervene against the same finite-window error along a different axis than Section 8.3.4's static per-layer allocation. Rather than assigning a fixed \(T_l\) to each layer in advance, both vary a single, whole-network terminal horizon \(T_{\mathrm{stop}}(x)\) per input, stopping every layer together once a runtime criterion is satisfied, exactly the distinction Section 4.1 already establishes between per-input early exit and static per-layer allocation. Li, Geller, Kim, and Panda's SEENN offers a confidence-threshold variant, SEENN-I, and a reinforcement-learned policy variant, SEENN-II, built on a QCFS or temporal efficient training (TET) base network, reaching an average of 1.08 timesteps on CIFAR-10 for SEENN-II against full baselines of up to \(T=6\). Li, Jones, and Furber's Dynamic Confidence decodes a confidence signal from the SNN's output over time and applies a Pareto-calibrated threshold on top of a QCFS or QFFS backbone, reducing an average from baselines of \(T=4\) or \(T=14\) to roughly 1.28 to 4.71 timesteps depending on configuration. Both report GPU latency and a spike-count-based energy proxy rather than a chip measurement, and both are E5. Neither paper treats the hardware question as settled. SEENN's latency figure is explicitly measured on an NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPU, and Dynamic Confidence's own text states directly that the real power saving from running on neuromorphic hardware is unknown to its authors. Because every layer in both methods still advances on one shared running step count, a pipelined multi-core accelerator could in principle cut a uniform per-layer \(T\) short once its confidence criterion is met, without the stall failure mode QAC's appendix names for a genuinely heterogeneous per-layer schedule (Section 8.3.4); no source examined for this section demonstrates that behavior on a physical part.

8.3.6 Alternative-Code Conversion

The interventions in Sections 8.3.1 through 8.3.5 all keep Section 8.1.2's rate-matching correspondence and change what feeds it. A separate family instead changes the correspondence itself, replacing the encoder, channel, and decoder Section 4.2 defines with a different code entirely, intervening against Section 8.2.6's encoding and readout error rather than the statistics carried through a fixed rate code; Section 4.3's catalog, not repeated here, is where each code's own cost in steps, neurons, or payload width is fixed.

Lenz, Orchard, and Sheik's Quartz converts to a time-to-first-spike code using counterweight and rectifier synapses rather than the dynamic-threshold machinery earlier TTFS conversions required, and because each neuron fires at most once, the conversion needs no soft-reset mechanism at all, unlike every rate-coded method in Sections 8.3.1 through 8.3.5. Brehove, Tumpa, Kyubwa, Menon, and Narayanan convert a quantized ANN to a Sigma-Delta Neural Network built on Loihi 2's native, up to 24-bit signed graded-spike payload, transmitting activation deltas rather than a spike count. Jiang, Chen, Li, Liu, Gao, Zhang, and Fang's Adaptive Fission applies a population-coding transformation after training, on top of an already converted or directly trained network, trading additional temporal integration for a group of binary sub-neurons carrying unequal fixed weights, which Section 4.3 records as a spatial axis orthogonal to rate and temporal coding rather than a fifth primitive. Read as a design pattern rather than a specific mechanism, this split is a form of resolution reallocation; instead of raising \(T\) globally, it raises effective representational capacity only where conversion error concentrates. All three abandon rate coding at the point of conversion itself, rather than adjusting the activation function, the neuron, or the timestep schedule around a rate code, which distinguishes them structurally from every method in Sections 8.3.1 through 8.3.5. Section 8.4.1 records the hardware evidence for all three together.

Three further encoding-level methods pursue the same lever without the physical-silicon evidence Section 8.4.1 records for the three above. Huang et al.'s Differential Coding with Graded Units and Spiking Neurons (DCGS) transmits changes in rate information rather than rates directly, aiming to preserve content with fewer effective steps than rate coding needs. Yang et al.'s NeuBridge builds a decode-encode neuron into the neuron model, paired with adaptive temporal coding, separating the encoding and decoding stages to remove the sequential error that uneven spike sequences cause, rather than assuming a fixed rate-coding interface suffices for every layer and distribution. Hwang and Kung's One-Spike SNN carries a value through a single spike's arrival phase rather than through a spike count, using base manipulation to reduce ANN-to-SNN conversion loss under this phase code.

Rank order coding is the code family for which no conversion theorem exists among the sources reviewed for this survey, for the reason Section 4.3 develops in full. Every conversion proof surveyed matches a per-layer magnitude, and an ordinal object drawn from \(N!\) permutations has no natural target for that convergence argument. BrainChip's Akida carries a genuine rank-order-coding lineage at its input-encoding stage, but the standard QuantizeML-to-CNN2SNN route nearly all published Akida results use collapses to a single-pass, order-agnostic threshold comparison inside hidden layers . The collapse is total rather than partial. There is no user-exposed multi-timestep simulation horizon, so the mapped network evaluates in a single feedforward pass; no membrane potential carries across timesteps; and there is no reset by subtraction, only a static threshold computed once at conversion time. Rate coding of an activation as a spike count over a horizon \(T\) is replaced outright by a step-wise quantized ReLU evaluated once, a substitution two independent physical-hardware studies confirm in different words, Ziegler et al. writing that the chip squashes the rate-code approximation of the ReLU into one time step and Lunghi et al. that the converted model reduces the computation to a single time step, and a reading of the toolkit's own source code, which this survey records but has not independently verified, supports the same conclusion . That converted route is therefore neither a new rate-coded low-timestep conversion algorithm nor an order-sensitive one, and this survey does not count it toward either family's evidence base. Section 11.4 traces this same transformation as a named deployment route, with its pipeline mechanics and its Akida-generation-specific route state. MorphIC's same-chip, same-task comparison of rate and rank-order coding, and the resulting tenfold measured energy difference at a 1.9-percentage-point accuracy cost, is developed fully in Section 4.3 and is not repeated here.

8.4 Conversion Toward Hardware

The methods of Section 8.3 change what a conversion produces without naming where it will run. The three families here start from the target. Hardware-aware conversion maps a network onto a specific platform, and its subsection is where this survey records which conversion algorithms have been measured on physical silicon. Hybrid fine-tuning corrects the converted network after conversion, statistically or across the branch boundary Section 6 draws. Emerging architectures partition one network between artificial and spiking forms rather than converting it whole.

8.4.1 Hardware-Aware Conversion

Through the falsifier search this survey conducted into 16 September 2026, no audited source both introduces a recent, low-timestep, spike-count or firing-rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion algorithm and reports that same proposed algorithm measured on named physical neuromorphic silicon . This is a bounded search result over a stated population of paper-level conversion algorithms; it is not a claim that rate-coded conversion cannot reach silicon, and it says nothing about direct training, classical conversion, or later application papers that reuse an existing converter. Every confirmed instance in the audited corpus of a paper that both proposes a conversion method and reports its own physical-silicon measurement abandons rate coding for a code native to the target platform.

Quartz's own comparison is against the published rate-coded NxTF and SNN Toolbox numbers on the same platform rather than a re-run on the same chip; Quartz measured on a Nahuku 32 Loihi 1 board with NxSDK 1.0.0, while the baseline had been measured by other authors on a different board with NxSDK 0.9.5 and on a different, larger network, and the released Quartz code targets nxSDK 0.9.8, an earlier version than the paper's measurements state. The paper reports 0.77 percent MNIST error against a 0.73 percent ANN baseline, and separately a considerably higher 25.14 percent CIFAR-10 error than the rate-coded NxTF result of 8.52 percent on the same dataset, which the paper itself frames as an energy-accuracy trade rather than an unconditional improvement; its preferred CIFAR-10 energy-delay product substitutes an estimated static power for the measured one . Brehove et al. measure their conversion on an in-house 16-chip Loihi 2 VPX board running nxkernel 0.3.0 with modified microcode, reporting chip-probe latency, throughput, power, energy per frame, and energy-delay product (EDP) against a Jetson Xavier baseline whose whole system-on-chip (SoC) power is not boundary-identical to Loihi's chip-side measurement. Adaptive Fission's population-coding conversion is deployed and measured on a physical Lynxi HP201 accelerator, reporting on-chip memory, per-epoch time, and system energy from board sensors that closed interfaces prevent from being separated into core-only figures. Andrei, Drăguțoiu, Béna, Akl, Li, Lohrmann, Mönich, and Boche extend Few-Spikes conversion to complex-valued layers for an unfolded multidimensional harmonic-retrieval network and map the result to a physical single-chip SpiNNaker2 board, reporting board power against a Jetson Xavier baseline; the underlying representation is a serialized temporal Few-Spikes code, not a spike-count rate estimate. All four reach E1 neuromorphic.

APEX and NeuroFlex , discussed for their temporal and architectural contributions in Sections 8.3.4 and 8.4.3, synthesize RTL for the PASC-IF neuron rather than fabricating silicon, an E4 simulator hardware model rather than a measured chip in either case. Datta et al.'s asserted Lava-DL-to-Loihi route, by contrast, names no board, generation, or measurement and remains E3 neuromorphic, the clearest example in this section of a documented-looking route that stops short of an exercised one. Read across this section, a hardware-aware conversion's evidence class tracks how specifically it names a generation, a toolchain version, and a measurement boundary, not how confidently its introduction or appendix invokes a chip's name.

8.4.2 Hybrid Fine-Tuning

A third strategy corrects a network's activation statistics after conversion, calibrating against Section 8.2.3's residual and reset error and Section 8.2.2's clipping and discretization error without altering the underlying correspondence theorem. Li, Deng, Dong, Gong, and Gu's calibration method adjusts bias, weight, and initial-membrane-potential statistics on a network already converted at up to 256 steps, explicitly keeping the first layer's input continuous rather than binary because generating binary spikes there degrades accuracy, and computes its energy from the per-operation constants of Rathi and Roy's DIET-SNN rather than from a measured value. Hao, Ding, Bu, Huang, and Yu's Calibrating Offset Spikes (COS) iteratively detects and corrects the offset between an SNN's spike output and the value its source ANN activation would produce, reaching a headline six-step configuration, four calibration steps plus two inference steps. Wang, Fang, Cao, Ren, and Xu's Adaptive Calibration (AdaFire) introduces a training-free calibration procedure paired with an adaptive-firing burst neuron, reaching a \(T=8\) headline against \(T=32\) competitors at equal energy, with its energy figures again computed from an appendix-stated theoretical method rather than measured. All three report GPU simulation only and are E5; where a chip is named at all in these three papers, it is cited as motivation for a design or calibration choice, never as an executed deployment.

A second, structurally different hybrid pattern fine-tunes across the branch boundary itself rather than statistically. One record in this survey's route index describes a hybrid SNN-ANN network, conventional PyTorch or TensorFlow layers alongside SLAYER- or Lava-trained spiking layers, mapped through NengoLoihi to physical Loihi 1 hardware, with power, core count, and spike-propagation-delay latency measured on-chip . This record is retrieved from structured metadata rather than the paper's full text and carries a provisional verification status in this survey's source registry; its reported E1 neuromorphic measurement should be read with that limitation attached until the underlying paper is independently checked against its full text. Section 8.4.3 returns to the architecture this method builds, a network that is neither wholly ANN nor wholly SNN.

8.4.3 Emerging Architectures

Every method in Sections 8.3.1 through 8.4.2 keeps one assumption of Section 8.1.5's architecture preparation intact, that a network is prepared once, as a whole, into a wholly artificial or a wholly spiking form before it runs. Two records in the audited corpus intervene on that partition itself, executing part of a network as an ANN and part as an SNN within a single inference, which targets Section 8.2.5's architecture-dependent error at the level of the network's structure rather than at a layer's activation, neuron, or timestep count. Manjunath, Ramesh, and Srinivasan's NeuroFlex extends PASCAL's PASC neuron and its underlying integer-exact equivalence proof from a layer-wide granularity to a column-level one, in which individual output columns within a single layer independently execute in ANN mode or PASC-IF SNN mode, chosen offline by a profiling-driven scheduler tied to each column's own QCFS quantization level. Because the equivalence proof it inherits depends only on local, per-neuron integer arithmetic, the paper argues it survives being applied column by column rather than layer by layer, and reports 98.6 to 99.3 percent processing-element-array utilization on VGG-16, ResNet-34, GoogLeNet, and BERT against 71.0 to 83.9 percent for coarser, layer-wise hybrid scheduling, synthesized in RTL at 560 MHz in a 40-nanometer library rather than fabricated, an E4 simulator result .

The hybrid SNN-ANN network introduced in Section 8.4.2 makes the same kind of partition at a coarser, layer-wide granularity instead of a column-wide one, and is the one record in this group reporting execution on physical rather than synthesized hardware, subject to the provisional status Section 8.4.2 already attaches to it . Both records are early enough, and few enough, that this survey states their existence as a design direction rather than as a demonstrated deployment pattern. Section 14 returns to algorithm-hardware co-design as an open research problem, and the column-level and layer-level granularities distinguished here are exactly the design axis that discussion resumes.
