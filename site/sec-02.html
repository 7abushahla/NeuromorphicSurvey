2 Computation and Representation

A trained model, a simulator, and a physical target must agree on what a number means before any claim about their equivalence is meaningful. This section defines the objects that recur through the rest of the survey, a static value, a persistent state, a discrete event, and a temporal signal. It separates dense computation from sparse activity and conditional execution, distinguishes the several uses of the words neuromorphic and event-driven, and explains why a representation choice constrains which execution strategies remain available downstream. It does not yet compare platforms. It fixes the vocabulary that Sections 3 through 13 depend on.

2.1 One Stack, Many Vocabularies

The vocabulary fixed in this section descends from one dependency chain, a biological mechanism, a biophysical description of that mechanism, a reduced dynamical model, an electrical circuit, an event representation the circuit can carry, a learning rule, a finite-precision computation, a system architecture, and a silicon implementation. Read this way, the chain states a dependency order, not a historical timeline. Later stages depend on the vocabulary the earlier stages fix, but the branches that produced them developed on separate tracks for decades and rejoined more than once rather than following one continuous line of descent. Surveys of the resulting model lineage converge on three principles that recur at every stage regardless of when that stage appeared, coding by spikes, computation driven by events, and a claim of biological plausibility . This subsection traces the branches that carry those principles into the spiking neural network, and the table below states which cross-layer readings of them this survey refuses.

The object at the root of every vocabulary is the biological neuron, and it is drawn once here in the neuroscience vocabulary alone, before any lineage renames it. Gerstner et al. divide the cell into three functional parts, dendrites that collect input from other neurons, a soma that integrates it, and an axon that carries the output to other cells. The junction between an axon terminal and a dendrite or soma is the synapse. At a chemical synapse an arriving action potential releases transmitter into the synaptic cleft, receptors on the postsynaptic membrane open ion channels, and the postsynaptic membrane potential moves away from its resting level of about −65 mV, upward at an excitatory synapse and downward at an inhibitory one. These postsynaptic potentials sum. When the summed potential at the soma crosses a threshold, the cell emits an action potential, a pulse of about 100 mV, typically lasting one to two milliseconds, that travels along the axon without changing shape, and an absolute and then a relative refractory period limit how soon the next one can follow. Figure 2 draws this cell and its membrane potential. Every later stage of the stack keeps some of these parts and discards the rest. The physical-behavior lineage keeps the membrane, its threshold, and its pulse. The computational lineage keeps the many weighted inputs, the summation, the threshold, and the single output fanned out to other units. The table that closes this subsection records where a later vocabulary keeps a word without keeping the part.

From the biological neuron, two questions fork. The question of how the neuron physically behaves runs through Lapicque's 1907 electrical model to integrate-and-fire and leaky integrate-and-fire, and separately through the Hodgkin-Huxley ionic description to the reduced dynamical models built on it, an imposed threshold on one path standing in for a mechanism that on the other emerges from the equations themselves. The question of what a neuron-like unit can compute runs through McCulloch and Pitts's logical neuron and the perceptron to the artificial neural network. The spiking neural network is where these two branches rejoin, an imposed-threshold unit computing inside a network architecture descended from the other branch. Section 6, The Two Branches, draws the same fork from the learning side. Direct training descends from the dynamics lineage, and ANN-to-SNN conversion descends from the ANN lineage.

Two corrections follow directly from the dates involved. Lapicque's 1907 electrical excitability model precedes the Hodgkin-Huxley description of 1952 , so integrate-and-fire is an older, independent electrical abstraction of the neuron rather than a later simplification of the ionic model, and its imposed threshold is not a rougher version of a mechanism that Hodgkin and Huxley discovered afterward. Adrian's 1926 observation relating stimulus strength to impulse frequency precedes Shannon's 1948 theory of communication by more than two decades, so rate coding is first an experimental description of a nerve fiber's firing and only later, and separately, an information-theoretic construct. Section 4.2, Encoder, Channel, and Decoder, states what this survey borrows from the second reading and what it refuses.

Mead's 1990 paper turns the question again . Simulating a neuron in software is no longer the goal; the question becomes whether hardware whose own physics or organization can implement the computation, rather than represent it numerically, can be built. This is where the word neuromorphic enters the literature and where this survey's deployment question begins . The silicon neuron of 1991 carried that question from equation to circuit, and the dynamic vision sensor of 2008 carried it to the sensor itself, event-native transduction before any spike reaches a network. Communication among the resulting circuits, address-event routing between cores and chips and the on-chip memory that holds synaptic state, is as much of the neuromorphic engineering problem as the neuron equation itself; Section 5, Where an SNN Runs, compares the platform families built on top of it.

The same words, neuron, spike, code, and neuromorphic itself, recur at every layer of this stack, and a reading correct at one layer is not automatically correct at the layer above or below it. Table 3 lists the equivalences this survey refuses to draw across those layers, each attached to the subsection elsewhere in the survey that enforces the refusal. A parallel refusal at the level of measurement, a reduced step count against reduced latency, a reduced spike count against a reduced operation count, a documented exporter against an exercised deployment route, is enforced separately in Section 4.1, Time Vocabulary, and Section 12.2, Measurement Boundaries, and is not repeated here.

The conventional column is the physical substrate every ANN and CNN runs on today. The transistor of 1948 is a switch, on or off. The logic families built from that switch, transistor-transistor logic and then the complementary MOS logic that Wanlass and Sah reported in 1963 , draw power chiefly when a gate switches . The organization those gates were made to realize is older than the transistor. Von Neumann's 1945 report on the EDVAC separates an arithmetic organ, a control organ, and a memory, keeps the orders and the numbers in that one memory, and gates every element with a central clock, and Backus's 1978 lecture named the word-at-a-time traffic between processor and memory the von Neumann bottleneck. The microprocessor of 1971 put the whole machine on one chip , the reduced instruction set computer of 1980 argued that a very simple instruction set can be very fast, RISC-V made such a set open in 2011 , and the GPU and the neural processing unit, of which the TPU's systolic array of 2017 is the documented example , are arrays of clocked multiply-accumulate units fed from that same memory. On this column an ANN is a program, a weight is a word in memory, a multiply-accumulate is an instruction, and time is the clock.

The neuromorphic column mirrors the hardware branch of Figure 3 level by level. Mead's 1989 book runs the same transistor below threshold, as an analog element whose physics mirrors the neuron's, and his 1990 paper states the program, implement the computation rather than simulate it. The silicon neuron of 1991 is a circuit whose dynamics are the neuron's. The address-event representation, defined in Mahowald's 1992 thesis and carried between chips by Boahen in 2000 , sends each spike as a neuron's address on a shared bus, with time representing itself and no clock. TrueNorth in 2014 and Loihi in 2018 build the same organization from asynchronous digital cores with synapse memory beside the neuron, BrainScaleS-2 runs physical neuron circuits faster than biology , and SpiNNaker is the first crossing between the columns, conventional ARM cores inside a neuromorphic packet router. On this column an SNN is the circuit's dynamics, a weight is synapse state beside the neuron, a spike is an event, and time is physics, or the event itself. The second crossing, drawn dashed, is what a simulator does. A simulator runs the right column's equations on the left column's machine, so a spiking network stepped on a CPU or a GPU is a program again, its state in memory and its time the clock, whatever model it steps. Section 5, Where an SNN Runs, makes the three targets that follow from this picture explicit; Section 10.2, Simulator and Library Catalog, catalogs the software that reaches each. Section 2.5, Why Representation Constrains Execution, follows both paths from the trained network down to the part.

2.2 Values, State, and Events

This survey considers the feedforward ANN inference setting, in which a trained network is evaluated layer by layer on a fixed input. In that setting, an ANN unit produces its output by a weighted sum of its inputs followed by a static nonlinearity, evaluated once for each input presentation. The result, denoted \(a^l\) for a unit in layer \(l\), is a real-valued number. It is a value in the strict sense used throughout this survey. Within one evaluation it depends only on the current input and carries no memory of a previous evaluation; recurrent and state-space architectures, where a unit's output also depends on state carried forward from a previous evaluation, fall outside this setting and outside the scope of this survey. An ANN activation is a static quantity within one evaluation, fully determined by the current input rather than updated from its own previous value. It is a complete description of that unit's output at that input, not a reduced or impoverished version of something else.

A spiking unit instead emits a discrete event. The plainest case is a bare binary spike, denoted \(z_i^l[t]\in\{0,1\}\) for unit \(i\) in layer \(l\) at discrete algorithmic step \(t\). At a known source and step it either occurs or it does not, and that occurrence is all the event reports. A deployed event is not always this bare. It may additionally carry a source or destination address, a timestamp, a polarity, an event type, or an integer or graded payload \(p_{ik}\), depending on the sensor, protocol, or neuron model that produces it. Address-event representation (AER) is the packet convention in which the digital platforms audited in this survey move such events between cores and chips, encoding the source address into the packet itself so that arrival order and timing, not a dedicated wire, identify which unit fired . This survey's \(z_i^l[t]\) notation covers the binary case; payload-carrying events are introduced with the signed and graded neuron models of Section 3, Neuron and Synapse Dynamics, and with the coding schemes of Section 4, Temporal Semantics, Neural Codes, and Decoding, where a code or a platform needs them. A spiking unit also typically carries an internal state \(u_i^l[t]\), the membrane potential, that persists from step to step and is written and later read rather than recomputed independently each time. \(t\) indexes a discrete algorithmic step here, not a duration of physical time. A physical or modeled duration is instead denoted \(\tau\), and the two are not interchangeable until a simulation-to-hardware mapping states how a step corresponds to a duration. On its own, a bare binary event carries almost no information. It either occurred at that step or it did not. What carries information is the relationship between events across steps, their count, their timing, or the pattern they form, together with the state that produced them, or, once a payload is declared, the payload itself.

In this survey, event is the general term, whereas binary spike means the special event \(z_i^l[t]\in\{0,1\}\). This distinction prevents a convenient rate-coded abstraction from becoming an unstated assumption about every sensor interface, interconnect protocol, or target execution model.

Three objects therefore need to stay distinct. A value is a quantity that depends only on the current input and needs no notion of time to be defined, such as \(a^l\). A state is a quantity that persists, is updated using its own previous value, and can be read at a later step, such as \(u_i^l[t]\). An event is a discrete occurrence tied to a specific step or time, binary at minimum and optionally carrying the address, timing, or payload fields introduced above, such as \(z_i^l[t]\). A temporal signal is any quantity indexed by \(t\) or \(\tau\), which includes a spike train, a decaying state trajectory, and a time-varying input current, whether or not any individual sample of it carries a large payload; a sample of such a signal can itself be a real-valued quantity, so carrying a temporal index is not what separates a value from a state. The distinction that matters instead is between a static activation, fully determined within one evaluation and not updated from a previous value, and a persistent state, whose current value is computed from its own previous value and so must be written and read again across evaluations or steps. In this sense \(a^l\) is static and \(u_i^l[t]\) is persistent. A state and an event both carry a temporal index, but a state is read continuously across steps while an event is a punctual occurrence at one of them.

Figure 5 sets the ANN unit and the SNN unit side by side against the biological neuron both abstractions simplify, so that what each one retains, in what form, and what it discards can be compared property by property rather than asserted in prose alone.

A network becomes spiking when this stateful event transformation is composed across layers. At each layer, incoming events are routed through fixed synaptic weights to update postsynaptic state. Threshold crossings generate the events delivered to the next layer. The network therefore communicates a temporally distributed representation, while each neuron retains local state between event arrivals or algorithmic steps. The binary case shown here is the reference abstraction used for rate-coded conversion. Later sections extend it to payload-carrying events, alternative codes, and target-specific execution models . 2.3 Dense Computation, Sparse Activity, and Conditional Execution

In a feedforward ANN evaluation, every unit at every layer produces an activation value on every input presentation, and the tensor that holds those activations is dense in the representational sense. It logically represents every position, including one whose value happens to equal zero. Layer-to-layer computation structured as a matrix multiplication touches every incoming weight and every position of that tensor by default. Whether the arithmetic engine that executes this matrix multiplication actually performs a multiply-accumulate (MAC) operation for a position that evaluates to zero, or recognizes the zero and skips it, is a separate question of execution. A dense representation is compatible with either choice, a fixed schedule that performs the operation everywhere or a compiler and hardware combination that inspects the tensor and conditionally skips a known zero.

Figure 7 compares the communicated object in the restricted feedforward-inference setting used throughout this survey. An ANN layer receives a real-valued activation vector and computes a weighted aggregation followed by a static nonlinearity once for an input presentation. A binary-event SNN layer receives event occurrences over steps, conditionally applies the fixed synaptic weights associated with those occurrences, updates persistent postsynaptic state, and emits events after thresholding and reset. This is a comparison of computational representations. It is not yet a claim about the physical operations, scheduling policy, or energy of a particular target .

The absence of an event and the numerical value zero are different representational facts. In a dense ANN tensor, every logical position is present, including positions whose value is zero. A sparse event representation communicates selected occurrences and omits silent positions. Omission can make conditional routing or accumulation available, but it does not establish that a runtime skips work. In particular, absence is interpretable only relative to a declared step, time window, or completion rule. An asynchronous stream does not encode an absence as a packet.

In a network of spiking units, the binary event \(z_i^l[t]\) defined above is either present or absent at a given source and step under the discrete-time model fixed here. A sparse representation, unlike the dense tensor above, explicitly stores or communicates only the selected nonzero positions rather than every position by default. When a substantial fraction of a layer's events are absent within a finite horizon, an activity pattern this survey calls sparse, that sparse representation carries less to store or transmit, and the computation associated with a silent unit can in principle be skipped rather than performed and discarded. Communication can be sparse in the same sense. A layer then transmits only its nonzero events rather than a dense vector covering every unit, so the amount transmitted depends on how many units fired rather than on how many units exist .

This distinction describes what a representation makes available, not what a given implementation does with it, and it applies symmetrically to both representations introduced above. Either a dense tensor or a sparse representation can be executed densely, with every position or every possible event slot processed uniformly, or with conditional skipping, an execution style this survey calls conditional, in which absent activity is recognized and the corresponding work is not performed. Which of the two a given implementation adopts is a hardware and compiler decision separate from the network's activity pattern. An event representation omits silent positions from communication. A receiver can treat that omission as inactivity only under a declared step, window, timeout, or barrier. This can make conditional communication and conditional accumulation a natural strategy to adopt, but it does not by itself guarantee that a given execution substrate adopts it. A high event rate, close to one event per unit per step, leaves sparse activity with little advantage over dense computation, and a network may require many steps before enough silence accumulates for skipping to matter at all.

Consequently, fewer spikes and fewer hardware operations are not the same claim. The second requires an execution substrate that treats an absent event as work to be skipped rather than work to be padded, and only a measurement on that substrate establishes that it did so, not the event count alone. Conditional execution names the substrate behavior that would make the skip real. Which platform families realize conditional execution natively and which emulate it with a dense pipeline underneath is compared once execution models are introduced, in the section on hardware execution models.

2.4 The Meanings of Neuromorphic and Event-Driven

The words neuromorphic and event-driven are applied to at least four different objects in this literature, and treating them as one property is a frequent source of overclaiming. A sensor is neuromorphic or event-driven when it asynchronously reports a change in its input as a discrete, timestamped event rather than sampling every input on a fixed clock. An event camera's output stream is event-native from the moment of acquisition in exactly this sense . An algorithm or network model is spiking, and often called neuromorphic, when it is specified in terms of the discrete events and persistent state defined above, independent of what substrate eventually executes it. A network simulated step by step on a conventional processor is a spiking algorithm in this sense whether or not any part of that simulation is triggered by an event. An execution model is event-driven when a core or process advances only when an event demands it, rather than on every tick of a shared clock. This is a property of how computation proceeds, not of how the algorithm was specified. A fourth sense applies to a complete system rather than to one stage. A system is an end-to-end event-driven system when an event-driven sensor, a spiking algorithm, and an event-driven execution substrate are paired throughout, with no stage reintroducing dense, clocked computation. This fourth sense is the conjunction of the previous three holding simultaneously across every stage, not a stronger or purer version of any single one of them, and it is also the least common of the four to hold in practice.

These four senses do not transfer into one another automatically. An event-native sensor stream does not establish that the algorithm consuming it is spiking, since a frame can be reconstructed from an event stream and handed to a conventional network. A spiking algorithm does not establish that its execution is event-driven end to end, since a simulator can recompute every unit's state at every step regardless of which events are present or absent. This is the specific, terminological form of a non-equivalence that recurs throughout the survey. Event-native input does not prove event-driven execution throughout a system. Establishing which stage of a given pipeline satisfies which of the four senses, and on which named platform, is deferred to the sections that introduce execution models and traced deployment routes. No platform claim is made here.

2.5 Why Representation Constrains Execution

The representation chosen at one point of the model-to-device chain restricts which execution strategies remain faithful to it at the next, though it does not by itself pick one. A dense, real-valued activation \(a^l\) is represented the same way whether its value is far from zero or exactly zero, so the dense layout does not omit that position structurally. Exploiting a zero therefore requires inspection, metadata, or a sparse transformation, and remains an execution decision. A value close to zero has the same representational status as any other value. Executing a layer of such units efficiently therefore favors, by default, an engine built around dense multiply-accumulate arithmetic that processes every unit uniformly. As in the discussion above, a compiler and hardware combination that inspects the tensor for zero-valued positions and skips the corresponding operation remains a separate execution choice that the dense representation neither forces nor forbids.

A binary event \(z_i^l[t]\) is a discrete occurrence rather than a magnitude to multiply. For a binary unit-payload event and a fixed synaptic weight, processing an arriving event can reduce to adding or subtracting that weight into postsynaptic state. Payload-carrying events, programmable transforms, and some hardware encodings can require multiplication or an equivalent scaling operation. The binary fixed-weight case is accumulated into the postsynaptic state only when the event fires . An event-based representation therefore admits an accumulate-only execution strategy that a dense, value-based representation does not offer as a comparable option, because a real-valued activation cannot degenerate to a data-dependent addition in the same way. Its magnitude is exactly the information that a spike's mere presence does not carry.

That an accumulate-only strategy is representationally admissible does not mean any particular hardware realizes it, and does not mean realizing it is free. Maintaining per-unit persistent state, routing events or advancing timestep state, and reading out a decision after a variable number of steps all add engineering cost that a fixed-schedule, dense pipeline avoids by construction. A representation that relaxes one constraint typically introduces another. State must be stored somewhere, and event timing must be tracked somewhere, even when no multiplication is required.

Figure 8 draws the two strategies as today's silicon realizes them, level by level from the trained network to the part. On the conventional path a trained ANN or CNN is quantized and compiled to integer kernels, on a Cortex-M microcontroller through the CMSIS-NN kernels of Lai et al. and on a CPU, GPU, or NPU through the vendor's libraries, and it runs as dense matrix multiplies under a global clock, one pass per input frame, with weights and activations fetched through a memory hierarchy whose accesses cost more energy than the arithmetic they feed, as Horowitz tabulated. On the neuromorphic path an SNN runs as event-triggered state updates in cores that hold their synaptic weights beside the neuron, with events carried between cores by address and with time either stepped by the fabric or driven by the events themselves. The picture is not a partition. A trained ANN reaches the neuromorphic path only through conversion, the subject of Section 8, and an SNN also runs, simulated or accelerated, on conventional silicon, which Du et al. benchmark on edge devices and Farsa et al. survey for GPUs and RISC-V processors. What differs between the two columns is where the weight lives, what moves between units, and when a unit computes, and the sections on hardware execution and deployment measurement turn each of those differences into a stated field of the deployment claim. Section 5.1, Three Targets, One Network, compares the three deployment paths, target by target, against the evidence ceiling each can reach.

The consequence carried forward is only that representation sets the space of physically consistent execution strategies available to a target. It does not by itself select one, and it does not establish that a specific chip, compiler, or runtime exploits the option a representation makes available. Which execution families exist, and which of their capabilities are native, transformed, emulated, host-assisted, unsupported, or undocumented for a given representation, is the comparison made in the section on hardware execution models. Which complete route a published deployment actually exercised is established only in the section that traces named deployment routes edge by edge.

2.6 Where This Survey Enters the Stack

From biology and biophysics, this survey takes the electrical vocabulary, capacitance, conductance, and membrane potential, and the spike as a discrete event rather than a continuous signal. It stops at ion-channel kinetics and the shape of the action-potential waveform. For that mechanism, see Zheng et al. , particularly useful for tracing the specific biological detail an integrate-and-fire abstraction discards, and Gerstner et al. , particularly useful for the fuller dynamical-systems treatment of the neuron models this survey uses only in reduced form.

From sensing, this survey takes event-native input semantics and the first-layer event convention that a spiking network consumes. It stops at the transduction physics that produces those events. For that physics, see Tayarani-Najaran et al. , particularly useful for comparing event-based transduction across visual, auditory, and olfactory sensing modalities, and Gallego et al. , particularly useful for the event camera's own sensing mechanism and output semantics.

From devices, this survey takes the distinction among model, implementation, and device technology, and the native, transformed, and emulated capability statuses built on it. It stops at device physics. For that physics, see Zolfagharinejad et al. , particularly useful for connecting emerging device behavior back to the same representational choices introduced here. Execution families and the mechanisms that realize them are not bounded out of this survey in the same way; Section 5, Where an SNN Runs, compares them platform by platform.

The next section opens with the membrane equation that vocabulary comes from (Section 3.1, From Membrane to Circuit to Model,) and then turns the persistent state and discrete events introduced here into one common, staged state-update rule (Section 3.2). The integrate-and-fire, leaky integrate-and-fire, current-based, adaptive, and multi-compartment models used throughout the remaining sections are then derived as variations of that single update rather than as an unrelated list of neuron types.
