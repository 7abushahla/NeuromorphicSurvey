10 Software, Interchange, and Compilation

Section 9.4 established that a contract field's representability must be checked before its execution is asked about at all. This section checks it, following the software path a network travels in the order it is actually traveled, a training or simulation framework, serialization or export, an intermediate representation where one is used, compiler lowering and mapping, a runtime, and the host input and output work that surrounds every stage. Every edge in that path is typed using the schema Table 25 fixes below, input, output, preserved, transformed, rejected, and evidence, so that a claim about one boundary is never confused with a claim about a different one.

10.1 The Framework Boundary

The sixteen training and simulation frameworks this survey's audited evidence covers do not form one category. They divide by what representation they commit to at the point a network is defined and by which hardware family, if any, their own maintainers built them to reach. Grouping by this commitment, rather than listing sixteen names in alphabetical or chronological order, is what lets the rest of this section state which boundary a given framework's output must cross next.

A first group trains a network natively in PyTorch and commits to a spiking representation from the first line of model code. SpikingJelly, snnTorch, and Norse all expose PyTorch-native spiking layers trained by the surrogate-gradient or exact spike-time methods of Section 7, and all three differ in their default reset rule at the point of definition, exactly the divergence Section 3.5 already traces for SpikingJelly. snnTorch's snn.Leaky defaults to a subtractive reset, matching SpikingJelly's own converted-network default, while Norse's LIFCell and LIFRecurrentCell default to reset-to-zero . Neither snnTorch nor Norse has a first-party path to a specific physical target audited in this survey; Norse instead exports to NIR, with its own documentation stating a caveat that a round-trip export and import can produce a different spike pattern on identical input, a framework-level instance of the representation risk this section develops in full . This group's frameworks are therefore evaluated by this survey chiefly as sources of the reset-semantics divergence itself, not as members of an audited hardware route.

A second group descends from biological point-neuron simulation rather than from a PyTorch training loop. Brian2 and NEST are differential-equation and point-neuron simulators whose native application is large-scale biological modeling rather than deep-network conversion; each reaches a converted convolutional network only through SNN Toolbox acting as an external output backend, not through its own native import path . Nengo instead implements the Neural Engineering Framework, analytically solving decoding weights over spiking tuning curves rather than training by backpropagated error, a construction distinct enough from the rate-matching correspondence of Section 8.2 that its NengoDL bridge and ReLU-to-spiking converter should not be read as an instance of the same theorem . PyNN sits above several of these simulators as a shared front end, letting one described model target NEST, BrainScaleS-2, or SpiNNaker without being rewritten per backend, though sPyNNaker, PyNN's own SpiNNaker-specific implementation, is the concrete backend this survey's audited routes actually exercise rather than the generic PyNN interface itself .

A third group is a vendor toolchain built around one hardware family from the outset. Lava and Lava-DL target Loihi 2, Sinabs targets SynSense's DYNAP-CNN and Speck family, Rockpool targets SynSense's Xylo family, QuantizeML targets BrainChip's Akida family, hxtorch targets BrainScaleS-2, and BIDL targets Lynxi's KA200 lineage . Because each of these frameworks is co-designed with its one target, the framework-boundary edge for this group is typically the smallest source of divergence in the entire chain; Section 10.6 shows the larger transformations for this group happen one or two edges downstream, at the mapper or the runtime rather than at model definition. mlGeNN occupies a narrower version of the conversion-specific role SNN Toolbox occupies more broadly, adding explicit deep-CNN conversion support to the GPU code-generation library GeNN and reporting large-scale conversion results at ImageNet scale, but as GPU simulation only, without a documented route to neuromorphic silicon audited in this survey .

SNN Toolbox is the fourth group's sole confirmed member in this survey's audited evidence, a conversion-specific tool whose entire purpose is producing a rate-coded, reset-by-subtraction IF network from a Keras, PyTorch, Caffe, or Lasagne source model and routing that network toward one of several output backends, NxTF, sPyNNaker, Brian2, or Cerebron among them . Section 11.2 traces two of these output paths to physical silicon in full.

The six brain simulators exist to study biologically detailed neuron and synapse dynamics at network scale, not to deploy a trained model to a target. NEST integrates a library of over 100 neuron and synapse models on ordinary CPUs and CPU clusters, NEURON runs neurons and networks of neurons on a local machine, in the cloud, or on an HPC system without naming its model classes on the page read, and neither states GPU support or a path onto neuromorphic silicon. Brian2 takes the same CPU-bound approach but compiles differential equations specified in strings into generated C++, reaching a GPU only through the separate Brian2GeNN and Brian2CUDA projects rather than through Brian2 itself. GeNN and mlGeNN invert that dependency, generating code that runs natively on NVIDIA GPUs and positioning that speed as an alternative to neuromorphic hardware rather than a route onto it. CARLsim keeps Izhikevich-level biological detail while adding CUDA acceleration on top of it. What separates a run through any of these six from a deployment claim is the fact NEST's own documentation states plainly and that holds for the group as a whole, the output is a numerical simulation of network dynamics, not hardware execution.

The twelve deep-SNN libraries target the other end of the same problem, training or running a network at the scale a convolutional or transformer architecture requires, inside a PyTorch- or TensorFlow-like workflow rather than a biological point-neuron model. SpikingJelly, snnTorch, and Norse share this shape closely, a PyTorch-native neuron library plus, for SpikingJelly, its own ANN-to-SNN conversion tools; each documents an NIR export path toward another tool without a hardware target named on the page read, so a run through any of the three stops at simulation, E5. Lava and Lava-DL name a specific target instead, Intel's Loihi, but the pages read describe that route rather than exercise it, so the CPU/GPU run stays E5 and the Loihi route stays a documented E3; Lava-DL's own repository is additionally archived. The Nengo family breaks this pattern. Nengo and NengoDL optimize and train on CPU or GPU through TensorFlow, E5, but NengoLoihi distinguishes an emulator backend written in pure Python from direct execution on physical Loihi hardware through NxSDK, a path whose own documentation reports accuracy degraded by float-to-integer discretization and partially recovered by optimization, E2 rather than E5. BindsNET is the sparsest record in the group, a peer-reviewed description of the library with neither a runs-on nor a reaches fact stated on the page read. Rockpool and Sinabs each name a chip of their own, the Xylo family and DYNAP-SE2 for Rockpool, Speck for Sinabs, and each is a documented route on the page read but a measured one in Section 11.2, Physical Routes, Rockpool to Xylo and Sinabs to Speck. BindsNET, snnTorch, and Norse stop at the page read, with no route this survey traces beyond it. SNN Toolbox converts a trained rate-based ANN and can target SpiNNaker, Loihi, or a software backend such as pyNN or Brian2, the same conversion-then-documented-route pattern repeated once more.

The seven chip backends and emulators sit one level below the general-purpose libraries above, each naming exactly one physical target and stating how a call reaches it, rather than offering a model library of its own. The Lava Loihi Simulator, covered by Lava's own records, reproduces Loihi's behavior on ordinary CPU rather than on the chip, a software model of the chip, pre-silicon, E4. NxSDK is the weakest record in the table, its own documentation page unreachable after repeated attempts, so its Loihi-execution claim is carried secondhand through NengoLoihi's description of it rather than confirmed on NxSDK's own page, E3 and provisional. sPyNNaker runs directly and only on fabricated SpiNNaker silicon, and its cited paper states a measured per-core throughput figure, over 5,000 synaptic events per core, E1. hxtorch keeps the sharpest internal distinction in the group, genuine execution on BrainScaleS-2 hardware that the page treats as returning measured chip data, E2, held explicitly apart from a mock simulation mode, E5. Akida's MetaTF backend carries the event NPU chip rather than the neuromorphic bucket, since Akida runs a converted, quantized network as sparse events without exposing a spiking-neuron model, and its own documentation describes power and energy per inference read from the device after mapping, E1. The Xylo Simulator trains and simulates on CPU toward eventual Xylo silicon, so its own run stays E5 even though the sub-mW figure it quotes describes the deployed chip rather than the simulator. samna is the lowest-level entry, an API that controls a physical Speck device directly rather than simulating anything, a documented route with no run reported on the page read, E3.

The four hardware-emulation entries sit pre-silicon, each estimating how an SNN-executing circuit would behave rather than reporting a measurement from a fabricated die on the pages read, so every claim in the group tops out at the class shared with the Lava Loihi Simulator above, E4, a hardware model, an RTL synthesis, or a cycle-level simulation without fabricated silicon. APEX and NeuroFlex are each their own accelerator design with no external chip named, quoting a relative efficiency gain against a software or another accelerator baseline, up to 40% energy reduction for APEX and a 57 to 67% energy-delay-product reduction for NeuroFlex, rather than an absolute measured figure. SENECA instead reports an absolute per-operation energy figure, 2.8 pJ per synaptic operation, from a design synthesized to a named process node. The fourth entry, Valancius et al.'s FPGA emulation of TrueNorth, differs from the other three in kind rather than degree, it reimplements another chip's own five-component architecture on a Xilinx development board and checks its outputs against that chip's own reference simulator, functional verification rather than a novel design's efficiency claim.

Reading the catalog against a specific question rather than by name, a biological point-neuron question is answered by NEST or NEURON for a stock model library, or by Brian2 when the model itself is a custom differential equation, every route ending at E5. A deep-network conversion at scale is answered by SpikingJelly or SNN Toolbox when the target chip named in the Reaches column matters, and by snnTorch or Norse when it does not. A chip-faithful pre-silicon run points to the Lava Loihi Simulator for Loihi behavior, or to Valancius et al.'s FPGA emulation when the chip in question is TrueNorth specifically, both capped at E4 rather than a measurement of the named chip's own silicon. A cycle count or an energy-per-operation figure is answered only by the hardware-emulation group, SENECA's 2.8 pJ per synaptic operation being the one absolute figure the catalog carries rather than a relative gain, and every number in that column stays a synthesis-level estimate, never a measurement the catalog can promote past E4.

10.3 Serialization and the Intermediate Representation

Serialization turns a framework's in-memory graph into a file or object another program can consume. Most vendor toolchains in this survey's audited evidence serialize into a format specific to that vendor's own downstream mapper, Sinabs's internal DynapcnnLayer representation and Lava-DL's HDF5 network-exchange schema being two audited instances . NIR is the one cross-vendor exception this survey's audited evidence covers in depth, an interchange format that multiple frameworks, SpikingJelly, Sinabs, Rockpool, and Norse among them, can both write to and read from, aiming to let one trained network reach several otherwise unrelated backends through a single schema rather than a bespoke pairwise exporter for every framework-to-framework combination .

NIR's own documentation states directly that its primitives are idealized and that the schema does not constrain a backend's integration order, spike timing, reset timing, or numerical quantization, so accepting a NIR graph never by itself establishes that two different backends execute it identically . Section 3.5 already gives the sharpest instance of this idealization becoming a representational limit rather than a merely stylistic one. NIR 1.0.8's IF, LIF, and CubaLIF primitives encode only a fixed-value post-spike reset, and a missing or null reset value is materialized as reset-to-zero, so a schema that has no field for reset by subtraction cannot silently misrepresent it. It rejects it structurally, at the level of what the format is capable of expressing at all . This is a property of NIR 1.0.8 specifically, introduced into the schema in 2025, so a source describing an earlier version of the format should not be read as describing the same reset contract .

Three callouts copy audited rows of Table 25, the SpikingJelly exporter that rejects the reset field outright, the NIR schema with no field to carry it into Sinabs, and the Loihi 1 mapping that preserves it by doubling one neuron into two compartments.

Table 25's two Sinabs and NIR edges are not a symmetric round trip. The Sinabs-to-NIR exporter turns a subtractive reset into NIR's fixed-zero default because it does not record the Sinabs reset_fn, and the NIR-to-Sinabs importer turns that same fixed-zero field back into Sinabs's subtractive default because it does not pass the node's \(v_{\mathrm{reset}}\); a network that leaves through NIR and returns lands on its native subtractive reset by coincidence of two silent conversions in opposite directions, not by either edge preserving the field. The official NIR-to-Speck notebook's physical accuracy result on a Speck 2F module proves that this route runs on physical hardware, not that it preserves reset semantics .

Two edges are absent from every row of Table 25, and their absence is itself informative rather than an omission. No audited source in this survey documents a route from Lava or NetX's own soft-reset intent to a custom Loihi 2 neuron process outside the public Lava and NetX artifacts; Loihi 2's neuron engine is documented as fully microcode-programmable, which makes an inference of impossibility unwarranted, and this survey records the edge as undocumented for exactly that reason rather than omitting it . Compilation and mapping edges outside the reset-semantics chain follow the same schema with different subject matter. SNN Toolbox's bridge into NxTF folds batch normalization, applies percentile-based weight and threshold normalization, and preserves the reset-by-subtraction default at the point of compilation, but its own boundary notes state plainly that NxTF is Keras-native, so a PyTorch-trained SpikingJelly model has no generic bridge into this specific compiler regardless of what its own reset rule is . QuantizeML's uniform, symmetric integer quantization is consumed directly by CNN2SNN's mapper, which folds batch normalization and activation quantization into a per-neuron static threshold computed once at conversion time, a transformation this survey records in full in Section 8.11 and revisits as a named route in Section 11.4 .

10.5 Runtime Scheduling and Host Input and Output

A runtime is the software layer that actually executes a compiled graph on a target, whether that target is physical silicon or a simulation running on a central processing unit (CPU) standing in for it. NxSDK is Loihi 1's register-level runtime, superseded for Loihi 2 by the Lava runtime's Magma layer, whose Loihi-targeting components remain proprietary and gated to members of Intel's Neuromorphic Research Community . samna is SynSense's cross-chip developer interface, applying a configuration object to physical Speck or DYNAP-CNN silicon and streaming event data through a chain of source, filter, and sink nodes . SpiNNaker's runtime splits across the on-chip SpiN1API operating system and the host-side SpiNNTools pipeline that loads per-core parameter blocks, signals synchronized execution start across every core, and reads results back through the PyNN API after the run completes . In every one of these cases, the runtime that executes the graph and the host process that prepares its input and reads its output are two different pieces of software crossing a boundary that the deployable contract's host-operations field, fixed in Section 9.2, exists to make explicit.

That boundary is not a peripheral detail this survey's audited evidence lets a platform avoid. Every reviewed digital platform still depends on a host-side step that converts an input into the target's native event or packet format before injection and converts output spikes or counters back into a readable result once execution finishes. Rockpool's Xylo mapper illustrates the same boundary from the accelerated-simulation side of a runtime rather than the physical side, deploying through either XyloSamna or XyloMonitor in accelerated-time or real-time streaming mode, with on-chip power recorded by current monitors sampled at 1280 hertz, while parameter quantization, sample-duration configuration, and application-level decoding all remain the host's responsibility rather than the chip's . This is the concrete instance behind the host-operations status recorded uniformly across every platform in Section 9.5's comparison table, and it is also a direct consequence of the boundary Section 2.4 already drew between an event-driven execution model and an end-to-end event-driven system. A platform whose neuron cores execute without a shared clock can still depend on a host that is neither event-driven nor spiking to prepare its input and interpret its output, and no platform audited in this survey removes that dependency entirely.

10.6 What Transforms, What Is Rejected

Reading Table 25 as a whole rather than edge by edge surfaces three patterns that recur across the software stack independent of which vendor built which piece of it.

The first pattern is that reset semantics are transformed or rejected far more often than they are preserved natively. Of the eleven typed edges in Table 25, two reject the source model outright, four transform the reset rule into a different one, one is undocumented, one concerns the sample boundary rather than the reset rule, and only two preserve subtractive reset, NxTF's route to Loihi 1 only through an emulation that doubles compartment allocation, and Sinabs's route to Speck natively. A third preserving row, Lava-DL NetX HDF5 to the Lava LIF process, preserves the opposite case, a hard-zero reset, not a subtractive one. No audited edge in this survey's evidence base preserves subtractive reset through a native, unmodified, single-compartment hardware primitive on a chip whose own default reset behavior is reset-to-zero. Norse's own documented reset-to-zero default and its separately documented round-trip NIR re-import discrepancy extend this same pattern one layer further back, into a framework's export and import of its own graphs rather than only its export toward a different framework .

The second pattern is that numeric precision is quantized at nearly every boundary this survey's audited evidence documents, and the specific bit widths chosen differ from edge to edge rather than converging on one industry default. NxTF's soft-reset workaround preserves 9-bit weight precision on Loihi 1. Sinabs's mapper quantizes to 8-bit weights and 16-bit membrane state for Speck. SpiNNaker2's NIR importer preserves 8-bit weights alongside 32-bit floating-point state. QuantizeML's uniform symmetric integer quantization for Akida commonly uses one, two, four, or eight bits depending on the layer and the configured precision . A precision field stated as bits alone, without naming which of weights, thresholds, or state that count applies to, is therefore an incomplete report against the contract fixed in Section 9.2.

The third pattern is that maintenance status and access conditions are themselves facts about a software boundary, not a footnote outside the contract. Intel archived the entire Lava, Lava-DL, and NetX stack in 2026, stating that all Lava repositories are archived with no successor SDK yet announced, which freezes every edge in Table 25 that terminates at Loihi 2 through that stack at whatever state it held at the point of archival . Loihi access more generally remains gated to members of Intel's Neuromorphic Research Community rather than openly available . Lynxi's BIDL, Lyngor, and LynSDK toolchain is documented in the sources this survey reviewed as effectively unavailable outside mainland China . A route that is otherwise exact or physical by every other test in this section can still fail a practitioner who cannot obtain the access it requires, a distinction Section 14 returns to as an open problem rather than resolving it here.

Software representation is necessary but not sufficient for a deployment claim. An edge can be native, physical, and E1 evidenced, and still say nothing about what happens once the represented network reaches silicon built on a fundamentally different execution model than the barrier-synchronized, digital, many-core assumption implicit in much of this section's evidence.
