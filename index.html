From Neural Networks to Neuromorphic Execution: A Deployment-Centered Survey of ANN-to-SNN Conversion, Software Stacks, and Silicon

This survey traces the handoffs a converted spiking network must survive to reach a physical neuromorphic chip, recording at each one what is preserved, what is broken, and how strong the evidence is at the destination.

Abstract

Spiking neural networks promise inference at a fraction of the energy cost of conventional accelerators, and ANN-to-SNN conversion is the most accessible route to a trained spiking model. A line of work anchored by quantization-clip-floor-shift conversion has driven the timestep count needed to match ANN accuracy from the hundreds into the single digits. Whether those gains reach silicon is a separate question, and it is the question this survey answers. A trained network that reaches a chip passes through a sequence of handoffs, each governed by a different toolchain, reset rule, spike payload, and time model, and each able to break the activation-to-spike-count correspondence the algorithm paper established. This survey records those handoffs. It presents the preliminaries a reader needs, namely neuron models, neural codes, and the execution contracts that hardware imposes, then reviews the primary training paths, advanced conversion techniques, and the alternative codes that abandon rate entirely. It then maps the deployment stack itself, covering neuromorphic platforms, training and simulation frameworks, export and interchange formats, compilers, and runtimes, and traces every documented route from a trained model to a named chip. Deployment claims are classified on a five-level evidence scale separating measured silicon from software simulation. Applying that scale to the literature exposes a division of labor that no prior survey states: algorithm papers advance accuracy at low timestep counts and do not deploy, while deployment papers reach silicon and do not adopt the new algorithms. Every confirmed case of a new conversion method reaching measured silicon abandons rate coding. The survey closes with the open challenges that division creates, from reset-semantics mismatch and operator coverage to toolchain decay and incomparable efficiency reporting.

1 Introduction

Deep neural networks have achieved strong performance in vision, speech, control, and other data-intensive tasks. This performance, however, commonly relies on large parameter sets and many multiply-and-accumulate operations. The resulting demand for computation, memory movement, and power complicates deployment where responses must be produced close to a sensor or actuator .

At the same time, sensing and inference are moving toward the network edge. Cameras, microphones, tactile arrays, and industrial sensors produce streams that may require local, low-latency interpretation. Sending every observation to a remote processor can add communication cost, delay, and system dependence. This has increased interest in computing systems that can operate within practical limits on memory, throughput, power, and response time while retaining reliable task performance .

Deploying learned models under these constraints remains difficult. The challenge is not only to reduce arithmetic. A deployed system must also represent sensor input, retain state, move data, schedule computation, and produce a decision within the relevant time window. These requirements are especially important for event-based sensing, where a signal records changes rather than a sequence of uniformly sampled frames .

Spiking neural networks (SNNs) offer a different abstraction. A neuron retains state, receives events, and emits discrete spikes when its dynamics meet a condition. Information can therefore be represented in spike count, timing, order, population activity, or graded payloads. This makes SNNs natural candidates for temporal signals and event-based sensing, while also motivating hardware that can exploit conditional activity and sparse communication . Those properties are opportunities, not universal performance guarantees. Their practical value depends on the code, model, workload, platform, and measurement boundary.

Neuromorphic computing is consequently a field rather than a single device class or learning method. It spans dynamical models, neural codes, learning rules, software frameworks, compilers, and digital, analog, and mixed-signal execution systems. Choices made at one layer change what can be expressed at another. A temporal code can require state, precision, routing, or stopping behavior that a target does not provide. A model that is correct in one simulator can require an explicit transformation before a mapper or runtime can execute it .

Researchers reach SNNs through two principal routes. Direct training optimizes a spiking model, usually with local rules, surrogate gradients, or related approximations. ANN-to-SNN conversion starts from a trained artificial neural network (ANN) and maps its activations into spiking dynamics. The routes differ in optimization, representation, and common failure modes. They meet, however, when the resulting SNN must be represented in software and executed on a target .

This survey adopts a deployment-centered perspective on neuromorphic computing. It develops the concepts needed to follow either SNN route from computation and representation to a defensible model-to-chip claim. It is written for readers entering the field, preparing a thesis, selecting a deployment path, or interpreting a result that spans algorithms and hardware. The survey first establishes what a model represents, how its dynamics and time are defined, and what a decoder recovers. It then examines whether those semantics can be preserved, transformed, or measured on a named target.

The scope is deliberately broad across the deployment path and selective within specialist topics. It covers learning only to the depth needed to compare direct training and conversion at their common deployment boundary. It likewise covers sensing, analog circuits, and benchmark protocol only where they change the deployable model or the meaning of a measurement. For broader treatments of SNN learning, see Nunes et al. and Khan et al. . For event-based sensing and its representations, see Gallego et al. . Throughout, the survey keeps an algorithmic horizon, a hardware tick, and wall-clock latency distinct. It also distinguishes a neural code from an input convention, and a documented toolchain capability from a published route that has actually been exercised.

1.1 Existing Surveys

Recent surveys have addressed spiking neural networks, neuromorphic computing, and event-driven systems from different angles, yet these topics are often treated separately. The existing literature can be broadly grouped into five main lines: model- and learning-centered surveys , coding and sensing surveys , software and interoperability surveys , hardware-platform surveys , and benchmarking, deployment, and application reviews . Each line establishes an important part of the field. None alone follows a trained SNN from its computational definition through software and hardware boundaries to a clearly stated measurement claim.

Model- and learning-centered surveys establish the background needed to understand SNN construction. Roy et al. connect learning requirements with hardware mechanisms and cross-layer co-design. Nunes et al. , Zheng et al. , Ayasi et al. , and Luu et al. provide broader introductions to neuron models, coding, learning, and applications. Eshraghian et al. focus on gradient-based training, while Caviglia et al. organize local learning rules and software benchmarks. Rathi et al. , Hu et al. , and He and Gao connect algorithmic structure with scaling or hardware considerations. Yamazaki et al. and Khan et al. extend the discussion across applications and hardware, whereas Xie et al. concentrate on compression for deep SNNs. Although extensive, this literature usually treats the learning method or model family as its primary unit of analysis rather than tracing a complete deployment route.

Coding- and sensing-centered surveys examine how physical or numerical signals become event and spike representations. Gallego et al. provide a comprehensive account of event-camera sensing, representations, algorithms, and applications. Tayarani-Najaran and Schmuker compare event-based sensing across visual, auditory, and olfactory domains. Cimarelli et al. connect neuromorphic vision-sensor hardware with algorithms and applications. These surveys explain the sensing and representation boundary in depth, but provide less coverage of how the resulting semantics survive framework export, compute mapping, and deployment measurement. For a broader taxonomy of neural codes and their signal-representation trade-offs, we refer the reader to Auge et al. .

Software and interoperability studies survey simulators, programming models, compilers, and mapping environments. Manna et al. compare nine SNN frameworks from a data-science perspective. Huynh et al. review software and mapping support for implementing SNNs on neuromorphic architectures. NIR defines a common intermediate representation and exposes semantic disagreement across simulators and hardware back ends. Farsa et al. extend the implementation view to GPU and RISC-V accelerators and identify incompatible reporting practices. Together, these works clarify the available software landscape, but a framework or graph format does not establish that a published model traversed a documented route or retained its reset, delay, payload, and readout semantics.

Hardware-centered surveys emphasize circuits, chip architectures, execution styles, and platform capabilities. Schuman et al. provide an early integration of models, algorithms, hardware, and applications. Bouvier et al. relate SNN algorithms to hardware implementations and their benchmark limitations. Basu et al. and Ferreira et al. compare circuit designs using normalized implementation metrics. Ivanov et al. , Wahid et al. , Zolfagharinejad et al. , and Chen et al. organize broader processor and architecture landscapes. Davies et al. synthesize measured Loihi results, while Shrestha et al. connect model semantics with platform-specific software and compiler support. These surveys establish what particular devices and architecture families can provide. They do not establish that a model definition or compiler route transfers unchanged across platforms.

Benchmarking, deployment, and application reviews provide the broadest system-level view. Schuman et al. connect algorithms and applications to full-stack deployment barriers, while Kudithipudi et al. identify the challenges involved in scaling neuromorphic systems and their software ecosystems. The EdgeSNN survey by Deng et al. organizes edge-oriented methods and separates proxy from hardware-aware evaluation. NeuroBench formalizes this distinction as a benchmarking framework. Rashed and Dias review efficient techniques for edge SNNs, and Du et al. compare SNNs across sensing modalities and edge devices. Gebregiorgis et al. extend the view from biological inspiration through learning, mapping, and hardware architectures. These studies establish why system context and measurement matter. They do not retrospectively classify every published claim by physical execution, documented path, hardware model, or software simulation, nor do they require an edge-by-edge route from a trained model to a named target.

The resulting gap is narrower than a claim that earlier work ignored deployment. Prior surveys address important pieces of the problem. The gap is an end-to-end synthesis that lets a reader ask the same questions of every route. What is mathematically defined? What can the software represent? What does the target execute? What was measured, where, and against which baseline? The answer may be a physical route, a transformed route, a blocked route, or an unexercised documented path. Each answer remains useful when its evidence boundary is stated.

This gap raises a practical question for the field. When a new low-timestep conversion method improves finite-window accuracy in simulation, does its own deployment route reach named physical silicon with measurement, or does deployment proceed through an older pipeline, a directly trained model, or a platform-native code? The evidence synthesis returns to this question without assuming its answer in advance.

1.2 Contributions

This survey makes the following key contributions:

  1. We provide a deployment-centered field-entry reference for neuromorphic computing, covering the computational and representational concepts needed to follow an SNN from model definition to a stated deployment claim.
  2. We present a pedagogical dependency chain from computation and representation through neuron and synapse dynamics, temporal semantics, neural codes, learning, ANN-to-SNN conversion, software, hardware execution, measurement, applications, and open problems.
  3. We connect direct training and ANN-to-SNN conversion at a common deployable SNN contract. The contract identifies the semantic fields that must be defined mathematically, represented in software, and faithfully executed on a target.
  4. We introduce a claim-level evidence framework and a typed route vocabulary. These distinguish physical measurement, physical fidelity, a documented route, a hardware model, and software evidence without assigning one label to an entire paper.
  5. We synthesize platform capabilities from vendor documentation, SDKs, mapper constraints, development kits, release notes, and examples. We then interpret applications by workload and measurement boundary rather than by vendor, making the limits of each reported route explicit.

2 Computation and Representation

A trained model, a simulator, and a physical target must agree on what a number means before any claim about their equivalence is meaningful. This section defines the objects that recur through the rest of the survey, a static value, a persistent state, a discrete event, and a temporal signal. It separates dense computation from sparse activity and conditional execution, distinguishes the several uses of the words neuromorphic and event-driven, and explains why a representation choice constrains which execution strategies remain available downstream. It does not yet compare platforms. It fixes the vocabulary that Sections 3 through 15 depend on.

2.1 Values, State, and Events

An ANN unit produces its output by a weighted sum of its inputs followed by a static nonlinearity, evaluated once for each input presentation. The result, denoted \(a^l\) for a unit in layer \(l\), is a real-valued number. It is a value in the strict sense used throughout this survey. It depends only on the current input, carries no memory of a previous evaluation, and is not scheduled in time. An ANN activation is static within one evaluation. It is a complete description of that unit's output at that input, not a reduced or impoverished version of something else.

A spiking unit instead emits a discrete event, denoted \(z_i^l[t]\in\{0,1\}\) for unit \(i\) in layer \(l\) at discrete algorithmic step \(t\), and typically also carries an internal state \(u_i^l[t]\), the membrane potential, that persists from step to step and is written and later read rather than recomputed independently each time. \(t\) indexes a discrete algorithmic step here, not a duration of physical time. A physical or modeled duration is instead denoted \(\tau\), and the two are not interchangeable until a simulation-to-hardware mapping states how a step corresponds to a duration. On its own, a single event carries almost no information. It either occurred at that step or it did not. What carries information is the relationship between events across steps, their count, their timing, or the pattern they form, together with the state that produced them.

Three objects therefore need to stay distinct. A value is a quantity that depends only on the current input and needs no notion of time to be defined, such as \(a^l\). A state is a quantity that persists, is updated using its own previous value, and can be read at a later step, such as \(u_i^l[t]\). An event is a discrete, typically binary, occurrence tied to a specific step or time, such as \(z_i^l[t]\). A temporal signal is any quantity indexed by \(t\) or \(\tau\), which includes a spike train, a decaying state trajectory, and a time-varying input current, whether or not any individual sample of it carries a large payload. A value has no temporal index. A state and an event both do, but a state is read continuously across steps while an event is a punctual occurrence at one of them.

2.2 Dense Computation, Sparse Activity, and Conditional Execution

In a feedforward ANN evaluation, every unit at every layer produces an activation value on every input presentation, regardless of that value's magnitude. Layer-to-layer computation is dense. A weighted sum touches every incoming weight and every activation, structured as a matrix multiplication, and the arithmetic performed does not depend on whether a particular activation happens to equal zero. A rectified activation of exactly zero still occupies a multiply-accumulate (MAC) operation whose result is simply discarded.

In a network of spiking units, the event \(z_i^l[t]\) defined above is either present or absent at a given step. When a substantial fraction of a layer's events are absent within a finite horizon, an activity pattern this survey calls sparse, the computation associated with a silent unit can in principle be skipped rather than performed and discarded. Communication can be sparse in the same sense. A layer then transmits only its nonzero events rather than a dense vector covering every unit, so the amount transmitted depends on how many units fired rather than on how many units exist .

This distinction describes what the representation makes available, not what a given implementation does with it. Whether a silent unit's absent event actually causes downstream work to be skipped, an execution style this survey calls conditional, or is instead computed by a dense array that pads out the same zero contribution, is a hardware and compiler decision separate from the network's activity pattern. A high event rate, close to one event per unit per step, leaves sparse activity with little advantage over dense computation, and a network may require many steps before enough silence accumulates for skipping to matter at all.

Consequently, fewer spikes and fewer hardware operations are not the same claim. The second requires an execution substrate that treats an absent event as work to be skipped rather than work to be padded, and only a measurement on that substrate establishes that it did so, not the event count alone. Conditional execution names the substrate behavior that would make the skip real. Which platform families realize conditional execution natively and which emulate it with a dense pipeline underneath is compared once execution models are introduced, in the section on hardware execution models.

2.3 The Meanings of Neuromorphic and Event-Driven

The words neuromorphic and event-driven are applied to at least four different objects in this literature, and treating them as one property is a frequent source of overclaiming. A sensor is neuromorphic or event-driven when it asynchronously reports a change in its input as a discrete, timestamped event rather than sampling every input on a fixed clock. An event camera's output stream is event-native from the moment of acquisition in exactly this sense . An algorithm or network model is spiking, and often called neuromorphic, when it is specified in terms of the discrete events and persistent state defined above, independent of what substrate eventually executes it. A network simulated step by step on a conventional processor is a spiking algorithm in this sense whether or not any part of that simulation is triggered by an event. An execution model is event-driven when a core or process advances only when an event demands it, rather than on every tick of a shared clock. This is a property of how computation proceeds, not of how the algorithm was specified. A complete system is neuromorphic in the strongest and least common sense when an event-driven sensor, a spiking algorithm, and an event-driven execution substrate are paired throughout, with no stage reintroducing dense, clocked computation.

These four senses do not transfer into one another automatically. An event-native sensor stream does not establish that the algorithm consuming it is spiking, since a frame can be reconstructed from an event stream and handed to a conventional network. A spiking algorithm does not establish that its execution is event-driven end to end, since a simulator can recompute every unit's state at every step regardless of which events are present or absent. This is the specific, terminological form of a non-equivalence that recurs throughout the survey. Event-native input does not prove event-driven execution throughout a system. Establishing which stage of a given pipeline satisfies which of the four senses, and on which named platform, is deferred to the sections that introduce execution models and traced deployment routes. No platform claim is made here.

2.4 Why Representation Constrains Execution

The representation chosen at one point of the model-to-device chain restricts which execution strategies remain faithful to it at the next. A dense, real-valued activation \(a^l\) must be multiplied against a weight and accumulated, and nothing about that representation says the multiplication can be omitted for a particular unit, since a value close to zero is still a value that must be multiplied. Executing a layer of such units efficiently favors an engine built around dense multiply-accumulate arithmetic, because every unit contributes a term regardless of magnitude.

A binary event \(z_i^l[t]\) is a discrete occurrence rather than a magnitude to multiply. Where a synaptic weight is fixed in advance, the operation triggered by an event degenerates to a single addition performed only when the event fires. The weight is accumulated into the postsynaptic state, and nothing happens otherwise . An event-based representation therefore admits an accumulate-only execution strategy that a dense, value-based representation does not offer as a comparable option, because a real-valued activation cannot degenerate to a data-dependent addition in the same way. Its magnitude is exactly the information that a spike's mere presence does not carry.

That an accumulate-only strategy is representationally admissible does not mean any particular hardware realizes it, and does not mean realizing it is free. Maintaining per-unit persistent state, routing events asynchronously, and reading out a decision after a variable number of steps all add engineering cost that a fixed-schedule, dense pipeline avoids by construction. A representation that relaxes one constraint typically introduces another. State must be stored somewhere, and event timing must be tracked somewhere, even when no multiplication is required.

The consequence carried forward is only that representation sets the space of physically consistent execution strategies available to a target. It does not by itself select one, and it does not establish that a specific chip, compiler, or runtime exploits the option a representation makes available. Which execution families exist, and which of their capabilities are native, transformed, emulated, host-assisted, unsupported, or undocumented for a given representation, is the comparison made in the section on hardware execution models. Which complete route a published deployment actually exercised is established only in the section that traces named deployment routes edge by edge.

2.5 Bounding Neuroscience and Device Physics

The vocabulary above is deliberately algorithmic. It does not derive spike generation from ion-channel biophysics, and it does not derive an accumulate-only execution strategy from the physics of a specific circuit or fabricated device. Both derivations exist, are well developed elsewhere, and are bounded out of this survey by the following referrals.

The present discussion stops after the distinction between a static value and a stateful, event-carrying representation. For a treatment that connects this distinction back to the biological mechanisms of spike generation, see Zheng et al. , which is particularly useful for tracing the specific biological detail an integrate-and-fire abstraction discards.

The present discussion stops after distinguishing an event-native sensor from a spiking algorithm and from an event-driven execution model. For the physical sensing mechanisms behind that first distinction, see Tayarani-Najaran et al. , which is particularly useful for comparing event-based transduction across visual, auditory, and olfactory sensing modalities.

The present discussion stops after noting that an accumulate-only execution strategy is representationally admissible, without deriving that admissibility from a specific circuit or material substrate. For the device-level physics of brain-inspired and analog neuromorphic substrates, see Zolfagharinejad et al. , which is particularly useful for connecting emerging device behavior back to the same representational choices introduced here.

The next section, on neuron and synapse dynamics, turns the persistent state and discrete events introduced here into one common, staged state-update rule. The integrate-and-fire, leaky integrate-and-fire, current-based, adaptive, and multi-compartment models used throughout the remaining sections are then derived as variations of that single update rather than as an unrelated list of neuron types.

3 Neuron and Synapse Dynamics

Section 2 distinguished a value from a state, an event, and a temporal signal without committing to any particular neuron equation. This section makes that commitment. It fixes one staged update that every neuron model used later in the survey instantiates, so that a difference between models is stated as a difference in one named stage rather than as an unrelated new equation. The same staging separates a model's continuous mathematical description from its discrete-time simulation and from its finite-precision hardware realization, three questions that later sections keep asking separately about reset, coding, and execution.

3.1 A Common Staged State Update

A spiking neuron in layer \(l\) receives events from presynaptic units \(j\), maintains an internal state, and emits an event \(z_i^l[t] \in \{0,1\}\) when that state crosses a threshold. Rather than writing this as one overloaded recurrence, this survey factors it into three stages, following the notation fixed for the whole article. A synaptic stage \(\mathcal{S}^l\) turns incoming events into a synaptic state or filtered current \(q_i^l[t]\). A membrane stage \(\mathcal{M}^l\) combines that current with the previous membrane state \(u_i^l[t-1]\), an optional adaptation state \(a_i^l[t-1]\), and a bias \(b_i^l\) into a pre-reset candidate \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]\). A threshold function \(\mathcal{H}\) compares that candidate against a threshold \(\theta_i^l[t]\) to decide whether an event is emitted. A reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\) then turns the pre-reset candidate, the emitted event, and the threshold into the post-reset state \(u_i^l[t]\) that stage \(\mathcal{M}^l\) reads at the next step.

\[ q_i^l[t] = \mathcal{S}^l\!\left(q_i^l[t-1],\{z_j^{l-1}[t-d_{ij}^l]\}_j,\{w_{ij}^l\}_j\right), \] \[ \tilde{u}_i^l[t] = \mathcal{M}^l\!\left(u_i^l[t-1],q_i^l[t],a_i^l[t-1],b_i^l\right), \qquad z_i^l[t] = \mathcal{H}\!\left(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]-\theta_i^l[t]\right), \] \[ u_i^l[t] = \mathcal{R}^l\!\left(\tilde{u}_i^l[t],z_i^l[t],\theta_i^l[t]\right). \]

Two indexing conventions hold throughout this section. First, the update order is fixed as written. \(\mathcal{S}^l\) is evaluated before \(\mathcal{M}^l\), and \(\mathcal{H}\) is evaluated on the pre-reset candidate \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]\), never on the already-reset \(u_i^l[t-1]\). Second, \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]\) always denotes the value before the reset stage has acted and \(u_i^l[t]\) always denotes the value after it, so a later equation that writes \(u_i^l[t]\) is referring to the quantity that stage \(\mathcal{M}^l\) will read at step \(t+1\). This vocabulary is a bookkeeping device, not a claim about implementation. It does not assert that every simulator or every chip literally computes four separate intermediate arrays in this order; Section 3.5 returns to what a hardware or software implementation is free to change and what it is not.

Framing every model this way lets the rest of the section ask one question per model. Which stage does this neuron modify relative to the plainest case, and does it introduce a new persistent state variable or only a new coefficient inside an existing stage? The plainest case, developed next, uses a trivial \(\mathcal{S}^l\) with no memory of its own, an \(\mathcal{M}^l\) with no decay, and a fixed threshold, which is the discrete-time abstraction that the classical literature on spiking neurons and network models builds on .

3.2 The Neuron Model Family

Integrate-and-fire (IF) is the baseline instance of the staged update. Its synaptic stage has no memory, computing \(q_i^l[t]=\sum_j w_{ij}^l z_j^{l-1}[t-d_{ij}^l]\) fresh at every step with nothing carried over from \(q_i^l[t-1]\). Its membrane stage adds that current to the previous state with no decay, \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]=u_i^l[t-1]+q_i^l[t]+b_i^l\), and its threshold is fixed, \(\theta_i^l[t]=\theta_i^l\). The mechanism is deliberately the simplest possible instance of the three stages. Its mathematical effect is that every unit of input charge that ever arrives is eventually spent as output, so a finite-window spike count is not systematically damped relative to the current driving it. This is the property the classical ANN-to-SNN conversion literature relies on when it equates a spike count with a target ReLU activation, and it is the reason that literature targets IF neurons specifically rather than a leaky variant . The trade-off is that IF has no mechanism for forgetting old input, which conversion accepts as a feature but which direct training on temporally structured data usually treats as a limitation. The deployment consequence is that IF's simplicity makes it the easiest model to realize with a single accumulator register; the evidence boundary is that this is a mechanism-level statement about the target of a mapping, not a deployment measurement on its own.

Leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) keeps IF's trivial synaptic stage and modifies only the membrane stage, introducing a per-layer leak factor \(\alpha^l\) with \(0<\alpha^l<1\) that this survey treats as a new local symbol, distinct from the activation or threshold scale \(\lambda^l\) that later sections use for conversion, giving \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]=\alpha^l u_i^l[t-1]+q_i^l[t]+b_i^l\). The membrane now relaxes toward its resting value between inputs instead of holding charge indefinitely. The trade-off is exactly the property IF avoids. A unit of charge that arrives and is not soon followed by enough further input to cross threshold is partially forgotten rather than eventually spent, so a naive spike count over a short window is no longer an unbiased read of the driving current. This is one reason the rate-coding conversion literature above targets IF rather than LIF even though LIF is the more common default in general spiking-network practice. LIF is also the more common built-in choice on programmable digital substrates; it is documented as SpiNNaker's default current-based neuron model and is available as a selectable leaky or leak-disabled mode on Sinabs-backed DYNAP-CNN and Speck deployments, where enabling the leak requires an accompanying bias term and an externally configured slow-clock rate .

Current-based LIF (CUBA-LIF) is the first model in this family that gives the synaptic stage genuine memory. Instead of the instantaneous sum used by IF and LIF, \(q_i^l[t]\) is itself a leaky integrator of the incoming weighted events, \(q_i^l[t]=\beta^l q_i^l[t-1]+\sum_j w_{ij}^l z_j^{l-1}[t-d_{ij}^l]\), and the membrane stage integrates this filtered current the same way LIF integrates the raw sum, \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]=\alpha^l u_i^l[t-1]+q_i^l[t]+b_i^l\). The mechanism adds a second decaying state variable, so a single presynaptic event spreads its influence over several steps instead of landing on the membrane in one step. The consequence for a hardware target is that this two-stage filter is exactly what a single-compartment current-based primitive is built to execute directly. CUBA-LIF is documented as Loihi's native default neuron dynamics and is exposed in Lava-DL as the SLAYER current-based integration block . The trade-off is the same forgetting property LIF introduces, now applied twice, once to the synaptic state and once to the membrane state, so a CUBA-LIF spike count is a still less direct read of any single driving input than an LIF count. Its native single-compartment reset defaults to reset-to-zero, a fact Section 3.4 returns to directly.

Adaptive LIF (ALIF) leaves the synaptic and membrane stages as in IF or LIF but couples the threshold itself to the neuron's own recent spiking through the adaptation state \(a_i^l[t]\) that the common update already reserves. A representative discrete form raises the threshold after every spike and lets it relax back toward a baseline between spikes, \[ \theta_i^l[t]=\theta_0^l+\kappa^l a_i^l[t-1], \qquad a_i^l[t]=\gamma^l a_i^l[t-1]+z_i^l[t], \] with \(0<\gamma^l<1\) a second local decay constant and \(\kappa^l\) a coupling gain, following the discrete adaptive formulation used for long-memory spiking networks . The adaptation update runs alongside the reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\), triggered by the same emitted event \(z_i^l[t]\), rather than being one of the three canonical stages itself. The semantic effect is that a constant input no longer produces a constant firing rate; the rate falls as the threshold climbs, giving the neuron a longer effective memory than a single leak constant can provide without an explicit multiplicative gate. The trade-off is a second persistent state variable per neuron and a nonstationary threshold that later sections must track wherever they otherwise treat \(\theta_i^l\) as fixed. Native support for this mechanism is uneven. It is documented as a directly implemented analog adaptation current on BrainScaleS-2 , while on Loihi it is realized through the multi-compartment mechanism introduced next, at the cost of a second compartment per adaptive neuron .

Multi-compartment neurons generalize the single state triple \((q_i^l,u_i^l,a_i^l)\) into several coupled instances indexed by a compartment \(c\), each running its own copy of the staged update, with the output of one compartment entering another compartment's synaptic or membrane stage as an additional term. Only a designated somatic compartment evaluates \(\mathcal{H}\) and emits \(z_i^l[t]\); the other compartments exist purely to hold auxiliary state that a single-compartment primitive cannot represent. In the deployment-relevant literature this survey reviews, the mechanism is used less for dendritic realism than as an engineering workaround. On Loihi, a second compartment is documented as the way both a subtractive reset and spike-frequency adaptation are recovered from a hardware primitive whose native single-compartment behavior does neither, at the declared cost of doubling compartment allocation per affected neuron . On BrainScaleS-2, multi-compartment structure is instead documented as native dendritic computation with genuine branch-dependent behavior, not only a workaround for a missing single-compartment feature . The deployment consequence is that a model description naming "a multi-compartment neuron" is incomplete until it states which of these two roles the extra compartment plays and at what resource cost, a distinction Section 10 revisits as part of the deployable contract's neuron-equation field.

The staged update as written assumes a binary event \(z_i^l[t]\in\{0,1\}\). Nothing in \(\mathcal{S}^l\), \(\mathcal{M}^l\), or \(\mathcal{R}^l\) requires this. The threshold function \(\mathcal{H}\) can instead emit a signed or graded payload \(p_{ik}\), so that a neuron reports a magnitude and a sign rather than a bare occurrence. This is a change to what the emission stage produces and to what the next layer's synaptic stage \(\mathcal{S}^{l+1}\) consumes, not a change to which of the five dynamical variants above is running underneath it; the survey therefore treats signed or graded emission as a payload property, developed further as a neural-code question in Section 4, rather than as a sixth neuron family. The distinction has a deployment motivation. A unipolar event cannot represent a negative activation, and batch normalization together with residual connections routinely produce substantial negative mass in a pre-activation distribution. Software mechanisms that keep a binary payload while still representing a signed quantity include tracking a running net spike count and gating negative spikes so the effective rate cannot fall below zero , and maintaining a shadow membrane potential that fires a compensating negative spike whenever it diverges from the true potential past a negative threshold . A graded integer payload carrying a signed magnitude directly in the spike message is documented for Loihi 2's microcode-enabled neurons ; this survey has not located an equivalent documented primitive elsewhere in the sources reviewed for this section, which is a statement about what has been located, not a claim that no other platform can do so.

The present discussion stops after the discrete state variables that ANN-to-SNN conversion and the neuromorphic targets covered later in this survey actually use. For a fuller treatment of continuous biophysical spike generation, see the Hodgkin-Huxley conductance model , which is particularly useful for the ionic mechanism underlying an action potential, and for the diversity of observed firing patterns arising from richer bifurcation dynamics, see the Izhikevich two-variable model . This literature treats both as a ceiling on biological realism rather than as a deployment target.

3.3 Synaptic State, Delay, and Recurrent Input

The synaptic stage \(\mathcal{S}^l\) is where the common update already accounts for filtering, delay, and recurrence, so this subsection names what varies inside it rather than introducing new stages. Filtering is the difference between IF or LIF's instantaneous sum and CUBA-LIF's leaky current developed above; a model family can therefore be extended with a different synaptic filter, for instance a second-order or dual-exponential current, by changing only \(\mathcal{S}^l\)'s internal recursion, without touching \(\mathcal{M}^l\), \(\mathcal{H}\), or \(\mathcal{R}^l\).

Delay enters through the index \(d_{ij}^l\) already present in \(\mathcal{S}^l\)'s argument list, which shifts a presynaptic event \(z_j^{l-1}\) by \(d_{ij}^l\) steps before it contributes to \(q_i^l[t]\). Two properties of \(d_{ij}^l\) matter for later sections and must not be conflated. First, whether delay is a configured per-connection parameter or a fixed architectural consequence of the time model itself is a separate question, developed once Section 4 fixes the vocabulary for hardware ticks and asynchronous propagation; this subsection only fixes that \(d_{ij}^l\) can be measured in discrete steps, hardware ticks, or physical time, and that a citation using it must state which. Second, a per-connection configurable delay is a documented capability on some targets and not others. SpiNNaker's software-defined synapse model exposes a programmable per-synapse delay, and a training pipeline built around Loihi 2's native delay mechanism learns per-synapse delays of up to several dozen steps and reports feedforward latency and dynamic energy for the resulting network on physical hardware . Neither example licenses a claim about delay support on a platform not named in that source.

Recurrent state is not a new stage either. A recurrent connection is a synaptic weight \(w_{ij}^l\) whose presynaptic index \(j\) ranges over units in the same layer, or over a later layer through a delay \(d_{ij}^l\) of at least one step so that the update in Section 3.1 remains causal. The adaptation state \(a_i^l[t]\) introduced for ALIF is a second, complementary way a neuron's own history feeds back into itself, operating through the threshold rather than through an explicit recurrent synapse. Refractory behavior, a period after a spike during which a neuron cannot fire again, is a boundary case of the same adaptation mechanism rather than a variable this survey reserves a new symbol for. It is realized by transiently raising \(\theta_i^l[t]\) to a value \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]\) cannot cross, using the same coupling that produces spike-frequency adaptation, rather than by suppressing the update or the emission stage directly.

3.4 Reset Semantics

The reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\) is the last of the four functions in Section 3.1's update, and it is the one this survey treats as load-bearing rather than incidental, because the correctness argument behind rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion depends on which reset rule it implements. Two rules recur across every model in Section 3.2; the literature and this survey call them reset-to-zero and reset-by-subtraction, with hard reset and soft reset as common aliases used once here and not repeated.

Reset-to-zero clamps the post-reset state to a fixed baseline, usually zero, whenever an event is emitted, and otherwise leaves the pre-reset candidate unchanged, as follows. \[ u_i^l[t]=\bigl(1-z_i^l[t]\bigr)\,\tilde{u}_i^l[t]+z_i^l[t]\,u_{\mathrm{reset}}^l . \] Reset-by-subtraction instead removes only the threshold that was just crossed, carrying any remainder forward into the next step, as follows. \[ u_i^l[t]=\tilde{u}_i^l[t]-z_i^l[t]\,\theta_i^l[t]. \] Both rules are read directly off the general reset term \(r_i^l[t]\) that the notation reserves for this purpose. Define the residual charge at a spike event as \(r_i^l[t]=\tilde{u}_i^l[t]-\theta_i^l[t]\), the amount by which the pre-reset candidate exceeded threshold. Reset-to-zero discards \(r_i^l[t]\) outright; reset-by-subtraction retains it inside \(u_i^l[t]\), where the membrane stage will integrate it again at the next step. This is the entire mechanical difference between the two rules, and it is the reason they are not interchangeable implementation details of the same neuron.

The semantic consequence of discarding \(r_i^l[t]\) is not zero-mean noise that a longer simulation window averages away. Because \(r_i^l[t]\) depends on the input driving the neuron rather than on any random process, reset-to-zero introduces a systematic, input-dependent bias in the resulting spike count relative to the current that produced it, one that compounds across the layers of a deep network. The derivation behind rate-coded conversion shows that reset-by-subtraction removes this bias term entirely, leaving only a bounded discretization error that shrinks as the simulation horizon \(T\) grows, rather than a persistent offset that does not . This is a cited asymptotic claim about the discrete-time model developed in Section 7, not a general property of every deployed system with finite \(T\); a numeric result reported under one target's discretization and precision should not be read as identical to a result on another target unless that other target implements the same reset stage over the same finite window.

The deployment consequence is that reset is not a property a toolchain name can be trusted to fix once. It must be tracked separately at the neuron equation, at the framework or conversion default, at the exporter or importer, and at the hardware execution primitive, because each of these boundaries can implement a different rule from its neighbor. The Neuromorphic Intermediate Representation (NIR) illustrates the first boundary directly. Its current schema encodes a post-spike reset to a fixed value and has no field for reset-by-subtraction, so a missing or null reset value is materialized as reset-to-zero rather than left as reset-by-subtraction . SpikingJelly illustrates the second and third boundaries together. Its generic neuron constructors default to reset-to-zero, while its own rate-coding ANN-to-SNN conversion recipe instead instantiates reset-by-subtraction neurons by default, and both its NIR exporter and its Lava exporter reject that converted default rather than silently reinterpreting it as a fixed-value reset . NxTF illustrates the hardware boundary. It recovers reset-by-subtraction on Loihi 1, whose native single-compartment primitive resets to zero, by allocating a second compartment that absorbs the residual and inhibits the first back to the correct level, which the source documents as doubling compartment allocation for every neuron configured this way; this is an emulated capability, not a native one, and the same source reports physical accuracy, execution time, and energy measurements on a Nahuku32 board, with the qualification that not every benchmark in that paper used the soft-reset configuration . Sinabs and Speck illustrate a case where the mapping is documented as native rather than emulated. Sinabs defaults ANN conversion to reset-by-subtraction, and its DYNAP-CNN mapper carries that behavior into Speck's own configurable reset mode; other parts of the same route, including pooling and weight precision, remain transformed even where reset itself is preserved . A custom-microcode route for reset-by-subtraction on Loihi 2 outside the public Lava and NetX toolchains is undocumented in the sources reviewed for this survey; an absence of documentation supports neither a claim that such a route exists nor a claim that it cannot.

None of these examples licenses a general verdict about a framework or a chip family. Each states a capability status, native, transformed, or emulated, scoped to a named version and a named target, together with whatever evidence class its accompanying measurement supports. Section 10 returns to reset as one of the fields a deployable contract must state explicitly, and Section 11 returns to the exporter and importer boundaries above as instances of a general software-boundary schema.

3.5 Continuous Dynamics, Discretization, and Hardware Realization

Every membrane equation in Section 3.2 can be read at three distinct levels, and this survey keeps them separate on purpose. The first is a continuous dynamical description in physical or modeled time \(\tau\). LIF's membrane, for instance, is conventionally written as a first-order relaxation toward a resting potential under an input current \(I_i^l(\tau)\), with a membrane time constant \(\tau_m^l\) governing the relaxation rate. The second level is the numerical discretization that turns this differential equation into the recurrence \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]=\alpha^l u_i^l[t-1]+q_i^l[t]+b_i^l\) used above, at a chosen step size \(\Delta t\). A forward-Euler discretization gives \(\alpha^l=1-\Delta t/\tau_m^l\), valid only while \(\Delta t\) is small enough to keep this quantity inside \((0,1)\); the exact discretization of the same linear equation instead gives \(\alpha^l=\exp(-\Delta t/\tau_m^l)\), which remains a valid decay factor for any \(\Delta t\). These are two different, individually defensible ways of turning the same continuous model into the same discrete stage, and they do not in general produce identical trajectories for the same \(\tau_m^l\) and \(\Delta t\). The third level is hardware realization. A target executes the chosen discrete recurrence with a finite-precision state and weight representation, using bit widths \(b_u\), \(b_w\), and \(b_\theta\) that are independent design choices from one another, and it advances that recurrence according to its own tick semantics, whether a fixed hardware quantity, a configurable parameter, or a scheduling convention layered on an otherwise asynchronous substrate.

Keeping these three levels distinct matters because a claim that is true at one level does not transfer automatically to the next. Accepting a graph that specifies \(\tau_m^l\) and a reset rule does not by itself establish which discretization a given backend applies to it, and a cross-platform intermediate representation is explicit that it declares idealized primitives without constraining backend-specific integration order, spike timing, reset timing, or numerical quantization, so a syntactically accepted graph does not establish execution-equivalent dynamics across two different backends . A tick duration illustrates the same separation from the hardware side. SpiNNaker's software convention sets a default tick near one millisecond to match a biological time reference, a scheduling convention rather than a hardware limit, since finer ticks trade a proportionally slower real-time factor for finer resolution , while an asynchronous barrier-synchronized target advances a fixed-size algorithmic step whose own wall-clock duration is not fixed by the model at all . Section 4 fixes the full vocabulary needed to compare an algorithmic step, a hardware tick, and wall-clock latency without conflating them, and Section 12 returns to how execution families differ at exactly this third level.

3.6 Semantics That Become Contract Fields

The staged update, the neuron model family it generates, and the reset derivation above are not only a vocabulary for this section. They fix the semantics that Section 10 later records as explicit fields of a deployable SNN contract, because a route that changes any of them without saying so has changed what the network computes rather than merely how it is executed. Those semantics are the neuron equations and their update order, fixed in Section 3.1; synaptic dynamics and delay, fixed in Section 3.3; threshold and reset, fixed in Section 3.4; spike payload, raised in Section 3.2 wherever emission carries more than a bare occurrence; and the precision assigned separately to weights, thresholds, and state, raised in Section 3.5. Time itself, the remaining quantity this section has deliberately used only through the symbols \(\tau\), \(\Delta t\), and \(\tau_{\mathrm{tick}}\) without comparing execution families, is defined in full in Section 4, together with the neural codes that decide what a sequence of these staged updates is understood to represent.

6.1.1 Per-Platform Notes

Loihi 1. The chip's fixed CUBA-LIF datapath hard-resets to zero; QCFS-style soft reset is not a hardware primitive but a compiler construction, the two-compartment trick, that consumes one extra compartment per neuron and was explicitly flagged by the NxTF authors as a feature they would prefer the hardware to provide directly . Loihi 1 is the platform with the clearest published physical-silicon result for a rate-coded, reset-by-subtraction conversion: NxTF-compiled CNNs reached 0.79% error on MNIST and 8.52% error on CIFAR-10, both measured for accuracy, energy, and latency on physical chips . It is not commercially available; access requires INRC membership and is mediated through a cloud of shared VMs .

Loihi 2. The architectural headline is a microcode-programmable neural engine that replaces Loihi 1's fixed datapath, enabling Resonate-and-Fire, Izhikevich, and sigma-delta neuron models alongside CUBA-LIF, and generalizing binary spikes into a 32-bit graded payload . No source located documents a soft-reset primitive for Loihi 2; the reference PyLifModel process explicitly hard-resets to zero and is annotated "Loihi-1 compatible" . The chip's own architects state directly that classic rate-coded conversion is a poor fit for Loihi 2, citing long latencies at scale, and that this is the stated rationale behind Loihi 2's shift toward graded and sigma-delta coding for deep-learning workloads .

Intel archived the entire Lava software stack on 13 May 2026, stating a next-generation SDK is in development ; the only published conversion result on physical Loihi 2 abandons rate coding for sigma-delta coding specifically because rate coding "required many simulation time steps per inference, which degraded efficiency" .

SpiNNaker 1. A general-purpose, software-defined many-core system rather than a fixed-function neuromorphic ASIC: every neuron/synapse model is compiled C code, so reset semantics and precision are whatever the user's model implements, not a hardware constant . A converted, rate-coded LeNet reached 98.20% accuracy on physical SpiNNaker-103 hardware, the clearest CNN-conversion result found on this platform, though no CIFAR-10-scale or larger converted CNN has been measured on physical SpiNNaker 1 or 2 silicon in any source located . Access is free through EBRAINS for research and evaluation .

SpiNNaker 2. Adds a dense 8/16-bit MAC array alongside the SpiNNaker-lineage event-driven core, explicitly targeting hybrid ANN/SNN/rule-based execution on the same processing element . Its NIR importer is the only backend in this survey documented to support both reset-by-subtraction and reset-to-zero as software options, a hardware/software capability that sits outside NIR's own formal primitive specification . The one VGG-16/ResNet-50-scale SpiNNaker2 deployment result found in the literature is a pre-silicon timing simulation, explicitly stated by its own authors ("the SpiNNaker2 chip is not yet available"), not a measurement on fabricated hardware . SpiNNcloud Systems now sells SpiNNaker2 as a commercial on-prem or cloud-accessed supercomputer platform .

Speck / DYNAP-CNN. Fully asynchronous, clockless convolutional compute: nine fixed-order Conv-to-IF-to-SumPool cores route events with a measured end-to-end latency of 3.36 microseconds across all nine layers, contrasted explicitly by the chip's authors against clocked chips (Loihi, TrueNorth), which "architecturally introduce a latency of one timestep on event generation in each layer" . Reset-by-subtraction is the software and hardware default, not an option a user must opt into . On the hardware side the rule is a boolean on each core of the samna configuration, return_to_zero, where False subtracts the threshold and True clamps to zero, and the devkit ships with it set to subtract . SynSense documents the choice for this exact case, directing users who arrive by ANN-to-SNN conversion to the subtractive setting . One constraint rides along with it. Because the neuron is evaluated only when an input event arrives, it emits at most one spike per arrival, so a potential driven past twice the threshold still produces a single spike and holds the remainder until the next input . No charge is lost, which is what separates this from hard reset, but the instantaneous multi-spike emission that conversion theory grants an IF neuron is spread across later events instead. The chip has no native BatchNorm support and no native AvgPool2d; both are handled by architectural rewriting at conversion time, not by a transparent mapping . Hardware is commercially purchasable with no research-membership gate, distinguishing it sharply from the INRC- and EBRAINS-gated platforms above .

Xylo. A genuinely synchronous, globally clocked digital SNN core for audio/IMU workloads, the opposite time model from Speck: "Synchronous time-stepped architecture with a global time-step dt" is a hardware property, not a software convention . Reset-by-subtraction is likewise a fixed hardware property. Deployment is via Rockpool, a separate SynSense toolchain from the Sinabs path that targets Speck . Xylo neurons can emit up to 31 spikes per neuron per timestep, a materially different regime from the single-spike-per-step assumption most conversion theory makes .

BrainScaleS-2. A physical analog emulation, not a digital simulator: circuit time constants are genuinely 1000x faster than the biological values they represent, and experiments run as PPU-orchestrated batched frames with explicit, digitally controlled stimulus and readout windows . A converted, rate-coded ANN-to-SNN pipeline has been demonstrated on the wafer-scale predecessor system, recovering from a 72%-to-95% accuracy collapse only after hardware-in-the-loop retraining, for a small digit-classification network, not a deep CNN . The CNN-scale results on BrainScaleS-2 itself use a distinct, non-spiking "hagen" MAC mode rather than multi-timestep rate-coded IF accumulation . Analog fixed-pattern mismatch and trial-to-trial variability are irreducible even after calibration; every source in this literature reports hardware-in-the-loop training, not an assumption of exact IF correspondence, as the practical countermeasure . Access is free through EBRAINS, not a commercial purchase .

TrueNorth. Discontinued since the mid-2010s DARPA SyNAPSE program and never commercially sold . Its Eedn training methodology is not multi-timestep temporal rate coding: activation magnitude is represented spatially, by the aggregate spike count across a population of redundant trinary-weighted neuron copies evaluated once per 1 kHz tick, not by accumulation over repeated ticks . TrueNorth should not be cited as a hardware validation of the temporal rate-coded, reset-by-subtraction IF conversion this survey tracks. IBM's successor chip, NorthPole, is a purely digital, non-spiking, low-precision inference accelerator with no membrane dynamics; it is architecturally descended from TrueNorth's memory-locality principles but is out of scope as a spiking substrate.

Tianjic / Lynxi. Designed explicitly to fuse ANN and SNN execution on shared silicon within a single network, with some cores relaying full-precision membrane potentials between compartments rather than binary spikes . Lynxi, the commercial spinout, ships the BIDL PyTorch-based framework and states plainly in its own documentation that "most brain-inspired chips operate in a timestep driven manner, where a timestep iteration is located outside the neural network," confirming a global, not per-layer, time model . No evidence was found of Lynxi hardware being sold, distributed, or benchmarked outside mainland China .

Akida. Treated in full critical detail below.

Innatera. A commercially shipping mixed analog/digital SNN microcontroller family (T1, Pulsar) aimed at always-on sensor edge tasks, sub-millisecond wake triggers at microwatt power . No independent, peer-reviewed benchmark of either chip was located; every quantitative claim retrieved (100x latency, 500x energy reduction) is vendor-sourced . Reset semantics, spike payload, and weight precision are not documented in any source found.

Academic ASICs. ODIN, ReckOn, and MorphIC are research-fabricated single-digit-count prototype dies whose design center is on-chip online learning (SDSP, e-prop, stochastic SDSP), not deep-CNN throughput or reset-semantics fidelity to the conversion literature . MorphIC provides the clearest same-silicon, same-task energy comparison in this survey: rate coding measured 205 microjoules per MNIST classification versus 21.8 microjoules for rank-order coding on the identical chip, a roughly tenfold difference attributable to the coding scheme alone . SENECA, a RISC-V-based digital neuromorphic processor from imec, has not been fabricated as of any source found; all reported efficiency figures are synthesis and simulation results .

FPGA. No unified toolchain exists; each accelerator surveyed is an independent open-source RTL or HLS project. SyncNN is the design closest to the assumptions of the conversion literature, explicitly implementing the IF model with reset-by-subtraction and Poisson rate encoding, measured across three physical Xilinx boards . Spiker+ is the only accelerator surveyed offering reset-by-subtraction as a named, user-selectable configuration option alongside hard reset . Cerebron is the clearest FPGA accelerator explicitly built around the SNN Toolbox conversion pipeline . All measured results in this row are physical-board measurements (E1), not synthesis estimates alone.

6.1.2 Akida, Examined Critically

Akida gets a full platform entry above and a separate critical treatment here because its marketing framing ("neuromorphic," "spiking neural processor") and its actual converted-CNN execution mechanism diverge sharply, and because that divergence is directly relevant to a thesis built on the ReLU-to-IF, rate-coded conversion correspondence.

The standard deployment path, QuantizeML to CNN2SNN, is quantization-aware training followed by a batch-norm fold, not a rate-coding conversion. QuantizeML performs uniform, symmetric, zero-centered integer quantization of weights and activations; it contains no weight-normalization pass calibrated to a target firing rate over a chosen horizon T, and no iterative "spike count over T approximates the ReLU" simulation . CNN2SNN's own documentation states that BatchNorm scale and shift, along with the quantized-activation math, "are never performed during inference" as separate floating-point operations; they are folded once, at conversion time, into the neuron's firing threshold and other static parameters . Direct inspection of the CNN2SNN 2.19.1 source confirms the mechanism at the code level: the Akida 1.0 activation output is computed as y = x / act_step, where act_step derives from the QuantizeML output quantizer's scale, and the threshold itself is offset by a rounded half-step before division, a rounding-to-nearest-bin correction folded into a static comparator, not an accumulator integrating over a simulation horizon . There is no membrane potential carried across timesteps on this path, and no user-exposed T.

Two independent papers, trained and deployed on real AKD1000 hardware, describe the same collapse in near-identical language. A robotics group building a fast-moving-object detector states that Akida "squashes the rate-code approximation of the ReLU into one time step, where it is then represented by a step-wise quantized ReLU," and that this representation "allows for processing in a single time step" . A space-applications benchmarking paper independently concludes that "the AKD1000 does not support leaky neurons... the implementation suggest[s] that the converted spiking model reduces the computation to a single time step" . Both papers were checked and corroborated against the CNN2SNN source directly; the empirical claim and the mechanism agree.

This is not simply marketing dressed up as neuromorphic computing. Akida's lineage is genuine: BrainChip acquired Spikenet Technology in 2016, and Simon Thorpe, the originator of rank order coding, has since sat on BrainChip's Scientific Advisory Board . Rank order coding is a real, peer-reviewed temporal code in which a neuron population is maximally activated when its inputs arrive in the order of their synaptic weights, via a shunting-inhibition mechanism, with at most one spike per neuron carrying the information . A trade-press technical analysis independently corroborates the pivot: "BrainChip started with rate coding, but decided that wasn't commercially viable. Instead, it uses rank coding" . Akida's input-encoding stage is described by multiple independent, non-BrainChip sources as rank-order-coded. However, no source examined in this research, including direct inspection of the CNN2SNN output and activation code, shows evidence of an order-sensitive, shunting-inhibition mechanism operating inside the hidden layers of a converted CNN on Akida hardware. The threshold mechanism visible in the source is a magnitude comparator, order-agnostic within a layer: it does not matter which input arrived first, only whether the weighted sum crosses a threshold . The honest summary is that Akida's lineage and its input-encoding stage draw on genuine rank-order-coding research, but the converted-CNN inference path that essentially all published Akida benchmarks use is not shown, by any source located, to implement rank order coding's defining order-sensitivity inside hidden layers.

The central finding of this section: reset by subtraction is the single most consistently dropped feature across every surveyed interoperability path to real hardware. NIR's own formal primitive specification encodes only reset to a fixed value for its Integrate-and-Fire and LIF primitives, not reset-by-subtraction .

SpikingJelly is a self-contained illustration of the same problem inside a single codebase. Its ann2snn conversion module defaults to soft reset, v_reset=None, precisely because "ReLU activations in ANNs are strongly related to the firing rates of IF neurons with subtractive reset" . Yet neither of SpikingJelly's own two hardware/interop export paths can accept that output as-is. lava_exchange.py raises ValueError("lava only supports for v_reset == 0!") at five separate call sites , and nir_exchange's to_nir.py raises NotImplementedError("NIR does not distinguish soft reset.") whenever it encounters a soft-reset neuron . A network produced by SpikingJelly's own ann2snn.convert() cannot be handed to SpikingJelly's own Lava or NIR export code without either silently re-parameterizing to hard reset, which reintroduces approximately the twenty-percentage-point accuracy gap Rueckauer et al. measured when comparing the two reset modes on CIFAR-10 , or writing new code that does not currently exist. This self-inconsistency, one framework producing an output its own export paths refuse, is the clearest, most concrete evidence for the survey's broader argument that reset-by-subtraction is the feature interoperability tooling drops first.

6.4 Runtime and Host I/O

NxSDK. Intel's Loihi 1-era runtime, structured around three high-level APIs (the third-party Nengo API, Intel's own low-level NxNet, and the Keras-derived NxTF), all compiling down through a common register-level NxCore API . It is superseded; INRC's own 2022 migration guidance tells members to move to Lava .

Lava ProcessModels. Lava structures computation as CSP-style communicating Processes, each with one or more ProcessModel implementations (e.g. a PyLifModel running on CPU, or a Loihi-targeted model compiled through the proprietary Magma layer). The Process/ProcessModel separation is explicitly extensible: a user can write a custom PyLoihiProcessModel with arbitrary run_spk() dynamics, which in principle makes a subtract-reset neuron implementable, though no source found documents one having been written . Core Lava (the Process API, BSD-3/LGPL-2.1) is open; the Magma components that compile Processes specifically to Loihi hardware remain proprietary to Intel, distributed only as the "Lava extension for Loihi," gated to INRC members .

samna. SynSense's own developer interface and runtime for all SynSense devices (Speck, DYNAP-CNN, Xylo, DYNAP-SE), described by the vendor as running its core in C++ with a Python API layered on top, featuring an event-based stream-filter system for real-time, multi-branch processing of event streams entering or leaving the device . Chip "Models" expose a hardware-agnostic interface: apply_configuration() validates and applies a device configuration; get_source_node() and get_sink_node() stream events to and from the chip . samna is the runtime layer common to both the Sinabs-to-Speck and Rockpool-to-Xylo deployment paths.

SpiNNaker runtime. SpiNNTools generates the full compile pipeline, partitioning an application graph into a machine graph, placing vertices onto specific cores, routing multicast tables, and loading per-core parameter blocks and compiled C executables into each core's tightly-coupled memory . At execution time, the event-driven SpiN1API operating system on each core runs for the configured duration; results are extracted off-machine via the PyNN API .

Host-side encoding and readout. Every platform surveyed requires an explicit host-side step to turn a static image or a continuous-valued vector into whatever the device consumes, and to turn device output back into a classification. For rate-coded static-image conversion, this is Bernoulli/Poisson rate encoding, generated on the host and streamed as timestamped AER events (raster_to_events), a step Sinabs itself documents as separate from the chip's native event stream . Readout is either raw spike accumulation read back over a fixed window and interpreted as a firing-rate vote, or, on Speck, an on-chip readout layer performing majority-vote classification directly on-chip .

7 Applications and Deployment Evidence

This section inventories every published result located for this survey in which a trained network ran on, or was synthesized for, physical or near-physical neuromorphic hardware. Each result is separated by evidence class, E1 through E5, and by whether the network was converted from a pretrained ANN, directly trained with a surrogate gradient, or hand-designed with local plasticity. The pattern that emerges is not a single deployment literature but several disconnected ones, each tied to a specific chip vendor's own toolchain, with the low-T conversion literature almost entirely absent from the physical-silicon record.

7.1 Evidence Classes

A benchmark protocol separates physical evaluation from a proxy so that a future submission can report both honestly. A literature survey has a different task. It must classify claims already in print, including the route evidence that surrounds an accuracy, energy, or latency result. The five classes below refine that distinction. They apply to a claim, not to an entire paper. One paper can therefore contain an E5 accuracy result and an E3 documented route, or an E2 fidelity result without an energy measurement. The classes are not quality grades.

Table 8. Evidence classes for individual deployment claims.
ClassMeaningWhat it does not establish
E1Physical hardware with measured latency, energy, power, or system performance.Equivalence to a different platform, workload, or boundary.
E2Physical hardware with accuracy or fidelity measurement only.Measured latency, power, or energy.
E3A documented but unexercised deployment route.A published run of the model across that route.
E4A hardware model, RTL synthesis, or cycle-level simulation without fabricated silicon.Physical execution.
E5Software simulation, an analytical proxy, or algorithm-only evidence.A hardware capability or deployment measurement.

The classes prevent several recurring substitutions. Fewer timesteps do not prove lower wall-clock latency. Fewer spikes do not prove lower energy. A lower operation count does not prove lower measured power. A documented exporter does not prove that a published model reached silicon. The evidence guide remains fixed throughout the deployment analysis.

7.2 Loihi-family deployments

The NxTF-era conversion results, on physical Loihi 1. Rueckauer et al.'s NxTF compiler took a 4-layer CNN, trained conventionally in Keras, through SNN Toolbox's rate-coded conversion pipeline, and ran it on physical Loihi 1 hardware. On MNIST, the converted network reached 0.79% error at 100 timesteps, using 14 neurocores, measuring 0.66 mJ energy, 6.65 ms delay, and 4.38 µJ·s energy-delay product (EDP) . On CIFAR-10, an off-the-shelf MobileNet converted the same way reached 8.52% error at roughly 400 timesteps, spanning 1,753 neuromorphic cores across 14 Loihi chips, at 102 mJ, 340 ms, and 34,926 µJ·s EDP . The same paper also ran SLAYER-trained (directly trained, not converted) convolutional SNNs on N-MNIST and DVS Gestures on the same physical chip . These are the only classic rate-coded, IF-neuron ANN-to-SNN conversions of a CNN found running on physical Loihi silicon anywhere in this survey, and both are Loihi 1, not Loihi 2.

Massa et al. deployed a gesture-recognition network to physical Loihi 1 the same way, through SNN Toolbox, but the pipeline first accumulates DVS events into frames and trains a conventional DNN on those frames before conversion, so the deployed SNN is a frame-trained network wearing an event-camera dataset, not a native event processor . Measured: 11.43 ms per-frame classification plus 150 ms of preprocessing, 6.24 FPS effective throughput, 89.64% accuracy, 37 cores. QCFS itself cites this paper, along with Singh et al.'s Gesture-SNN, in its related work as prior physical-hardware evidence, making QCFS the single exception among its own lineage to citing deployment work as acknowledged prior art rather than motivational throat-clearing .

The genuine falsifiers. Two papers propose a new conversion method and measure it on physical silicon. Quartz, a time-to-first-spike (TTFS) conversion scheme, deploys to physical Loihi 1 and reports a like-for-like, on-chip EDP comparison against a rate-coded baseline also run on Loihi: 0.9 µJ·s versus 4.38 µJ·s on MNIST, 10.3 mJ·s versus roughly 34.9 mJ·s on CIFAR-10 . Brehove et al. convert a post-training-quantized ANN into a Sigma-Delta Neural Network and deploy it on a physical 16-chip Loihi 2 VPX board, benchmarking a YOLOv3-style detector against a Jetson Xavier . Both papers abandon rate coding for a platform-native code, temporal single-spike coding for Quartz and graded sigma-delta spikes for Brehove, and no paper was found that keeps rate coding, proposes a new conversion algorithm, and reaches physical silicon.

Directly trained networks reaching Loihi through a vendor toolchain. Shrestha et al.'s PilotNet steering-angle regression network and accompanying audio-denoising networks, trained natively with Lava-DL (SLAYER and bootstrap), not converted from a separately pretrained ANN, ran on physical Loihi 2 with energy, latency, and EDP measured against a Jetson Orin Nano and an Intel i9 . Boeshertz et al. mapped a converted sigma-delta-neuron RNN to physical Loihi 1 with 3-bit weights for speech classification . Mészáros, Knight, Timcheck, and Nowotny trained an SNN with synaptic delays using EventProp (an exact-gradient method that maps onto event-driven hardware by construction), then deployed it to a physical single-chip Loihi 2, measuring latency around 1.46 to 1.54 ms and total energy of 0.28 to 0.46 mJ per inference on keyword-spotting benchmarks, an 18-fold speedup and 250-fold energy reduction over a Jetson Orin Nano at batch size 1 . A continual-learning system (CLP-SNN) that adapts on-chip via a local three-factor rule reached 0.33 ms latency and 0.05 mJ total energy per inference on physical Loihi 2, 113 times lower latency and 6,600 times lower energy than a Jetson Orin Nano baseline . A 2026 preprint trained an object detector with SpikingJelly, exported it through a Lava-DL-compatible format, and benchmarked it on physical Loihi 2 against Jetson Orin Nano, Jetson Nano B01, and Apple M2 . A separate SpikingJelly-trained DVS128 Gesture network reached physical, FPGA-based CRI neuromorphic hardware through the hs_api conversion path, an independent, non-Loihi hardware target .

The hand-designed, local-plasticity body, the largest E1 concentration on Loihi. None of this work is gradient-trained, converted, or QCFS-lineage.

Imam and Cleland's olfactory circuit, trained by one-shot local plasticity rather than backpropagation, ran on physical Loihi with 2.75 ms per sniff and 0.43 mJ total energy per classification, reported at 92% accuracy .

A 2023 replication study found the underlying dataset suffers from sensor drift and a non-randomized measurement protocol, and that a simple hash-table baseline matches the network's accuracy under a proper train/test split, a caution against treating the hardware measurement as evidence for the algorithmic claim even though the latency and energy figures themselves are not in question .

Tang, Shah, and Michmizos's biologically constrained head-direction circuit for unidimensional SLAM ran on physical Loihi with dynamic power 100 times lower than GMapping on a CPU . The Locally Competitive Algorithm, a provably convergent spiking solver for LASSO, appeared in Loihi's own introductory paper with an EDP improvement of more than three orders of magnitude over an Atom CPU running LARS/FISTA , and was subsequently extended to Loihi 2 for sparse coding and convolutional sparse coding . A Boolean satisfiability solver encoded as an SNN ran on physical Loihi, the first reported CSP/SAT implementation on embedded neuromorphic hardware .

Directly trained (gradient-based) robotics, audio, EEG, and radar work. A spiking actor network co-trained end-to-end with a deep critic via a surrogate-gradient extension of STBP drove mapless robot navigation on a physical Kapoho Bay Loihi board, reaching 75 times lower energy per inference than DDPG on a Jetson TX2 . An industrial peg-in-hole force-control policy, trained via spiking reinforcement learning in simulation, ran on both physical Loihi 1 and Loihi 2, at 1.8 ms and 1.5 ms latency respectively and 53 µJ dynamic energy per inference on Loihi 2, against roughly 3,800 µJ on a CPU . Kreiser et al.'s hand-designed path-integration circuit for iCub head-pose estimation ran on Kapoho Bay with a closed loop under 10 ms, though only accuracy, not energy, was reported . Recurrent SNNs for EMG gesture classification reached 983 times lower energy and 19 times lower latency than a GPU at batch size 50 on a Nahuku 32 board , and a separate implementation on a Kapoho Bay board reached 74% accuracy at 5.7 ms and 41 mW . A tri-modal EMG-plus-DVS sign-language classifier spanning Loihi and the ODIN/MorphIC chips matched GPU accuracy with a 30 to 600 times better energy-delay product . A graded-SNN seizure predictor reached 99.14% accuracy and 25.1 mJ per input on Loihi 2 ; a separate motor-imagery decoder reported a self-described 95% energy advantage over DNNs, not independently re-verified in this pass . A vehicle-mounted automotive radar pipeline ran real-time, on-chip FFT, non-coherent integration, and CFAR detection on Loihi 2, the first such deployment on real (not simulated) radar data , and a separate spiking-resonator radar pipeline on simulated data measured full-activation power at 2.62 W with activity-gated sparsity cutting energy roughly 60% . Keyword spotting on Loihi beat CPU, GPU, Jetson TX1, and Movidius NCS baselines at equivalent accuracy, with the advantage growing with network size .

Toolchain status. NxSDK/NxTF targeted Loihi 1 only and is superseded. Lava, Lava-DL, and NetX targeted Loihi 2 and were the documented forward path from 2021 onward, but all lava-nc repositories were archived by Intel around May 2026, with Intel stating it is developing a next-generation Loihi architecture and SDK rather than continuing Lava . No published, peer-reviewed paper was found running a classic rate-coded, IF-neuron, ANN-to-SNN-converted CNN, the style the QCFS lineage established, on physical Loihi 2 silicon. The closest analogues are Brehove et al.'s sigma-delta conversion, a different, non-rate-coded scheme purpose-built to avoid rate coding's inefficiency on Loihi 2, and the NxTF rate-coded conversions, which ran on Loihi 1, not Loihi 2 . Intel's own technical brief states typical use of Loihi 2's 32-bit graded-spike payload at 8-bit precision, "a negligible extra energy cost compared to binary spike processing" , and Davies et al.'s survey states directly why: rate-coded feedforward conversion needs more timesteps as layer count grows, and large networks spanning multiple chips congest inter-chip mesh links that carry roughly 30 times less bandwidth than on-chip links, making converted rate-coded models "less attractive for neuromorphic architectures optimized for sparsity" on Loihi 2 specifically .

7.3 DYNAP-CNN and Speck event-vision deployments

DYNAP-CNN is the processor core, and Speck is a system-on-chip integrating DYNAP-CNN with an on-chip dynamic vision sensor on one die . Both expose nine configurable computing cores, each a fixed Conv2d → Integrate-and-Fire → optional SumPool2d pipeline stage, with no global clock driving the convolutional path . Richter et al.'s characterization of the fabricated Speck1 ASIC measured an end-to-end latency of 3.36 µs for a single event traversing all nine sCNN layers, and 1.58 µs through one layer, explicitly contrasted against clocked chips: "Speck1 is specifically built to consume real-time data, its latency is significantly lower than Loihi1/2 and TrueNorth which architecturally introduce a latency of one timestep Δt on event generation in each layer" . A software-side num_timesteps in the Sinabs toolchain only exists at the boundary where a rate-coded raster is converted into individually timestamped events; once on-chip, the network has no notion of a timestep and reacts continuously to each event's arrival .

Yao et al.'s Nature Communications characterization of the fabricated Speck chip measured real-time power as low as 0.70 mW and resting power of 0.42 mW across DVS128 Gesture, Gait-day, Gait-night, and HAR-DVS, with input masking trading accuracy for power (for example, plus 9.0 percentage points on Gesture at 50% masking, at 3.8 mW versus 9.5 mW unmasked) . Liu et al.'s live face-recognition demonstration on the DynapCNN predecessor chip reported sub-mW power and a 98% recognition rate on a recorded DVS dataset, though the accuracy figure was validated against a software simulation rather than measured live on-chip . A book-chapter comparison of a small CNN on a DynapCNN devkit against a Kendryte K210 and an STM32L4R9 used the toolchain's raster_to_events path to feed a genuinely rate-coded, static-image MNIST classifier to physical silicon, reaching 98.79% balanced accuracy with energy estimated from synaptic operation counts rather than directly measured on a power sensor . No object-detection network was found measured on physical DYNAP-CNN or Speck silicon anywhere in this search.

The weight resolution on DYNAP-CNN and Speck is 8 bits, and membrane-potential state is 16 bits . The default neuron is nonleaky integrate-and-fire with reset-by-subtraction, the same reset convention the classical rate-coding conversion literature uses . There is no native BatchNorm layer type; Speck- targeted networks are built without BatchNorm from the start rather than relying on a fold-in utility . SynSense's separate Xylo product line, for audio and IMU rather than vision, is genuinely clocked and synchronous, unlike DYNAP-CNN and Speck, and its own published keyword-spotting deployment reached 95% accuracy at 6.6 µJ dynamic energy per inference on physical XyloAudio 2 silicon, beating Loihi and SpiNNaker2 on the same benchmark table .

7.4 SpiNNaker and BrainScaleS deployments

SpiNNaker. sPyNNaker's software stack exposes a documented SNN Toolbox backend, and the clearest physical result is Patiño-Saucedo et al.'s conventional LeNet, trained in Keras, converted with SNN Toolbox's rate-coded threshold-balancing pipeline, and deployed on a physical SpiNNaker-103 machine (48 chips, 864 ARM cores). The full 10,000-sample MNIST test set was propagated through the physical hardware, reaching 98.20% accuracy, with 15 ms of recorded spiking activity per sample and a measured wall-clock inference time of roughly 0.4 s per sample, against about 10 s per sample for a NEST software simulation of the identical PyNN model . A companion STBP spiking CNN for event-based N-MNIST, directly trained rather than converted, reached 97.92% on the same physical machine . An earlier DBN-to-SNN conversion (rate-coded, Poisson pixel encoding) ran on a single physical SpiNNaker chip, reaching 95.01% accuracy, 0.3 W power, and roughly 20 ms latency . A design-space-exploration study reported 98.85% accuracy on a physical SpiNNaker target with a faster hyperparameter search, though the latency and energy figures were not confirmed in the retrieved text . A motor-imagery EEG decoder mapped directly to a spiking CNN and deployed on physical SpiNNaker reached 73.72% against 75.63% for the original CNN, a 1.91-point accuracy drop from conversion and deployment . No CIFAR-10-scale or larger, VGG- or ResNet-class rate-coded CNN conversion was found running on physical SpiNNaker 1 or SpiNNaker 2 silicon anywhere in this search. The one VGG-16/ResNet-50-scale SpiNNaker2 result, Kelber et al.'s mapping study, reports 43.5 ms and 19.5 ms inference times, but the paper states plainly that "as the SpiNNaker2 chip is not yet available, we developed a Python simulator that replicates the timings," making the reported figures a pre-silicon timing simulation, not a hardware measurement . Arfa et al. deployed natively trained, 8-bit quantized SNNs on a physical single-chip SpiNNaker2 test board for DVS Gesture recognition, reaching 94.13% on-chip against roughly 94.6% in floating-point software, an SNN-native deployment rather than a converted static-image CNN .

BrainScaleS. Schmitt et al. converted a deep ReLU network to a spiking network on the BrainScaleS-1 wafer-scale analog system using explicit rate coding, disabling the AdEx model's adaptation and exponential features so the analog neuron approximates a ReLU . Direct transfer without retraining collapsed accuracy from 97% (software) to 72% on a handwritten-digit classification task, because analog device mismatch and trial-to-trial variation distort the rate-coded correspondence. Hardware- in-the-loop training, computing gradients in software using the ReLU derivative as an approximation of the untractable hardware activation function, recovered accuracy to 95%, close to the software model . The paper is explicit that the algorithm had no exact knowledge of the neuron and synapse parameters on the analog substrate, and that using the ReLU derivative as a stand-in circumvented rather than solved the problem of an intractable analog activation function .

BrainScaleS-2's CNN-scale demonstrations use a non-spiking, analog matrix-multiply mode, not spike-rate conversion. The same physical substrate can be reconfigured, by disabling neuron leak and choosing the reset point so the ADC saturates at the reset voltage, into a stateless analog vector-matrix multiplier rather than a genuine multi-timestep spiking integrator . A three-layer CNN classifying MNIST in this "hagen" mode, with weights and activations quantized to 6 and 5 bits, went from 98.29% in float32 software to 98.10% after quantization, collapsed to 92.13% on direct, uncorrected transfer to the chip, and recovered to 98.01% after hardware-in-the-loop training . A deployed BrainScaleS-2 mobile system running this same non-spiking mode for ECG atrial-fibrillation detection measured 276 µs classification time and 192 µJ ASIC energy per sample at 5.6 W system power, with a 93.7% detection rate . A separate line of work trains genuinely spiking, surrogate- gradient networks directly on BrainScaleS-2, not by conversion, reaching 97.6% MNIST accuracy at 8 µs latency and 2.4 µJ per classified image on physical silicon . No source was found reporting a rate-coded, spike-count IF conversion of a CNN at CIFAR-10 or ImageNet scale running on any BrainScaleS system; every BrainScaleS deployment above that reaches usable accuracy does so through hardware-in-the- loop retraining, not a one-shot convert-and-deploy pipeline.

7.5 FPGA and custom ASIC implementations

Board-measured (E1). SyncNN explicitly targets rate-encoding IF-model SNNs with reset-by-subtraction, stating directly, "we focus on the IF model: the membrane potential is just added or subtracted by the weight of the connection... and is reset immediately once it reaches the threshold value," and proves a synchronous reformulation that runs downstream layers once rather than for the full timestep window . Measured across three physical Xilinx ARM-FPGA boards (ZCU102, ZCU104, ZED), it reaches 99.6% accuracy and 13,086 FPS on MNIST, its headline throughput claim, with SVHN and CIFAR-10 also evaluated at 16-, 8-, and 4-bit weight quantization . Spiker+ generalizes an earlier LIF-only FPGA accelerator into a configurable multi-layer architecture offering six neuron models, IF, first-order LIF, and second-order LIF, each with a choice of hard or subtractive reset, the first FPGA accelerator surveyed to offer reset-by-subtraction as a named, user-selectable option matching the QCFS-style IF neuron . Measured on physical Xilinx FPGA synthesis and deployment, it reaches 93.85% on MNIST at 780 µs and 180 mW using a 100-timestep encoding window, and 95% on AudioMNIST at 290 mW . Cerebron is the clearest FPGA accelerator explicitly built around the ANN-to-SNN conversion literature, stating directly that it uses "an open-source SNNtoolbox" for conversion . Measured on a physical Xilinx XC7Z100 FPGA at 200 MHz, it reaches 99.40% on an MNIST ConvNet at 0.026 ms and 0.04 mJ per image, 91.90% on a thinned MobileNet for CIFAR-10 at 10.63 ms and 14.88 mJ, and 97.30% on a lane-segmentation SegNet at 0.80 ms and 1.12 mJ .

Synthesized but unfabricated (E4). PASCAL proves an exact, integer-level equivalence between a QCFS-activated ANN and an IF-neuron SNN using a spike-accumulation neuron with soft reset, and reports a hardware-efficiency result derived from an analytical operation-count model, not a synthesized circuit . Its acknowledgments separately credit a collaborator for benchmarking the work on an unnamed "neuromorphic architectural simulator," but no simulator result is reported in the paper body, so PASCAL's own hardware-adjacent claim remains E5 with an unreported E4 gesture . APEX takes PASCAL's spike-accumulation neuron and implements its full spike-generation datapath as a fully combinational circuit inside the LoAS accelerator framework, synthesized with Synopsys Design Compiler at 400 MHz on 40 nm CMOS, evaluated by cycle-level simulation rather than fabricated silicon, and reports a 61.9% energy reduction at matched accuracy against a standard, non-PASC IF neuron baseline . NeuroFlex independently extends PASCAL's neuron to column-level hybrid ANN/SNN scheduling within a layer, synthesized with Synopsys Design Compiler at 560 MHz on the same 40 nm node, again evaluated by RTL synthesis and a Python cycle-level simulator rather than a fabricated chip, reporting PE-array utilization gains from fine-grained scheduling . APEX and NeuroFlex both build directly on PASCAL's spike-accumulation neuron and reuse near-identical LoAS and SparTen baselines and Synopsys synthesis methodology; APEX shares a co-author, Gopalakrishnan Srinivasan, with PASCAL itself . None of the three has been fabricated. The board-measured accelerators above, SyncNN, Spiker+, and Cerebron, are E1 because a physical device ran the network and reported the number; PASCAL, APEX, and NeuroFlex are E4 at best because Synopsys Design Compiler synthesis and a cycle-level simulator, however precise, are not a fabricated chip.

7.6 Event-based sensing and what actually computes on the events

Most published event-vision pipelines do not process events natively. A 2025 survey states this directly: "most event-based deep learning pipelines convert asynchronous events into dense representations (particularly voxel grids) before neural network processing" . Of the six representation families a comprehensive event-vision survey catalogs, image-based, surface-based, learning-based, voxel-based, graph-based, and spike-based, only the last is native to spiking hardware, and the other five collapse the asynchronous stream into a dense tensor handed to a conventional CNN or transformer . Concretely, Prophesee's own production object-detection pipeline accumulates events into a frame buffer before calling a conventional TorchScript network on CPU or GPU ; the pedestrian-detection literature frames its central research question as frame CNNs versus asynchronous sparse CNNs, with frame reconstruction remaining one of exactly two mainstream options ; and even a "Loihi paper," Massa et al.'s gesture recognition, states plainly that events must first be collected into frames and used to train a DNN before that DNN is converted for deployment .

Where the survey looked across roughly twenty application papers spanning optical flow, detection, automotive, SLAM, high-speed robotics, and gesture recognition, the compute platform behind the majority is a GPU or workstation for algorithm development, an embedded CPU, Raspberry Pi, or Jetson for the majority of real-time onboard robotics demos, and an FPGA running a conventional, non-spiking CNN accelerator (the AMD/Xilinx DPU, in most cases) for a distinct and fairly common third category, especially for drones and SLAM . A vendor's own production reference designs make the pattern explicit. Prophesee's GenX320 sensor documentation states its CPI/AER output path "is mainly destined for integration with neuromorphic systems," implying the sensor's default, primary output path is not, and the vendor's own reference kits target a Raspberry Pi 5 and an STM32 microcontroller, not a spiking chip .

Some processors marketed as neuromorphic are confirmed non-spiking by their own makers. GrAI Matter Labs is the explicit case. An EE Times interview with the company states directly, "while GrAI Matter describes its technology as 'brain-inspired,' its NeuronFlow technology is based on a digital SoC architecture optimized for deep learning acceleration (no spiking networks here)" . NeuronFlow's core primitive performs change-based, delta-quantized activations of a conventional ANN, exploiting temporal sparsity inside an otherwise ordinary MAC-based dataflow accelerator, and its own architecture paper describes its events as "valued events... not simple spikes as usual in neuromorphic systems" . A conventional Xilinx DPU fed by an event sensor, as in the Kria-Prophesee object-detection pipeline and the FPGA-drone obstacle-avoidance work, is architecturally an ordinary systolic-array CNN accelerator with zero spiking neurons; from the DPU's perspective, an event camera is just a low-latency frame source .

Genuine sensor-to-spiking-chip pipelines exist but are a minority, concentrated in gesture recognition and simple closed-loop control. IBM's TrueNorth-plus-DVS128 gesture recognizer is the clearest landmark instance, a live DVS event stream feeding a TrueNorth spiking CNN with no host-side frame accumulation in the deployed path, measuring 105 ms onset latency, under 200 mW, and 96.5% accuracy . SynSense's Speck reports vendor-measured sub-5 mW power and sub-50 ms response for gesture recognition with no frame reconstruction , and the GelNeuro tactile system feeds DVS-like marker-motion events directly into a physical Speck2f spiking CNN, reaching 96.3% accuracy at 80 ms and 19.6 mW . DYNAP-SE closes a spiking control loop directly against an iCub robot's joint encoder , and a separate DYNAP-SE deployment localizes touch on an e-skin at 185 µW median inference power . A SpiNNaker-driven goalkeeper robot closes an event-camera-to-servo loop fully on-chip, disconnected from any PC, at 6.5 ms response latency and 85% interception accuracy . Outside this cluster of gesture recognition and simple closed-loop control, event-vision applications, SLAM, optical flow, automotive detection, and high-speed catching or robotics, essentially all route through a conventional compute platform after frame reconstruction.

7.8 Measurement and comparability

Reported efficiency numbers across this literature are not comparable to one another, for reasons that recur across every platform surveyed above.

SynOps estimates establish an operation-count ratio, not joules. The "0.9 pJ MAC, 0.1 pJ AC" figures cited across most SNN papers trace to a single ISSCC 2014 plenary talk's Table 1.1.9, reporting rough energy costs for a 45 nm process at 0.9 V . The talk itself stresses that computation is a minority of total system energy once memory access is included, and that a programmable processor's per-instruction overhead, roughly 70 pJ per instruction from fetch and register clocking, dwarfs the per-operation figure entirely . Multiple independent, hardware-realistic studies confirm the gap this produces is large and systematic. Bhattacharjee et al. benchmark published SNN algorithms on two hardware-realistic accelerator models and find "the actual energy-efficiency improvements of recent SNN algorithmic works differ significantly from their estimated values due to various hardware bottlenecks" . Shen et al. propose replacing the timestep-versus-bit-width asymmetry with a unified "Bit Budget" metric precisely because SNN papers are routinely compared against unquantized, rather than similarly optimized, ANN baselines . A SynOps-based energy number establishes, at best, a relative operation-count ratio under an implicit, usually unstated hardware model, one process node, one precision, no memory-access cost, no neuron-state-update cost, and no routing cost.

Static and leakage power dominate measured energy on synchronous chips, so reducing T does not reduce energy proportionally. A sigma-delta conversion study on Loihi 2 varies effective sparsity and reports throughput improving substantially, from 176 to 362 FPS, while "total power usage... remained relatively consistent for all experiments as it is dominated by static draw" . A separate LCA study on Loihi 2 measures static power essentially constant near 0.54 W across all sparsity settings, nearly double the dynamic power even at the sparsest, most favorable operating point . On a synchronous, clocked digital chip with SRAM-resident weights and state, static power is drawn continuously for the full duration a network is mapped and powered, independent of how much useful spiking work a given timestep performs, so cutting T reduces only the dynamic term and can even increase energy per inference if the reduction also reduces the throughput benefit of parallelism across a fixed static floor.

Measurement-boundary choices alone produce large swings for the identical chip and network. Shrestha et al.'s PilotNet benchmark on Loihi 2 reports 0.09 mJ total energy and 1.21 ms latency with weights pre-staged and no host I/O counted, against 1.26 mJ and 65.41 ms with streaming host I/O included, a 14-fold difference in energy and a 54-fold difference in latency for the same chip and the same network, purely as a function of where the measurement window is drawn . Static power dominates the IO-limited configuration so completely that over 94% of the reported 1.26 mJ is static or leakage, not spiking computation . Intel's own recommended reporting convention, followed by later independent papers citing the same source, reports static, dynamic, and total power separately and computes EDP from the total, precisely because a headline "dynamic energy" figure by construction excludes the leakage contribution that this boundary problem shows can dominate the true total .

When neuromorphic hardware wins and when it does not, in the architects' own words. Davies et al.'s survey of Loihi results states the finding directly in its abstract: "conventional feedforward deep neural networks show modest if any benefit on Loihi," while "more brain-inspired networks using recurrence, precise spike-timing relationships, synaptic plasticity, stochasticity, and sparsity perform certain computation with orders of magnitude lower latency and energy" . The same paper states that it "would be naïve to expect SNNs to outperform ANN accelerators on the very task that they have been optimized for," and that per-synaptic-operation energy on Loihi is in practice greater than a MAC on a dedicated ANN accelerator, because of the overhead of supporting sparse, event-driven, time-and-space-sparse computation that a dense accelerator does not have to pay for .

Quantizing the ANN baseline alone closes much of the reported gap. Shrestha et al. report both floating-point and int8-quantized ANN baselines for PilotNet on a Jetson Orin Nano alongside the Loihi 2 int8 result. Quantizing the ANN from fp32 to int8 at batch size 1 on the identical GPU cuts its energy from 21.94 mJ to 13.72 mJ, roughly 37%, and cuts its energy-delay product from 126.69 µJ·s to 47.08 µJ·s, a 63% reduction, before Loihi's own numbers change at all . Loihi 2 still shows an order-of-magnitude-plus advantage over the int8, batch-1 ANN baseline for this specific workload, but a substantial fraction of the advantage claimed against an unquantized baseline evaporates once the ANN side is quantized to the same precision the SNN uses, and the workload itself, a small regression CNN with sparse sigma-delta activations, sits close to the sparse, low-dynamic-range regime Davies et al. identify as Loihi's favorable case, not a generic large-scale classifier .

8 Challenges and Future Directions

Each subsection below is a seam: a point where a trained network passes from one layer of the stack to the next and something is lost. Each becomes a numbered pin in the survey's main figure, linking the layer bands to the specific mechanism that breaks at that handoff.

8.1 Operator coverage in SNN toolchains

Every documented software path from a trained network to physical silicon accepts a narrower set of operators than a standard vision CNN uses, and closing the gap is architectural rewriting, not configuration.

Every documented software path from a trained network to physical silicon accepts a narrower set of operators than a standard vision CNN uses, and closing the gap is architectural rewriting, not configuration.

SpikingJelly's lava_exchange module, the only first-party bridge from SpikingJelly to Loihi, dispatches on layer type and handles exactly four cases: nn.Linear, nn.Conv2d, nn.AvgPool2d (rewritten internally as a Lava SumPool2d), and nn.Flatten. Anything else raises ValueError(type(net[i])) or NotImplementedError(type(net[i])) . A VGG-family network hits this immediately, since VGG's canonical pooling is nn.MaxPool2d, which the dispatch does not handle. A ResNet hits it at every residual addition, since no elementwise-add or skip-connection handling appears in the operator dispatch, and again at any BatchNorm2d layer not already fused away before export . The module also asserts no bias term on exported Conv2d/Linear layers and rejects any LIF neuron with decay_input=True .

Sinabs, SynSense's PyTorch library for the Speck/DYNAP-CNN line, has no built-in BatchNorm folding. Its DynapcnnLayer template is fixed to a conv → spike → pool sequence, and the discretize module's public functions (discretize_conv, discretize_conv_spike, discretize_spk) operate only on (Conv2d, IAF) pairs; there is no discretize_bn or fold_bn counterpart . Sinabs's own hardware-targeted tutorials avoid the problem by construction, building bias-free convolutional layers followed directly by IF layers with no BatchNorm in the architecture at all, which places the burden of BN removal on the user before the model reaches from_model() . A Conv2d bias, where present, is repurposed on-chip as the per-timestep leak current rather than an ordinary additive bias, a further departure from what a standard trained CNN expects .

BrainChip's Akida, evaluated here only as a constraint-list illustration and not as a deployment target this survey otherwise covers, documents perhaps the most exhaustive operator contract of any platform reviewed. Input width must fall between 5 and 256 pixels; exceeding 256 forces the convolutional layers back onto software execution, a silent performance cliff rather than a hard failure. Input channels are restricted to 1 or 3. Activations must be a bounded ReLU (ReLU(max_value=6.0)) expressed as a separate layer; an unbounded QuantizedReLU raises ValueError: unbounded QuantizedReLU is not supported in AkidaVersion.v1. All convolutional layers after the first must use 'same' padding only, a 'valid'-padded intermediate layer raises RuntimeError, and no dilated or grouped convolutions are supported at all. Dense layers must receive a spatially flattened input, with total input features capped at 57,334 . This is the contract of a quantized, integer-only CNN accelerator with a strict graph-compiler front end, comparable in spirit to a TFLite Micro or TensorRT deployment target, not a general-purpose spiking-network simulator willing to accept an arbitrary topology .

The older SNN Toolbox pipeline, by contrast, supports max-pooling natively, offering a choice of three spiking approximations (fir_max, exp_max, avg_max), and extracts BatchNorm parameters directly from the source Keras layer for fusion into the preceding convolution . Its operator coverage is broader on exactly the two axes (max-pooling, BatchNorm) that the newer, purpose-built export paths above either drop or push back onto the user. No toolchain surveyed accepts a standard VGG or ResNet as trained; every one requires either an architectural rewrite before conversion (avg-pool in place of max-pool, no BatchNorm, bounded activations, same-padding-only convolutions) or a bespoke translation step after it.

8.2 Reset-semantics mismatch

Despite this, the interoperability layer built after the fact does not carry the mechanism.

This is the sharpest finding in the survey. ANN-to-SNN conversion under IF neurons requires reset by subtraction, not reset to a fixed value. Rueckauer et al. derive analytically that reset to zero discards a systematic, input-dependent residual charge at every spike, a bias that compounds across layers rather than averaging out with longer simulation; reset by subtraction instead carries that residual into the next integration window, and the firing-rate estimator converges exactly to the target ReLU activation as T → ∞ . Their own CIFAR-10 ablation, switching only the reset mechanism while holding Poisson input and weight normalization fixed, improves accuracy by roughly twenty percentage points . This result is now treated as settled practice throughout the conversion literature .

Despite this, the interoperability layer built after the fact does not carry the mechanism. NIR's own primitive specification defines the reset rule for its Integrate-and-Fire and LIF primitives as reset to a fixed target value (v ← v_reset if spiking, v otherwise); the published primitive table contains no subtract-reset primitive . The gap is not theoretical. snnTorch's neuron classes internally implement subtract-reset but historically exported v_reset = 0 to NIR, silently discarding the distinction, a bug tracked in two separate pull requests against the reference exporter . SpikingJelly's own exporters go further and refuse the case outright: lava_exchange.py raises ValueError('lava only supports for v_reset == 0!') at five separate call sites, and asserts the same for its CubaLIFNode path; its nir_exchange/to_nir.py raises NotImplementedError("NIR does not distinguish soft reset.") whenever v_reset is None . A network converted by SpikingJelly's own ann2snn module, which itself defaults to soft reset, cannot be handed to either of SpikingJelly's own export modules without either re-parameterizing to hard reset, and reintroducing the twenty-point cost above, or writing new code .

On hardware, the picture splits three ways. Loihi 1 and Loihi 2 hard-reset the membrane voltage to zero natively, in a single compartment . NxTF's compiler works around this with a two-compartment neuron: one compartment plays the soma, the second is recurrently connected and activates only once the soma crosses threshold, then inhibits the soma by an amount equal to the threshold, reproducing subtraction at the cost of doubling the compartment count per neuron . The paper's own authors recommend that future hardware add a built-in soft reset specifically to eliminate this duplication . SpiNNaker is not hard-wired to either mode; its ARM cores are fully software-programmable, and a neuron_model_has_spiked() callback lets a user implement subtraction in a custom C neuron model, but this is not one of sPyNNaker's built-in cell types (IF_curr_exp and its relatives use conventional PyNN hard-reset semantics), so soft reset on SpiNNaker means writing and compiling a bespoke neuron kernel . BrainScaleS-2's native analog AdEx circuit clamps the membrane to a fixed reset potential for a refractory period on every spike, a hard reset by construction; no source describes a native or workaround soft-reset mode on BrainScaleS-2, despite the chip's otherwise extensive multi-compartment capability, which is used there for genuine dendritic computation rather than as a reset workaround .

Native support exists in three places. TrueNorth's digital neuron model implements a "linear mode" reset as a first-class, natively configurable hardware option, in which the residual potential above threshold is not discarded but carried forward exactly as reset-by-subtraction requires . Speck/DYNAP-CNN exposes the choice as a single hardware register, return_to_zero; False selects subtractive reset and is the devkit's documented default, with SynSense's own guidance explicitly directing ANN-to-SNN conversion users to that setting . FPGA-targeted SNN accelerator generators (Spiker+, SIA) support subtractive reset as a synthesizable, and in SIA's case preferred, option because it measurably improves classification accuracy . Sinabs, the software library targeting Speck, defaults its IAF layer to MembraneSubtract(), matching the hardware default . Taken together, the mechanism proven worth roughly twenty accuracy points is the single most consistently dropped feature across every interoperability path to accessible hardware surveyed in this report.

8.3 Input encoding and output readout

The consequence for hardware is that the first layer is, in effect, an ANN layer.

Direct (analog, constant-current) input encoding applies the raw, real-valued input at every timestep as a constant current to the first hidden layer, rather than first converting it into a spike train. Rueckauer et al. introduce this explicitly as one of three mechanisms, alongside reset-by-subtraction and robust weight normalization, that close the ANN-SNN accuracy gap: replacing Poisson spike-train input with constant analog current for the first layer alone raised CIFAR-10 accuracy from roughly 59.8 percent to 83.6 percent, holding weight normalization and reset-to-zero fixed . Deterministic count coding under a fixed threshold achieves quantization error that scales as 1/T, while Poisson coding's variance scales only as 1/√T, so Poisson input needs quadratically more timesteps for equivalent precision . This is the direct reason essentially all modern low-latency conversion work, including the QCFS lineage, uses direct encoding at the input layer rather than Poisson.

The consequence for hardware is that the first layer is, in effect, an ANN layer. Because its computation is a real-valued multiply-accumulate rather than an accumulate over binary spikes, it forfeits the accumulate-only efficiency the rest of the network enjoys; one Eyeriss-scale energy study measured rate coding costing roughly fifty percent less energy than direct coding specifically because the direct-encoded first layer carries sixteen-bit precision . Toolchains that document this explicitly route the first layer off the spiking core entirely: Nengo-Loihi's own tooling notes that the input-encoding layer "should run off-chip" , and FPGA accelerator designs place a conventional MAC array ahead of the spiking core rather than attempt to spike it .

No source located confirms or denies whether Speck, Xylo, BrainScaleS-2, or SpiNNaker support a direct-encoded first layer as a hardware-native path; there is no architectural reason it should be infeasible, since a constant input current is a basic capability, but no worked deployment example was found, so the gap is recorded as unconfirmed rather than assumed closed .

Readout cost is not free either. SNN Toolbox's default output decoding is temporal_mean_rate, spike count divided by T, with temporal_pattern and three TTFS-family variants offered as alternatives . TTFS decoding instead reads a winner-take-all over first-spike time, or a learned temporal-weighting decoder over decayed contributions . The decision window is not a modeling abstraction; it is real latency paid on the chip. NxTF's CIFAR-10 run on physical Loihi 1 required a 340 ms per-sample decision delay at 102 mJ per sample . Quartz's TTFS conversion trades this differently: measured on the same chip family, it reaches 0.9 µJ·s energy-delay product on MNIST against 4.38 µJ·s for a rate-coded Loihi baseline, but its CIFAR-10 accuracy (25.14 percent error) is markedly worse than the rate-coded NxTF result (8.52 percent error), even though the TTFS run uses far fewer total spikes . Reducing the readout window is a real design axis with a real accuracy cost, not a free efficiency lever.

8.4 Time-model mismatch

A T value reported in a software simulation paper is therefore not a portable physical quantity across platforms, and on a fully event-driven chip there may be no hardware T to map it onto at all.

T names three physically different things depending on the platform. Under a synchronous tick, exemplified by SynSense's own Xylo line, the hardware genuinely advances all state once per a global dt; up to fifteen input events per channel and up to thirty-one output spikes per neuron can be processed within a single tick, and T corresponds to a real, chip-wide clock edge . Under an asynchronous event stream, exemplified by SynSense's own Speck line, there is no clock in the compute path at all: computation is triggered directly by changes arriving at each block, communicated via a request/acknowledge protocol, and a measured per-layer propagation latency of roughly 0.37 µs (3.36 µs across nine layers) is a hardware propagation delay, not a multiple of any chosen simulation Δt . The paper's own comparison table draws this out explicitly against clocked chips: "Loihi1/2 and TrueNorth... which architecturally introduce a latency of one timestep Δt on event generation in each layer," a delay stated as 1 ms for TrueNorth's clocked design . Under analog continuous time, exemplified by BrainScaleS-2, the substrate runs accelerated, physical-model dynamics at roughly ten-thousand times real time, with no discretized tick at all .

The Speck-versus-Xylo contrast is instructive precisely because it sits inside one vendor's product line. On Xylo, a software num_timesteps/dt parameter is a hardware-level guarantee: the chip is architecturally a synchronous time-stepped machine, and per-layer heterogeneous T is physically impossible as currently built . On Speck, the equivalent software parameter (Sinabs's num_timesteps) exists only at the boundary where a discrete-time raster is converted into individually timestamped address-event-representation spikes; past that conversion point the chip has no notion of a timestep whatsoever, and it reacts continuously to each event's arrival regardless of how many software timesteps were used to construct the input stream . The only clocked element anywhere in the Speck2e-and-later compute path is an auxiliary slow clock governing leak, DVS-filter, and readout-averaging behavior, not the convolutional datapath itself . A T value reported in a software simulation paper is therefore not a portable physical quantity across platforms, and on a fully event-driven chip there may be no hardware T to map it onto at all.

8.5 Per-layer timestep horizons have no execution model

No framework surveyed expresses a per-layer timestep horizon, layer A running T=2 while layer B runs T=4, in a single forward pass. SpikingJelly drives every layer from one shared scalar T, either through a user-written for t in range(T) loop in single-step mode or a shared [T, N, *] tensor in multi-step mode . snnTorch, Norse, and Lava follow the identical pattern: one external loop, one sequence length, one RunSteps(total_run_time) condition applied to the whole compiled process graph . Rockpool/Xylo enforces this at the hardware level, not merely in software, since the chip is architecturally a single global-clock machine (8.4). PyNN inherits whatever global-clock convention its chosen backend uses. The underlying reason is architectural, stated most plainly in SpikingJelly's own paper: because a stateful spiking layer's output at time t depends on hidden state carried from t-1, t-2, ..., 0, "the for-loop concerning time steps is inevitable," and every framework builds exactly one such loop per network, not one per layer .

NeuroScale (Li, Imam, and Manohar, *Nature Communications* 16, 10329, 2025) confirms it architecturally for the four most-cited large-scale digital neuromorphic platforms: "The TrueNorth system uses an externally controlled signal that is distributed to all the hardware components of the architecture... typically operating with a frequency of 1 kHz... Two papers that assign a different quantization step, and hence a different unrolled timestep count, to each layer make the resulting cost explicit without resolving it. QAC's own hardware-efficiency appendix states that under sequential dataflow-accelerator execution (one layer computed at a time, iterating across the network), cost scales as O(N·T), layers times timesteps, a sum over layers, which is precisely QAC's own motivation for moving to mixed timesteps in the first place . Under the alternative, pipelined multi-core execution that is the standard model for chips like TrueNorth and Loihi, the same appendix states plainly: "Layer 2 must wait until Layer 1 completes T_l1 timesteps before it can start computation... pipeline stalling may occur, introducing computational delays and preventing the hardware from achieving optimal performance" . Under pipelining, the pipeline's overlap benefit is lost exactly at the boundary between two layers of unequal horizon, so the effective latency again accumulates as a sum, this time via stall rather than via sequential iteration. QAC's own headline number, 2.76 average timesteps for ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10, is computed as an unweighted mean across layers, not either of these two costs, and the gap between the accuracy-optimized mean and the hardware-realizable sum is flagged by the paper's own text but never closed . PASCAL's analogous T_eff of 3.14 for the same architecture and dataset is likewise a weighted arithmetic mean over layers (Eq. 16), and PASCAL's own hardware section, RTL synthesis via Synopsys Design Compiler at 40 nm and 560 MHz with a cycle-accurate simulator, validates only that its modified IF neuron adds negligible overhead relative to a standard IF neuron at a single, uniform T=4; it never synthesizes, simulates, or measures the mixed-timestep execution model that produces the 3.14 headline . No paper surveyed, PASCAL, QAC, or QAC's ICLR-2026-withdrawn successor, performs RTL synthesis, cycle-accurate simulation, or a physical chip run of the actual heterogeneous-T execution path itself.

The one physical deployment of heterogeneous timing found in this survey, Mixed Time-step Training (Du, Wu, Deng, and Gu, ICLR 2025 poster), works only by targeting a timestep-free chip. MTT trains a network whose stages are randomly assigned different T during training, so the resulting weights transfer across many single global T values and onto a fully event-driven chip that has no discrete tick to be heterogeneous about in the first place. Deployed on a Speck2e devkit, the measured spike-difference between the training-time software simulator and the physical chip's output is 3.92 percent, close to the 2.81 percent difference measured between two identical on-chip runs, and far below the 28.77 percent difference between a conventional time-stepped simulator and the chip's actual behavior . This confirms physical deployment of a temporally flexible model, not execution of a genuine T_1 ≠ T_2 ≠ ... ≠ T_L schedule on synchronous silicon; the two problems, mixed-timestep training for cross-platform generalization and per-layer heterogeneous-horizon execution, are only superficially related, and MTT's Speck2e result is evidence for the former, not the latter.

8.6 Measurement boundaries and incomparable efficiency claims

SynOps is not joules.

SynOps is not joules. The per-operation energy figures nearly every SNN paper cites, roughly 0.9 pJ for a multiply-accumulate against 0.1 pJ for an accumulate, trace to Mark Horowitz's ISSCC 2014 plenary talk, a table specific to a 45 nm process at 0.9 V . The talk itself states that a 32 KB SRAM access already costs 5 pJ, an off-chip DRAM access costs 640 pJ, and a programmable processor's instruction fetch and register overhead adds roughly 70 pJ per instruction, none of which a SynOps-times-constant estimate captures . Three independent hardware-realistic studies converge on the same conclusion from three different angles. Bhattacharjee, Yin, Moitra, and Panda benchmark published SNN algorithms on two hardware-realistic accelerator models (a sparsity-aware systolic array, an RRAM crossbar simulator) and report that "the actual energy-efficiency improvements of recent SNN algorithmic works differ significantly from their estimated values" . Shen, Zhao, Li, Li, and Zeng propose replacing SynOps with a unified "Bit Budget" metric specifically because comparisons against unquantized ANN baselines are not fair comparisons . Narduzzi, Zenke, Liu, and Dunbar find that SynOps and spike-count metrics "fail to account for" membrane-update and gating-dynamics overhead and propose an alternative, EFLOP, that does . None of these are marginal corrections; all three report the gap between the SynOps estimate and measured hardware energy as large and systematic.

Static power dominates, so cutting T does not cut energy proportionally. On Loihi 2, a sigma-delta conversion study reports total power "remained relatively consistent for all experiments as it is dominated by static draw," even as throughput rose from 176 to 362 frames per second with increasing sparsity . A separate Loihi 2 study of the Locally Competitive Algorithm shows static power nearly constant (approximately 0.54 W) across sparsity settings while dynamic power varies only modestly (0.17 to 0.39 W), so at the sparsest, most favorable operating point, static power still exceeds dynamic power .

No source in this survey sweeps T alone on identical silicon holding everything else fixed, but the underlying mechanism, an SRAM-per-core design that draws static power continuously regardless of whether a given tick performs useful spiking work, is confirmed independently by both studies; reducing T shrinks only the dynamic term, and can even raise energy per inference if the reduction also lowers achievable throughput.

Boundary choice alone produces order-of-magnitude swings. Shrestha, Timcheck, Frady, Campos-Macías, and Davies report, for the identical Loihi 2 chip and identical PilotNet network, 0.09 mJ total energy and 1.21 ms latency per frame when host I/O is excluded from the measurement window (weights pre-staged), against 1.26 mJ and 65.41 ms when streaming from the host is included, a fourteen-fold energy difference and a fifty-four-fold latency difference from the boundary choice alone . In the host-inclusive configuration, over ninety-four percent of the reported energy is static leakage, not spiking computation . Other boundary choices swing the number further: ANN baseline energy on the same Jetson Orin Nano differs by roughly 3.5 times between batch sizes one and sixteen; input-encoding circuitry (the Poisson spike-generator itself) is frequently excluded from rate-coded SNN energy totals, though one sixteen-bit Eyeriss-scale study costs it explicitly and finds it material ; and quantizing the ANN comparison baseline from fp32 to int8 on the same Jetson cuts its energy by roughly thirty-seven percent and its energy-delay product by roughly sixty-three percent, narrowing without eliminating the reported SNN advantage . The field itself names the underlying cause structurally rather than accidentally: NeuroBench identifies "lack of a formal definition," "implementation diversity" (no ONNX-equivalent exchange format for SNNs), and "rapid research evolution" as the three reasons a single enforced measurement protocol is currently infeasible, and settles for transparency and reporting guidelines instead of a strict standard .

8.7 Device availability and toolchain decay

Reproducing NxTF's own published CIFAR-10 result on Loihi 1 today would require pinning software Intel no longer distributes support for, against a chip Intel's own current community guidance no longer directs new users toward.

SNN Toolbox's last PyPI release, v0.6.0, shipped 2021-03-17; its last substantive code commits date to August 2022 (a kernel-conversion slicing fix, a deprecated GUI import fix); one further, documentation-only commit in January 2023 adds a citation, and no commit has been found after . The issue tracker was still answered personally by the maintainer into 2024, but with an explicit admission of reduced capacity ("I won't be able to help much with debugging at this point"), and no issue in the tracker ever requests a feature from the post-2020, QCFS-family literature .

Intel archived all Lava repositories on 13 May 2026: "Intel is developing the next-generation Loihi architecture and SDK to power the coming era physical AI... All Lava repositories are archived. Stay tuned for announcements about our new SDK and next generation Loihi processor" . No patches are accepted and no maintenance is offered; a successor SDK is named only as forthcoming, with no release as of this survey. NxTF's own host repository, intel-nrc-ecosystem/models, which carries the only bridge between SNN Toolbox and NxTF, carries a blanket "DISCONTINUATION OF PROJECT" notice, and Intel's own INRC guidance has redirected users to Lava for Loihi 2 development since 2022, treating NxSDK and NxTF as Loihi-1-era and superseded even before Lava's own archival . Loihi 1 itself is superseded hardware, not the current INRC access target.

Access to this hardware is gated, while the hardware that supports reset-by-subtraction natively is not. Loihi 1 and 2 access runs through INRC membership, behind a research proposal and participation agreement, not a guaranteed or open channel ; BrainScaleS and SpiNNaker access runs through EBRAINS's queued-job Neuromorphic Computing Platform . Speck, by contrast, was presold as a 199 US dollar demo kit, and its dev kit is sold through an ordinary sales channel with no described approval process; Xylo development kits are likewise commercially purchasable . The two toolchains with the deepest published CNN-scale conversion results in this survey, SNN Toolbox through NxTF to Loihi 1, and Lava-DL's NetX to Loihi 2, sit behind the least accessible, least currently maintained software-hardware combination surveyed. 8.8 Interoperability and the portability gap

No universal exporter exists because every hardware deployment surveyed required nontrivial, platform-specific rewriting on top of whatever the interchange format carried.

NIR's reach is genuine and, among the interchange formats surveyed, unmatched. Nine simulators write and or read NIR (hxtorch and jaxsnn for BrainScaleS-2, Lava-DL read-only, Nengo, Norse, Rockpool, Sinabs, snnTorch, SpiNNaker2 read-only, and Spyx), reaching five physical hardware platforms: BrainScaleS-2, Loihi 2, Speck, SpiNNaker2, and Xylo, up from the seven simulators and four hardware platforms reported at initial publication . This reduces what would otherwise be an m × n interoperability problem, every framework needing a bespoke exporter to every chip, to m + n: every framework needs only one NIR exporter, and every chip needs only one NIR importer .

The paper's own hardware validation shows where this degrades. Cross-platform agreement is reported as faithful for feedforward dynamics, a single LIF neuron and a spiking convolutional network with IF neurons, but diverges for a recurrent current-based LIF network, where the authors state explicitly that reaching the highest accuracy required "platform-dependent optimization, such as quantization-aware training" .

NIR by itself does not guarantee bit-accurate transfer even for the model classes it covers well.

And it stops well short of the full primitive space a conversion paper needs: its own stated limitations exclude adaptive-threshold mechanisms, gating, resonate-and-fire, and multicompartmental neuron models from the current primitive set, and its own porting guide permits a hardware backend to simply ignore an unsupported node rather than approximate it, with approximation strategies for such cases listed as future work, not implemented . Section 8.2 documents the same gap for reset by subtraction specifically.

No universal exporter exists because every hardware deployment surveyed required nontrivial, platform-specific rewriting on top of whatever the interchange format carried. Per-neuron thresholds are collapsed to a single value per layer for Speck, whose hardware does not support individual per-neuron parameters . Linear layers are rewritten as 1×1 Conv2d layers and AvgPool2d as SumPool2d to fit a fixed per-core template . Multi-core targets require explicit per-core neuron-count partitioning. A working IR handoff is therefore a starting point for hand adaptation, not a drop-in compile step, and framework-native export paths compound the problem: SpikingJelly's own lava_exchange and nir_exchange modules both reject the soft-reset neurons its own ann2snn converter produces by default, so a model converted by one first-party SpikingJelly module cannot be handed to another first-party SpikingJelly export module without either reintroducing the reset-related accuracy cost documented in 8.2 or writing new code .

No QCFS-to-silicon route is asserted by any source reviewed. The nearest analogues are, first, NxTF and SNN Toolbox to Loihi 1, which realizes the same conversion family, rate-coded IF neurons under reset by subtraction, on physical hardware, but not QCFS's specific clip-floor-shift activation or learnable per-layer threshold ; and second, APEX and its predecessor PASCAL, which are QCFS-specific in their neuron design but target a synthesized, simulated accelerator, not fabricated, accessible silicon .

8.9 The two-population division of labor

Thirteen QCFS-lineage papers audited directly against full text report E5, pure software simulation, for thirteen of thirteen of their own experiments; zero report a run on a named chip. Twelve of the thirteen cite neuromorphic hardware only as generic architecture-paper motivational material, Davies 2018 for Loihi, Akopyan 2015 for TrueNorth, in their introductions, never in methods or experiments. QCFS itself is the sole partial exception, citing two genuine physical-silicon deployment papers, Massa et al. 2020 and Singh et al. 2021, as acknowledged prior art in its related work section rather than as introductory motivation .

The deployment side mirrors this. Four hardware-deployment papers audited (Stromatias 2015, Patiño-Saucedo 2020, Massa 2020, and NxTF 2021/2022) use zero post-2020 low-latency conversion methods between them; every one runs SNN Toolbox's classical threshold-balancing pipeline or direct STBP or SLAYER training, rate-coded throughout . NxTF's own related-work section, written by the same laboratory that authors SNN Toolbox, shows no forward awareness of the low-latency program about to accelerate . The toolchain freeze overlaps almost exactly with that acceleration: SNN Toolbox's last substantive commit, August 2022, falls within months of QCFS's own publication window, and the entire subsequent low-latency literature audited here, spanning 2022 through 2025, unfolds after the toolbox's code was already frozen .

The falsifier was searched for deliberately, and every confirmed instance found abandons rate coding. Quartz (Lenz, Orchard, and Sheik) proposes a time-to-first-spike conversion scheme, states in its own abstract that "most conversion methods rely on rate coding in the SNN to represent ANN activation, which uses enormous amounts of spikes," and reports measured results on physical Loihi 1: 0.9 µJ·s energy-delay product on MNIST against 4.38 µJ·s for a rate-coded Loihi baseline, and 10.3 mJ·s against roughly 34.9 mJ·s on CIFAR-10, a same-chip, method-versus-method comparison, not an isolated demonstration . Brehove et al. propose a sigma-delta, graded-payload conversion targeting Loihi 2's native graded-spike hardware and report measured, field-deployed results on a Loihi 2 VPX board . No paper was found that keeps rate coding, proposes a new conversion algorithm, and reaches physical silicon with measurements; the nearest near-miss, a Speck chip-introduction paper, uses a conventional conversion baseline rather than a novel low-latency algorithm and is a hardware paper first .

One qualification is owed here, stated honestly rather than smoothed over. A rate-coded QCFS and SRP baseline configuration has, once, been measured on physical silicon, as a comparison row inside Adaptive Fission (Jiang et al., NeurIPS 2025), whose own algorithmic contribution is a population-coding scheme layered on top of existing conversion methods, deployed on a Lynxi HP201 accelerator . QCFS and SRP's own authors never ran this measurement; a different group's non-rate-coding innovation motivated the one instance that exists. The defensible claim is therefore not that rate-coded QCFS-lineage output has never touched silicon under any circumstances, but that no QCFS-lineage algorithm paper deploys its own method to silicon, and the one time it happened, it required a different paper's rate-coding-abandoning innovation to make it happen . A second, weaker qualification: one non-peer-reviewed, self-reported GitHub project claims a QCFS implementation with dedicated SpiNNaker and BrainScaleS-2 backends and reports "physical silicon confirmed" runs, but its own documentation describes a two-neuron validation network, not a benchmark-scale classifier, and does not rise to the bar of a published counterexample; it suggests the gap may be sociological rather than technically forced, without closing it in the published record.

The two-population claim above was tested against a candidate counterexample, sought out deliberately rather than merely asserted. The candidate was "Temporal Flexibility in Spiking Neural Networks" (Du, Wu, Deng, and Gu, ICLR 2025, arXiv:2503.17394) , initially recorded in the survey's own registry as a both-population instance, E1, deployed on physical Speck2e, and not abandoning rate coding. On closer reading it does not clear the bar, on two independent grounds. First, population. The paper is a direct-training temporal-generalization technique, Mixed Time-step Training, and its own related-work section draws an explicit line between direct training and ANN-SNN conversion, placing itself in the former; its low-timestep results are reported on GPU only, never on the chip. Second, evidence class. The only quantity measured on the physical Speck2e chip is spike difference (SD), a fidelity check against the software simulator, not an efficiency measurement; SD reaches 3.92 percent on NMNIST, and no latency or energy figure is measured on the chip itself, the paper's own statements about power and latency cite prior Speck hardware papers rather than report new numbers. That is E2, not E1. The phrase "rate coding" appears nowhere in the paper. What actually runs on Speck2e is native asynchronous DVS events through reset-by-subtraction IF neurons, the chip's own default event-driven mode, not a rate code the authors chose to keep or abandon. A further search for papers matching the same profile, a new low-timestep or conversion method reaching physical silicon while keeping rate coding, turned up genuine E1 rate-coded silicon deployments, but every one of them reuses existing conversion tooling, CNN2SNN/MetaTF or a previously cited architecture, for a downstream application rather than proposing a new method; these are logged as boundary cases, not counterexamples. The claim survives, and it survives because the candidate was reclassified on two independently sufficient grounds, not because it was waved away.

9 Conclusion

This survey set out to answer whether the ReLU-to-integrate-and-fire correspondence that anchors the low-T ANN-to-SNN conversion literature is realized on physical silicon or only in simulation, and where the disconnect between that literature and the accessible neuromorphic landscape actually lies. The seam-by-seam audit in Section 8 answers both questions concretely rather than by assertion. A trained network passing from a conversion paper toward physical silicon loses at least one of its pooling or skip-connection topology (8.1), its BatchNorm folding (8.1), its reset rule (8.2), its encoding scheme's energy accounting (8.3), or its timestep semantics (8.4, 8.5) at nearly every handoff surveyed, and no single documented toolchain preserves all of them simultaneously. The two-population finding (8.9) is not an artifact of an incomplete search: it rests on full-text citation audits of thirteen algorithm papers and four deployment papers, a dated toolchain-freeze overlap, and a deliberately adversarial search for a falsifier that returned exactly two positive instances, Quartz and Brehove et al., both of which abandon rate coding to reach silicon. Whether a Lava-to-Loihi-2 deployment direction remains viable has a direct answer: the software stack was archived on 13 May 2026, accepting no further patches, with an unnamed successor SDK, and the reset-semantics blocker (8.2) together with the operator-coverage blocker (8.1) both apply before the gated, proposal-based hardware access (8.7) is even reached.

Four concrete problems remain open, and each follows directly from a seam documented above rather than from a general appeal to future work.

  1. A conversion theorem for an ordinal, non-rate code is missing. The correspondence proved for IF neurons under reset by subtraction, that a spike count over T steps realizes exactly a uniform T-level quantizer, has no published analogue for the codes that actually reach silicon alongside a new algorithm: time-to-first-spike (Quartz) and graded sigma-delta (Brehove et al.). Until such a theorem exists, "abandon rate coding to reach hardware" and "prove the QCFS-as-QAT correspondence" remain two separate, currently incompatible research programs rather than one.
  2. An execution model for heterogeneous per-layer timestep horizons is missing. NeuroScale confirms that every major digital neuromorphic chip audited here, TrueNorth, Loihi, Loihi 2, and Tianjic, advances through a single global synchronization barrier. No simulator or chip surveyed executes a genuine T_1 ≠ T_2 ≠ ... ≠ T_L schedule within one forward pass, and the two papers that compute an average-timestep headline, QAC and PASCAL, do not resolve, and in QAC's case do not even quantify, the sequential-sum or pipeline-stall cost their own appendices identify as the consequence of mapping that schedule onto real hardware.
  3. A maintained, soft-reset-preserving export path is missing. Every framework-native bridge surveyed, SpikingJelly's lava_exchange and nir_exchange, and the NIR primitive specification itself, rejects or fails to express reset by subtraction, the one mechanism independently shown worth roughly twenty accuracy points. The platforms that support it natively, TrueNorth, Speck via return_to_zero=False, and the FPGA frameworks, are either legacy hardware or require bespoke, per-project glue rather than offering a general, maintained tool.
  4. A measured comparison of a modern low-T method against a quantized ANN baseline on the same silicon is missing. The single closest data point found, Loihi 2 against an int8-quantized Jetson Orin Nano on PilotNet, shows that quantizing the ANN baseline closes a substantial fraction, though not all, of the reported SNN advantage. No equivalent same-chip comparison exists for a post-2021 low-T conversion method measured against an INT8 or lower ANN baseline of matching bit budget.

The difficulty of connecting conversion methods to hardware execution is not a defect of any particular literature review. It is a gap in the field's published record, established here from the primary sources rather than assumed. This survey's edge structure, tracing what each handoff preserves and what it breaks, is offered as a first attempt to make that gap an object of study rather than an implicit background condition that each new conversion paper works around without naming it.
