This survey traces the handoffs a converted spiking network must survive to reach a physical neuromorphic chip, recording at each one what is preserved, what is broken, and how strong the evidence is at the destination.

Abstract

Spiking neural networks promise inference at a fraction of the energy cost of conventional accelerators, and ANN-to-SNN conversion is the most accessible route to a trained spiking model. A line of work anchored by quantization-clip-floor-shift conversion has driven the timestep count needed to match ANN accuracy from the hundreds into the single digits. Whether those gains reach silicon is a separate question, and it is the question this survey answers. A trained network that reaches a chip passes through a sequence of handoffs, each governed by a different toolchain, reset rule, spike payload, and time model, and each able to break the activation-to-spike-count correspondence the algorithm paper established. This survey records those handoffs. It presents the preliminaries a reader needs, namely neuron models, neural codes, and the execution contracts that hardware imposes, then reviews the primary training paths, advanced conversion techniques, and the alternative codes that abandon rate entirely. It then maps the deployment stack itself, covering neuromorphic platforms, training and simulation frameworks, export and interchange formats, compilers, and runtimes, and traces every documented route from a trained model to a named chip. Deployment claims are classified on a five-level evidence scale separating measured silicon from software simulation. Applying that scale to the literature exposes a division of labor that no prior survey states: algorithm papers advance accuracy at low timestep counts and do not deploy, while deployment papers reach silicon and do not adopt the new algorithms. Every confirmed case of a new conversion method reaching measured silicon abandons rate coding. The survey closes with the open challenges that division creates, from reset-semantics mismatch and operator coverage to toolchain decay and incomparable efficiency reporting.

1 Introduction

Deep neural networks have achieved strong performance in vision, speech, control, and other data-intensive tasks. This performance, however, commonly relies on large parameter sets and many multiply-and-accumulate operations. The resulting demand for computation, memory movement, and power complicates deployment where responses must be produced close to a sensor or actuator .

At the same time, sensing and inference are moving toward the network edge. Cameras, microphones, tactile arrays, and industrial sensors produce streams that may require local, low-latency interpretation. Sending every observation to a remote processor can add communication cost, delay, and system dependence. This has increased interest in computing systems that can operate within practical limits on memory, throughput, power, and response time while retaining reliable task performance .

Deploying learned models under these constraints remains difficult. The challenge is not only to reduce arithmetic. A deployed system must also represent sensor input, retain state, move data, schedule computation, and produce a decision within the relevant time window. These requirements are especially important for event-based sensing, where a signal records changes rather than a sequence of uniformly sampled frames .

Spiking neural networks (SNNs) offer a different abstraction. A neuron retains state, receives events, and emits discrete spikes when its dynamics meet a condition. Information can therefore be represented in spike count, timing, order, population activity, or graded payloads. This makes SNNs natural candidates for temporal signals and event-based sensing, while also motivating hardware that can exploit conditional activity and sparse communication . Those properties are opportunities, not universal performance guarantees. Their practical value depends on the code, model, workload, platform, and measurement boundary.

Neuromorphic computing is consequently a field rather than a single device class or learning method. It spans dynamical models, neural codes, learning rules, software frameworks, compilers, and digital, analog, and mixed-signal execution systems. Choices made at one layer change what can be expressed at another. A temporal code can require state, precision, routing, or stopping behavior that a target does not provide. A model that is correct in one simulator can require an explicit transformation before a mapper or runtime can execute it .

Researchers reach SNNs through two principal routes. Direct training optimizes a spiking model, usually with local rules, surrogate gradients, or related approximations. ANN-to-SNN conversion starts from a trained artificial neural network (ANN) and maps its activations into spiking dynamics. The routes differ in optimization, representation, and common failure modes. They meet, however, when the resulting SNN must be represented in software and executed on a target .

This survey adopts a deployment-centered perspective on neuromorphic computing. It develops the concepts needed to follow either SNN route from computation and representation to a defensible model-to-chip claim. It is written for readers entering the field, preparing a thesis, selecting a deployment path, or interpreting a result that spans algorithms and hardware. The survey first establishes what a model represents, how its dynamics and time are defined, and what a decoder recovers. It then examines whether those semantics can be preserved, transformed, or measured on a named target.

The scope is deliberately broad across the deployment path and selective within specialist topics. It covers learning only to the depth needed to compare direct training and conversion at their common deployment boundary. It likewise covers sensing, analog circuits, and benchmark protocol only where they change the deployable model or the meaning of a measurement. For broader treatments of SNN learning, see Nunes et al. and Khan et al. . For event-based sensing and its representations, see Gallego et al. . Throughout, the survey keeps an algorithmic horizon, a hardware tick, and wall-clock latency distinct. It also distinguishes a neural code from an input convention, and a documented toolchain capability from a published route that has actually been exercised.

1.1 Existing Surveys

Recent surveys have addressed spiking neural networks, neuromorphic computing, and event-driven systems from different angles, yet these topics are often treated separately. The existing literature can be broadly grouped into five main lines: model- and learning-centered surveys , coding and sensing surveys , software and interoperability surveys , hardware-platform surveys , and benchmarking, deployment, and application reviews . Each line establishes an important part of the field. None alone follows a trained SNN from its computational definition through software and hardware boundaries to a clearly stated measurement claim.

Model- and learning-centered surveys establish the background needed to understand SNN construction. Roy et al. connect learning requirements with hardware mechanisms and cross-layer co-design. Nunes et al. , Zheng et al. , Ayasi et al. , and Luu et al. provide broader introductions to neuron models, coding, learning, and applications. Eshraghian et al. focus on gradient-based training, while Caviglia et al. organize local learning rules and software benchmarks. Rathi et al. , Hu et al. , and He and Gao connect algorithmic structure with scaling or hardware considerations. Yamazaki et al. and Khan et al. extend the discussion across applications and hardware, whereas Xie et al. concentrate on compression for deep SNNs. Although extensive, this literature usually treats the learning method or model family as its primary unit of analysis rather than tracing a complete deployment route.

Coding- and sensing-centered surveys examine how physical or numerical signals become event and spike representations. Gallego et al. provide a comprehensive account of event-camera sensing, representations, algorithms, and applications. Tayarani-Najaran and Schmuker compare event-based sensing across visual, auditory, and olfactory domains. Cimarelli et al. connect neuromorphic vision-sensor hardware with algorithms and applications. These surveys explain the sensing and representation boundary in depth, but provide less coverage of how the resulting semantics survive framework export, compute mapping, and deployment measurement. For a broader taxonomy of neural codes and their signal-representation trade-offs, we refer the reader to Auge et al. .

Software and interoperability studies survey simulators, programming models, compilers, and mapping environments. Manna et al. compare nine SNN frameworks from a data-science perspective. Huynh et al. review software and mapping support for implementing SNNs on neuromorphic architectures. NIR defines a common intermediate representation and exposes semantic disagreement across simulators and hardware back ends. Farsa et al. extend the implementation view to GPU and RISC-V accelerators and identify incompatible reporting practices. Together, these works clarify the available software landscape, but a framework or graph format does not establish that a published model traversed a documented route or retained its reset, delay, payload, and readout semantics.

Hardware-centered surveys emphasize circuits, chip architectures, execution styles, and platform capabilities. Schuman et al. provide an early integration of models, algorithms, hardware, and applications. Bouvier et al. relate SNN algorithms to hardware implementations and their benchmark limitations. Basu et al. and Ferreira et al. compare circuit designs using normalized implementation metrics. Ivanov et al. , Wahid et al. , Zolfagharinejad et al. , and Chen et al. organize broader processor and architecture landscapes. Davies et al. synthesize measured Loihi results, while Shrestha et al. connect model semantics with platform-specific software and compiler support. These surveys establish what particular devices and architecture families can provide. They do not establish that a model definition or compiler route transfers unchanged across platforms.

Benchmarking, deployment, and application reviews provide the broadest system-level view. Schuman et al. connect algorithms and applications to full-stack deployment barriers, while Kudithipudi et al. identify the challenges involved in scaling neuromorphic systems and their software ecosystems. The EdgeSNN survey by Deng et al. organizes edge-oriented methods and separates proxy from hardware-aware evaluation. NeuroBench formalizes this distinction as a benchmarking framework. Rashed and Dias review efficient techniques for edge SNNs, and Du et al. compare SNNs across sensing modalities and edge devices. Gebregiorgis et al. extend the view from biological inspiration through learning, mapping, and hardware architectures. These studies establish why system context and measurement matter. They do not retrospectively classify every published claim by physical execution, documented path, hardware model, or software simulation, nor do they require an edge-by-edge route from a trained model to a named target.

The resulting gap is narrower than a claim that earlier work ignored deployment. Prior surveys address important pieces of the problem. The gap is an end-to-end synthesis that lets a reader ask the same questions of every route. What is mathematically defined? What can the software represent? What does the target execute? What was measured, where, and against which baseline? The answer may be a physical route, a transformed route, a blocked route, or an unexercised documented path. Each answer remains useful when its evidence boundary is stated.

This gap raises a practical question for the field. When a new low-timestep conversion method improves finite-window accuracy in simulation, does its own deployment route reach named physical silicon with measurement, or does deployment proceed through an older pipeline, a directly trained model, or a platform-native code? The evidence synthesis returns to this question without assuming its answer in advance.

1.2 Contributions

This survey makes the following key contributions:

  1. We provide a deployment-centered field-entry reference for neuromorphic computing, covering the computational and representational concepts needed to follow an SNN from model definition to a stated deployment claim.
  2. We present a pedagogical dependency chain from computation and representation through neuron and synapse dynamics, temporal semantics, neural codes, learning, ANN-to-SNN conversion, software, hardware execution, measurement, applications, and open problems.
  3. We connect direct training and ANN-to-SNN conversion at a common deployable SNN contract. The contract identifies the semantic fields that must be defined mathematically, represented in software, and faithfully executed on a target.
  4. We introduce a claim-level evidence framework and a typed route vocabulary. These distinguish physical measurement, physical fidelity, a documented route, a hardware model, and software evidence without assigning one label to an entire paper.
  5. We synthesize platform capabilities from vendor documentation, SDKs, mapper constraints, development kits, release notes, and examples. We then interpret applications by workload and measurement boundary rather than by vendor, making the limits of each reported route explicit.

2 Computation and Representation

A trained model, a simulator, and a physical target must agree on what a number means before any claim about their equivalence is meaningful. This section defines the objects that recur through the rest of the survey, a static value, a persistent state, a discrete event, and a temporal signal. It separates dense computation from sparse activity and conditional execution, distinguishes the several uses of the words neuromorphic and event-driven, and explains why a representation choice constrains which execution strategies remain available downstream. It does not yet compare platforms. It fixes the vocabulary that Sections 3 through 15 depend on.

2.1 Values, State, and Events

This survey considers the feedforward ANN inference setting, in which a trained network is evaluated layer by layer on a fixed input. In that setting, an ANN unit produces its output by a weighted sum of its inputs followed by a static nonlinearity, evaluated once for each input presentation. The result, denoted \(a^l\) for a unit in layer \(l\), is a real-valued number. It is a value in the strict sense used throughout this survey. Within one evaluation it depends only on the current input and carries no memory of a previous evaluation; recurrent and state-space architectures, where a unit's output also depends on state carried forward from a previous evaluation, fall outside this setting and outside the scope of this survey. An ANN activation is a static quantity within one evaluation, fully determined by the current input rather than updated from its own previous value. It is a complete description of that unit's output at that input, not a reduced or impoverished version of something else.

A spiking unit instead emits a discrete event. The plainest case is a bare binary spike, denoted \(z_i^l[t]\in\{0,1\}\) for unit \(i\) in layer \(l\) at discrete algorithmic step \(t\). At a known source and step it either occurs or it does not, and that occurrence is all the event reports. A deployed event is not always this bare. It may additionally carry a source or destination address, a timestamp, a polarity, an event type, or an integer or graded payload \(p_{ik}\), depending on the sensor, protocol, or neuron model that produces it. This survey's \(z_i^l[t]\) notation covers the binary case; payload-carrying events are introduced with the signed and graded neuron models of Section 3, Neuron and Synapse Dynamics, and with the coding schemes of Section 4, Temporal Semantics, Neural Codes, and Decoding, where a code or a platform needs them. A spiking unit also typically carries an internal state \(u_i^l[t]\), the membrane potential, that persists from step to step and is written and later read rather than recomputed independently each time. \(t\) indexes a discrete algorithmic step here, not a duration of physical time. A physical or modeled duration is instead denoted \(\tau\), and the two are not interchangeable until a simulation-to-hardware mapping states how a step corresponds to a duration. On its own, a bare binary event carries almost no information. It either occurred at that step or it did not. What carries information is the relationship between events across steps, their count, their timing, or the pattern they form, together with the state that produced them, or, once a payload is declared, the payload itself.

In this survey, event is the general term, whereas binary spike means the special event \(z_i^l[t]\in\{0,1\}\). This distinction prevents a convenient rate-coded abstraction from becoming an unstated assumption about every sensor interface, interconnect protocol, or target execution model.

Three objects therefore need to stay distinct. A value is a quantity that depends only on the current input and needs no notion of time to be defined, such as \(a^l\). A state is a quantity that persists, is updated using its own previous value, and can be read at a later step, such as \(u_i^l[t]\). An event is a discrete occurrence tied to a specific step or time, binary at minimum and optionally carrying the address, timing, or payload fields introduced above, such as \(z_i^l[t]\). A temporal signal is any quantity indexed by \(t\) or \(\tau\), which includes a spike train, a decaying state trajectory, and a time-varying input current, whether or not any individual sample of it carries a large payload; a sample of such a signal can itself be a real-valued quantity, so carrying a temporal index is not what separates a value from a state. The distinction that matters instead is between a static activation, fully determined within one evaluation and not updated from a previous value, and a persistent state, whose current value is computed from its own previous value and so must be written and read again across evaluations or steps. In this sense \(a^l\) is static and \(u_i^l[t]\) is persistent. A state and an event both carry a temporal index, but a state is read continuously across steps while an event is a punctual occurrence at one of them.

A network becomes spiking when this stateful event transformation is composed across layers. At each layer, incoming events are routed through fixed synaptic weights to update postsynaptic state. Threshold crossings generate the events delivered to the next layer. The network therefore communicates a temporally distributed representation, while each neuron retains local state between event arrivals or algorithmic steps. The binary case shown here is the reference abstraction used for rate-coded conversion. Later sections extend it to payload-carrying events, alternative codes, and target-specific execution models .

2.2 Dense Computation, Sparse Activity, and Conditional Execution

In a feedforward ANN evaluation, every unit at every layer produces an activation value on every input presentation, and the tensor that holds those activations is dense in the representational sense. It logically represents every position, including one whose value happens to equal zero. Layer-to-layer computation structured as a matrix multiplication touches every incoming weight and every position of that tensor by default. Whether the arithmetic engine that executes this matrix multiplication actually performs a multiply-accumulate (MAC) operation for a position that evaluates to zero, or recognizes the zero and skips it, is a separate question of execution. A dense representation is compatible with either choice, a fixed schedule that performs the operation everywhere or a compiler and hardware combination that inspects the tensor and conditionally skips a known zero.

Figure 3 compares the communicated object in the restricted feedforward-inference setting used throughout this survey. An ANN layer receives a real-valued activation vector and computes a weighted aggregation followed by a static nonlinearity once for an input presentation. A binary-event SNN layer receives event occurrences over steps, conditionally applies the fixed synaptic weights associated with those occurrences, updates persistent postsynaptic state, and emits events after thresholding and reset. This is a comparison of computational representations. It is not yet a claim about the physical operations, scheduling policy, or energy of a particular target .

The absence of an event and the numerical value zero are different representational facts. In a dense ANN tensor, every logical position is present, including positions whose value is zero. A sparse event representation communicates selected occurrences and omits silent positions. Omission can make conditional routing or accumulation available, but it does not establish that a runtime skips work. In particular, absence is interpretable only relative to a declared step, time window, or completion rule. An asynchronous stream does not encode an absence as a packet.

In a network of spiking units, the binary event \(z_i^l[t]\) defined above is either present or absent at a given source and step under the discrete-time model fixed here. A sparse representation, unlike the dense tensor above, explicitly stores or communicates only the selected nonzero positions rather than every position by default. When a substantial fraction of a layer's events are absent within a finite horizon, an activity pattern this survey calls sparse, that sparse representation carries less to store or transmit, and the computation associated with a silent unit can in principle be skipped rather than performed and discarded. Communication can be sparse in the same sense. A layer then transmits only its nonzero events rather than a dense vector covering every unit, so the amount transmitted depends on how many units fired rather than on how many units exist .

This distinction describes what a representation makes available, not what a given implementation does with it, and it applies symmetrically to both representations introduced above. Either a dense tensor or a sparse representation can be executed densely, with every position or every possible event slot processed uniformly, or with conditional skipping, an execution style this survey calls conditional, in which absent activity is recognized and the corresponding work is not performed. Which of the two a given implementation adopts is a hardware and compiler decision separate from the network's activity pattern. An event representation omits silent positions from communication. A receiver can treat that omission as inactivity only under a declared step, window, timeout, or barrier. This can make conditional communication and conditional accumulation a natural strategy to adopt, but it does not by itself guarantee that a given execution substrate adopts it. A high event rate, close to one event per unit per step, leaves sparse activity with little advantage over dense computation, and a network may require many steps before enough silence accumulates for skipping to matter at all.

Consequently, fewer spikes and fewer hardware operations are not the same claim. The second requires an execution substrate that treats an absent event as work to be skipped rather than work to be padded, and only a measurement on that substrate establishes that it did so, not the event count alone. Conditional execution names the substrate behavior that would make the skip real. Which platform families realize conditional execution natively and which emulate it with a dense pipeline underneath is compared once execution models are introduced, in the section on hardware execution models.

2.3 The Meanings of Neuromorphic and Event-Driven

The words neuromorphic and event-driven are applied to at least four different objects in this literature, and treating them as one property is a frequent source of overclaiming. A sensor is neuromorphic or event-driven when it asynchronously reports a change in its input as a discrete, timestamped event rather than sampling every input on a fixed clock. An event camera's output stream is event-native from the moment of acquisition in exactly this sense . An algorithm or network model is spiking, and often called neuromorphic, when it is specified in terms of the discrete events and persistent state defined above, independent of what substrate eventually executes it. A network simulated step by step on a conventional processor is a spiking algorithm in this sense whether or not any part of that simulation is triggered by an event. An execution model is event-driven when a core or process advances only when an event demands it, rather than on every tick of a shared clock. This is a property of how computation proceeds, not of how the algorithm was specified. A fourth sense applies to a complete system rather than to one stage. A system is an end-to-end event-driven system when an event-driven sensor, a spiking algorithm, and an event-driven execution substrate are paired throughout, with no stage reintroducing dense, clocked computation. This fourth sense is the conjunction of the previous three holding simultaneously across every stage, not a stronger or purer version of any single one of them, and it is also the least common of the four to hold in practice.

These four senses do not transfer into one another automatically. An event-native sensor stream does not establish that the algorithm consuming it is spiking, since a frame can be reconstructed from an event stream and handed to a conventional network. A spiking algorithm does not establish that its execution is event-driven end to end, since a simulator can recompute every unit's state at every step regardless of which events are present or absent. This is the specific, terminological form of a non-equivalence that recurs throughout the survey. Event-native input does not prove event-driven execution throughout a system. Establishing which stage of a given pipeline satisfies which of the four senses, and on which named platform, is deferred to the sections that introduce execution models and traced deployment routes. No platform claim is made here.

2.4 Why Representation Constrains Execution

The representation chosen at one point of the model-to-device chain restricts which execution strategies remain faithful to it at the next, though it does not by itself pick one. A dense, real-valued activation \(a^l\) is represented the same way whether its value is far from zero or exactly zero, so the dense layout does not omit that position structurally. Exploiting a zero therefore requires inspection, metadata, or a sparse transformation, and remains an execution decision. A value close to zero has the same representational status as any other value. Executing a layer of such units efficiently therefore favors, by default, an engine built around dense multiply-accumulate arithmetic that processes every unit uniformly. As in the discussion above, a compiler and hardware combination that inspects the tensor for zero-valued positions and skips the corresponding operation remains a separate execution choice that the dense representation neither forces nor forbids.

A binary event \(z_i^l[t]\) is a discrete occurrence rather than a magnitude to multiply. For a binary unit-payload event and a fixed synaptic weight, processing an arriving event can reduce to adding or subtracting that weight into postsynaptic state. Payload-carrying events, programmable transforms, and some hardware encodings can require multiplication or an equivalent scaling operation. The binary fixed-weight case is accumulated into the postsynaptic state only when the event fires . An event-based representation therefore admits an accumulate-only execution strategy that a dense, value-based representation does not offer as a comparable option, because a real-valued activation cannot degenerate to a data-dependent addition in the same way. Its magnitude is exactly the information that a spike's mere presence does not carry.

That an accumulate-only strategy is representationally admissible does not mean any particular hardware realizes it, and does not mean realizing it is free. Maintaining per-unit persistent state, routing events or advancing timestep state, and reading out a decision after a variable number of steps all add engineering cost that a fixed-schedule, dense pipeline avoids by construction. A representation that relaxes one constraint typically introduces another. State must be stored somewhere, and event timing must be tracked somewhere, even when no multiplication is required.

The consequence carried forward is only that representation sets the space of physically consistent execution strategies available to a target. It does not by itself select one, and it does not establish that a specific chip, compiler, or runtime exploits the option a representation makes available. Which execution families exist, and which of their capabilities are native, transformed, emulated, host-assisted, unsupported, or undocumented for a given representation, is the comparison made in the section on hardware execution models. Which complete route a published deployment actually exercised is established only in the section that traces named deployment routes edge by edge.

3 Neuron and Synapse Dynamics

Section 2 distinguished a value from a state, an event, and a temporal signal without committing to any particular neuron equation. This section makes that commitment. It fixes one staged update that every neuron model used later in the survey instantiates, so that a difference between models is stated as a difference in one named stage rather than as an unrelated new equation. The same staging separates a model's continuous mathematical description from its discrete-time simulation and from its finite-precision hardware realization, three questions that later sections keep asking separately about reset, coding, and execution.

3.1 A Common Staged State Update

A spiking neuron in layer \(l\) receives events from presynaptic units \(j\), maintains an internal state, and emits an event \(z_i^l[t] \in \{0,1\}\) when that state crosses a threshold. Rather than writing this as one overloaded recurrence, this survey factors it into three stages, following the notation fixed for the whole article. A synaptic stage \(\mathcal{S}^l\) turns incoming events into a synaptic state or filtered current \(q_i^l[t]\). A membrane stage \(\mathcal{M}^l\) combines that current with the previous membrane state \(u_i^l[t-1]\), an optional adaptation state \(a_i^l[t-1]\), and a bias \(b_i^l\) into a pre-reset candidate \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]\). A threshold function \(\mathcal{H}\) compares that candidate against a threshold \(\theta_i^l[t]\) to decide whether an event is emitted. A reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\) then turns the pre-reset candidate, the emitted event, and the threshold into the post-reset state \(u_i^l[t]\) that stage \(\mathcal{M}^l\) reads at the next step.

\[ q_i^l[t] = \mathcal{S}^l\!\left(q_i^l[t-1],\{z_j^{l-1}[t-d_{ij}^l]\}_j,\{w_{ij}^l\}_j\right), \] \[ \tilde{u}_i^l[t] = \mathcal{M}^l\!\left(u_i^l[t-1],q_i^l[t],a_i^l[t-1],b_i^l\right), \qquad z_i^l[t] = \mathcal{H}\!\left(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]-\theta_i^l[t]\right), \] \[ u_i^l[t] = \mathcal{R}^l\!\left(\tilde{u}_i^l[t],z_i^l[t],\theta_i^l[t]\right). \]

Two indexing conventions hold throughout this section. First, the update order is fixed as written. \(\mathcal{S}^l\) is evaluated before \(\mathcal{M}^l\), and \(\mathcal{H}\) is evaluated on the pre-reset candidate \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]\), never on the already-reset \(u_i^l[t-1]\). Second, \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]\) always denotes the value before the reset stage has acted and \(u_i^l[t]\) always denotes the value after it, so a later equation that writes \(u_i^l[t]\) is referring to the quantity that stage \(\mathcal{M}^l\) will read at step \(t+1\). This vocabulary is a bookkeeping device, not a claim about implementation. It does not assert that every simulator or every chip literally computes four separate intermediate arrays in this order; Section 3.5 returns to what a hardware or software implementation is free to change and what it is not.

Framing every model this way lets the rest of the section ask one question per model. Which stage does this neuron modify relative to the plainest case, and does it introduce a new persistent state variable or only a new coefficient inside an existing stage? The plainest case, developed next, uses a trivial \(\mathcal{S}^l\) with no memory of its own, an \(\mathcal{M}^l\) with no decay, and a fixed threshold, which is the discrete-time abstraction that the classical literature on spiking neurons and network models builds on .

3.2 The Neuron Model Family

Integrate-and-fire (IF) is the baseline instance of the staged update. Its synaptic stage has no memory, computing \(q_i^l[t]=\sum_j w_{ij}^l z_j^{l-1}[t-d_{ij}^l]\) fresh at every step with nothing carried over from \(q_i^l[t-1]\). Its membrane stage adds that current to the previous state with no decay, \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]=u_i^l[t-1]+q_i^l[t]+b_i^l\), and its threshold is fixed, \(\theta_i^l[t]=\theta_i^l\). The mechanism is deliberately the simplest possible instance of the three stages. Its mathematical effect is that every unit of input charge that ever arrives is eventually spent as output, so a finite-window spike count is not systematically damped relative to the current driving it. This is the property the classical ANN-to-SNN conversion literature relies on when it equates a spike count with a target ReLU activation, and it is the reason that literature targets IF neurons specifically rather than a leaky variant . The trade-off is that IF has no mechanism for forgetting old input, which conversion accepts as a feature but which direct training on temporally structured data usually treats as a limitation. The deployment consequence is that IF's simplicity makes it the easiest model to realize with a single accumulator register; the evidence boundary is that this is a mechanism-level statement about the target of a mapping, not a deployment measurement on its own.

Leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) keeps IF's trivial synaptic stage and modifies only the membrane stage, introducing a per-layer leak factor \(\alpha^l\) with \(0<\alpha^l<1\) that this survey treats as a new local symbol, distinct from the activation or threshold scale \(\lambda^l\) that later sections use for conversion, giving \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]=\alpha^l u_i^l[t-1]+q_i^l[t]+b_i^l\). The membrane now relaxes toward its resting value between inputs instead of holding charge indefinitely. The trade-off is exactly the property IF avoids. A unit of charge that arrives and is not soon followed by enough further input to cross threshold is partially forgotten rather than eventually spent, so a naive spike count over a short window is no longer an unbiased read of the driving current. This is one reason the rate-coding conversion literature above targets IF rather than LIF even though LIF is the more common default in general spiking-network practice. LIF is also available as a selectable leaky or leak-disabled mode on Sinabs-backed DYNAP-CNN and Speck deployments, where enabling the leak requires an accompanying bias term and an externally configured slow-clock rate .

Current-based LIF (CUBA-LIF) is the first model in this family that gives the synaptic stage genuine memory. Instead of the instantaneous sum used by IF and LIF, \(q_i^l[t]\) is itself a leaky integrator of the incoming weighted events, \(q_i^l[t]=\beta^l q_i^l[t-1]+\sum_j w_{ij}^l z_j^{l-1}[t-d_{ij}^l]\), and the membrane stage integrates this filtered current the same way LIF integrates the raw sum, \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]=\alpha^l u_i^l[t-1]+q_i^l[t]+b_i^l\). The mechanism adds a second decaying state variable, so a single presynaptic event spreads its influence over several steps instead of landing on the membrane in one step. The consequence for a hardware target is that this two-stage filter is exactly what a single-compartment current-based primitive is built to execute directly. CUBA-LIF is documented as Loihi's native default neuron dynamics and is exposed in Lava-DL as the SLAYER current-based integration block . The same synaptic-stage filter is sPyNNaker's documented default on first-generation SpiNNaker's PyNN front end, whose built-in cell type IF_curr_exp pairs a current-based synapse with an exponentially decaying synaptic current rather than the instantaneous sum IF and LIF use, which places SpiNNaker's default neuron in this row rather than under LIF above . The trade-off is the same forgetting property LIF introduces, now applied twice, once to the synaptic state and once to the membrane state, so a CUBA-LIF spike count is a still less direct read of any single driving input than an LIF count. Its native single-compartment reset defaults to reset-to-zero, a fact Section 3.4 returns to directly.

Adaptive LIF (ALIF) leaves the synaptic and membrane stages as in IF or LIF but couples the threshold itself to the neuron's own recent spiking through the adaptation state \(a_i^l[t]\) that the common update already reserves. A representative discrete form raises the threshold after every spike and lets it relax back toward a baseline between spikes, \[ \theta_i^l[t]=\theta_0^l+\kappa^l a_i^l[t-1], \qquad a_i^l[t]=\gamma^l a_i^l[t-1]+z_i^l[t], \] with \(0<\gamma^l<1\) a second local decay constant and \(\kappa^l\) a coupling gain, following the discrete adaptive formulation used for long-memory spiking networks . The adaptation update runs alongside the reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\), triggered by the same emitted event \(z_i^l[t]\), rather than being one of the three canonical stages itself. The semantic effect is that a constant input no longer produces a constant firing rate; the rate falls as the threshold climbs, giving the neuron a longer effective memory than a single leak constant can provide without an explicit multiplicative gate. The trade-off is a second persistent state variable per neuron and a nonstationary threshold that later sections must track wherever they otherwise treat \(\theta_i^l\) as fixed. Native support for this mechanism is uneven. It is documented as a directly implemented analog adaptation current on BrainScaleS-2 , while on Loihi it is realized through the multi-compartment mechanism introduced next, at the cost of a second compartment per adaptive neuron .

Multi-compartment neurons generalize the single state triple \((q_i^l,u_i^l,a_i^l)\) into several coupled instances indexed by a compartment \(c\), each running its own copy of the staged update, with the output of one compartment entering another compartment's synaptic or membrane stage as an additional term. Only a designated somatic compartment evaluates \(\mathcal{H}\) and emits \(z_i^l[t]\); the other compartments exist purely to hold auxiliary state that a single-compartment primitive cannot represent. In the deployment-relevant literature this survey reviews, the mechanism is used less for dendritic realism than as an engineering workaround. On Loihi, a second compartment is documented as the way both a subtractive reset and spike-frequency adaptation are recovered from a hardware primitive whose native single-compartment behavior does neither, at the declared cost of doubling compartment allocation per affected neuron . On BrainScaleS-2, multi-compartment structure is instead documented as native dendritic computation with genuine branch-dependent behavior, not only a workaround for a missing single-compartment feature . The deployment consequence is that a model description naming "a multi-compartment neuron" is incomplete until it states which of these two roles the extra compartment plays and at what resource cost, a distinction Section 10 revisits as part of the deployable contract's neuron-equation field.

The staged update as written assumes a binary event \(z_i^l[t]\in\{0,1\}\). Nothing in \(\mathcal{S}^l\), \(\mathcal{M}^l\), or \(\mathcal{R}^l\) requires this. The threshold function \(\mathcal{H}\) can instead emit a signed or graded payload \(p_{ik}\), so that a neuron reports a magnitude and a sign rather than a bare occurrence. 3.3 Synaptic State, Delay, and Recurrent Input

The synaptic stage \(\mathcal{S}^l\) is where the common update already accounts for filtering, delay, and recurrence, so this subsection names what varies inside it rather than introducing new stages. Filtering is the difference between IF or LIF's instantaneous sum and CUBA-LIF's leaky current developed above; a model family can therefore be extended with a different synaptic filter, for instance a second-order or dual-exponential current, by changing only \(\mathcal{S}^l\)'s internal recursion, without touching \(\mathcal{M}^l\), \(\mathcal{H}\), or \(\mathcal{R}^l\).

Delay enters through the index \(d_{ij}^l\) already present in \(\mathcal{S}^l\)'s argument list, which shifts a presynaptic event \(z_j^{l-1}\) by \(d_{ij}^l\) steps before it contributes to \(q_i^l[t]\). Two properties of \(d_{ij}^l\) matter for later sections and must not be conflated. First, whether delay is a configured per-connection parameter or a fixed architectural consequence of the time model itself is a separate question, developed once Section 4 fixes the vocabulary for hardware ticks and asynchronous propagation; this subsection only fixes that \(d_{ij}^l\) can be measured in discrete steps, hardware ticks, or physical time, and that a citation using it must state which. Second, a per-connection configurable delay is a documented capability on some targets and not others. SpiNNaker's software-defined synapse model exposes a programmable per-synapse delay, and a training pipeline built around Loihi 2's native delay mechanism learns per-synapse delays of up to several dozen steps and reports feedforward latency and dynamic energy for the resulting network on physical hardware . Neither example licenses a claim about delay support on a platform not named in that source.

Recurrent state is not a new stage either. A recurrent connection is a synaptic weight \(w_{ij}^l\) whose presynaptic index \(j\) ranges over units in the same layer, or over a later layer through a delay \(d_{ij}^l\) of at least one step so that the update in Section 3.1 remains causal. The adaptation state \(a_i^l[t]\) introduced for ALIF is a second, complementary way a neuron's own history feeds back into itself, operating through the threshold rather than through an explicit recurrent synapse. Refractory behavior, a period after a spike during which a neuron cannot fire again, is a boundary case of the same adaptation mechanism rather than a variable this survey reserves a new symbol for. It is realized by transiently raising \(\theta_i^l[t]\) to a value \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]\) cannot cross, using the same coupling that produces spike-frequency adaptation, rather than by suppressing the update or the emission stage directly.

3.4 Reset Semantics

The reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\) is the last of the four functions in Section 3.1's update, and it is the one this survey treats as load-bearing rather than incidental, because the correctness argument behind rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion depends on which reset rule it implements. Two rules recur across every model in Section 3.2; the literature and this survey call them reset-to-zero and reset-by-subtraction, with hard reset and soft reset as common aliases used once here and not repeated.

Reset-to-zero clamps the post-reset state to a fixed baseline, usually zero, whenever an event is emitted, and otherwise leaves the pre-reset candidate unchanged, as follows. \[ u_i^l[t]=\bigl(1-z_i^l[t]\bigr)\,\tilde{u}_i^l[t]+z_i^l[t]\,u_{\mathrm{reset}}^l . \] Reset-by-subtraction instead removes only the threshold that was just crossed, carrying any remainder forward into the next step, as follows. \[ u_i^l[t]=\tilde{u}_i^l[t]-z_i^l[t]\,\theta_i^l[t]. \] Both rules are read directly off the general reset term \(r_i^l[t]\) that the notation reserves for this purpose. Define the residual charge at a spike event as \(r_i^l[t]=\tilde{u}_i^l[t]-\theta_i^l[t]\), the amount by which the pre-reset candidate exceeded threshold. Reset-to-zero discards \(r_i^l[t]\) outright; reset-by-subtraction retains it inside \(u_i^l[t]\), where the membrane stage will integrate it again at the next step. This is the entire mechanical difference between the two rules, and it is the reason they are not interchangeable implementation details of the same neuron.

The semantic consequence of discarding \(r_i^l[t]\) is not zero-mean noise that a longer simulation window averages away. Because \(r_i^l[t]\) depends on the input driving the neuron rather than on any random process, reset-to-zero introduces a systematic, input-dependent bias in the resulting spike count relative to the current that produced it, one that compounds across the layers of a deep network. The derivation behind rate-coded conversion shows that reset-by-subtraction removes this bias term entirely, leaving only a bounded discretization error that shrinks as the simulation horizon \(T\) grows, rather than a persistent offset that does not . This is a cited asymptotic claim about the discrete-time model developed in Section 7, not a general property of every deployed system with finite \(T\); a numeric result reported under one target's discretization and precision should not be read as identical to a result on another target unless that other target implements the same reset stage over the same finite window.

The deployment consequence is that reset is not a property a toolchain name can be trusted to fix once. It must be tracked separately at the neuron equation, at the framework or conversion default, at the exporter or importer, and at the hardware execution primitive, because each of these boundaries can implement a different rule from its neighbor. The Neuromorphic Intermediate Representation (NIR) illustrates the first boundary directly. NIR 1.0.8's current schema encodes a post-spike reset to a fixed value and has no field for reset-by-subtraction, so a missing or null reset value is materialized as reset-to-zero rather than left as reset-by-subtraction . SpikingJelly 2.0.0rc1 illustrates the second and third boundaries together. Its generic neuron constructors default to reset-to-zero, while its own rate-coding ANN-to-SNN conversion recipe instead instantiates reset-by-subtraction neurons by default, and both its NIR exporter and its Lava exporter reject that converted default rather than silently reinterpreting it as a fixed-value reset . NxTF illustrates the hardware boundary. It recovers reset-by-subtraction on Loihi 1, whose native single-compartment primitive resets to zero, by allocating a second compartment that absorbs the residual and inhibits the first back to the correct level, which the source documents as doubling compartment allocation for every neuron configured this way; this is an emulated capability, not a native one, and the same source reports physical accuracy, execution time, and energy measurements on a Nahuku32 board, with the qualification that not every benchmark in that paper used the soft-reset configuration . Sinabs and Speck illustrate a case where the mapping is documented as native rather than emulated. Sinabs 3.1.3 defaults ANN conversion to reset-by-subtraction, and its DYNAP-CNN mapper carries that behavior into Speck's own configurable reset mode; other parts of the same route, including pooling and weight precision, remain transformed even where reset itself is preserved . A custom-microcode route for reset-by-subtraction on Loihi 2 outside the public Lava and NetX toolchains is undocumented in the sources reviewed for this survey; an absence of documentation supports neither a claim that such a route exists nor a claim that it cannot.

None of these examples licenses a general verdict about a framework or a chip family. Each states a capability status, native, transformed, or emulated, scoped to a named version and a named target, together with whatever evidence class its accompanying measurement supports. 3.5 Continuous Dynamics, Discretization, and Hardware Realization

Every membrane equation in Section 3.2 can be read at three distinct levels, and this survey keeps them separate on purpose. The first is a continuous dynamical description in physical or modeled time \(\tau\). LIF's membrane, for instance, is conventionally written as a first-order relaxation toward a resting potential under an input current \(I_i^l(\tau)\), with a membrane time constant \(\tau_m^l\) governing the relaxation rate. The second level is the numerical discretization that turns this differential equation into the recurrence \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]=\alpha^l u_i^l[t-1]+q_i^l[t]+b_i^l\) used above, at a chosen step size \(\Delta t\). A forward-Euler discretization gives \(\alpha^l=1-\Delta t/\tau_m^l\), valid only while \(\Delta t\) is small enough to keep this quantity inside \((0,1)\); the exact discretization of the same linear equation instead gives \(\alpha^l=\exp(-\Delta t/\tau_m^l)\), which remains a valid decay factor for any \(\Delta t\). These are two different, individually defensible ways of turning the same continuous model into the same discrete stage, and they do not in general produce identical trajectories for the same \(\tau_m^l\) and \(\Delta t\). The third level is hardware realization. A target executes the chosen discrete recurrence with a finite-precision state and weight representation, using bit widths \(b_u\), \(b_w\), and \(b_\theta\) that are independent design choices from one another, and it advances that recurrence according to its own tick semantics, whether a fixed hardware quantity, a configurable parameter, or a scheduling convention layered on an otherwise asynchronous substrate.

Keeping these three levels distinct matters because a claim that is true at one level does not transfer automatically to the next. Accepting a graph that specifies \(\tau_m^l\) and a reset rule does not by itself establish which discretization a given backend applies to it, and a cross-platform intermediate representation is explicit that it declares idealized primitives without constraining backend-specific integration order, spike timing, reset timing, or numerical quantization, so a syntactically accepted graph does not establish execution-equivalent dynamics across two different backends . A tick duration illustrates the same separation from the hardware side. SpiNNaker's software convention sets a default tick near one millisecond to match a biological time reference, a scheduling convention rather than a hardware limit, since finer ticks trade a proportionally slower real-time factor for finer resolution , while an asynchronous barrier-synchronized target advances a fixed-size algorithmic step whose own wall-clock duration is not fixed by the model at all . Section 4 fixes the full vocabulary needed to compare an algorithmic step, a hardware tick, and wall-clock latency without conflating them, and Section 12 returns to how execution families differ at exactly this third level.

4 Temporal Semantics, Neural Codes, and Decoding

Section 3 wrote every neuron update in terms of a discrete step index \(t\) without stating what that index means physically. It also left the emitted event \(z_i^l[t]\) undefined as a carrier of information, beyond the fact that it is a threshold crossing under the reset rule fixed at reset and refractory behavior. Neither omission is accidental. What \(t\) denotes in wall-clock terms, and what a sequence of \(z_i^l[t]\) values is defined to represent, are choices made independently of the state update in the common state update. This section fixes both choices before any later section compares a training method, a software representation, or a hardware platform. Section 4.1 separates the several distinct things a reader might mean by time. Sections 4.2 through 4.4 define a neural code as an encoder, a transmitted representation, and a decoder, then compare eight codes through that template. Section 4.5 fixes vocabulary for terms that recur, and are frequently conflated, for the remainder of the article.

4.1 Time Vocabulary

Seven distinct quantities share the word time in this literature. Continuous physical time \(\tau\) is measured in seconds and is the axis a biological or physically modeled process evolves on. The integration step or hardware tick duration \(\Delta t\) is the interval a discretization or a clocked substrate advances by; a numerical simulation and a physical clock can each define a \(\Delta t\), and the two need not agree. The discrete algorithmic step index \(t\) indexes the update order fixed in Section 3 and never by itself denotes a duration. The algorithmic inference horizon \(T\) is the maximum number of such steps a simulation or an unrolled network is allowed to run. A static layer-specific horizon \(T_l\) assigns a different maximum to each layer rather than one shared value. An input-dependent stopping step \(T_{\mathrm{stop}}(x)\) is decided during inference for a specific input rather than fixed in advance. A reported effective or averaged horizon \(T_{\mathrm{eff}}\) is a summary statistic computed after the fact from a set of per-layer or per-input values, and it inherits whatever weighting and truncation produced it. On the hardware side, the tick duration \(\tau_{\mathrm{tick}}\) is the physical time a clocked update actually occupies, a synchronization barrier is a mechanism that holds every part of a system at the same algorithmic step until all parts have finished it, and asynchronous event time is the absence of any such barrier, so that a component acts as soon as its own input arrives. Wall-clock inference latency \(\tau_{\mathrm{inf}}\) and decision latency \(\tau_{\mathrm{decision}}\) close the vocabulary. Both name a duration between a stated start event and a stated stop event; \(\tau_{\mathrm{decision}}\) may be shorter than a declared maximum window when a decision becomes available before that window elapses. None of \(T\), \(T_l\), \(T_{\mathrm{stop}}(x)\), or \(T_{\mathrm{eff}}\) is a synonym for \(\tau_{\mathrm{inf}}\). \(T\) is a step count. It is not wall-clock latency, spike count, energy, or numeric precision, and a claim that reports one of these quantities in place of another has changed what it is measuring without saying so.

The remaining risk is not the definitions themselves but four ways an algorithmic step count is regularly, and incorrectly, read as a hardware schedule. The first is a static per-layer horizon. PASCAL, the quantization-aware conversion method QAC, and its successor MT-SNN each assign a separate, fixed timestep count \(T_l\) to every layer before inference and reconcile the resulting mismatch between adjacent layers with an explicit realignment step, in each case implemented as averaging a source layer's full temporal dimension and repeating that average to the target layer's length . QAC's own hardware-efficiency appendix states directly that a pipelined multicore accelerator built to this schedule can stall, because a downstream layer must wait for an upstream layer to finish a different number of steps before it can begin . The headline efficiency number each paper reports, an average or effective timestep count, is a derived summary of this per-layer allocation. QAC's reported 2.76 average timesteps for a ResNet-18 network on CIFAR-10 is exactly this kind of summary, not a measured latency, a sum of sequential layer work, or a hardware cycle count . This distinction generalizes. A mean over per-layer or per-sample horizons carries only the weighting and truncation used to compute it; it does not carry synchronization, communication, scheduling, or stall time, and reporting it as if it were a duration substitutes an algorithmic statistic for a wall-clock measurement.

The second mechanism is per-input early exit. SEENN and Dynamic Confidence each vary a single, whole-network terminal horizon \(T_{\mathrm{stop}}(x)\) per input, stopping every layer together once a confidence threshold or a learned policy is satisfied at runtime . This is a different control variable from the static per-layer allocation above. Every layer within one inference still shares the same running step count; only the stopping point varies from one input to the next. A reported average timestep for an early-exit method is therefore a distribution over per-sample stopping times, not a set of per-layer horizons, and the two summaries should not be read as evidence for the same underlying mechanism.

The third mechanism is asynchronous execution, and it is the one most often overextended. Asynchronous core computation or event delivery does not by itself establish independent discrete time coordinates for different layers. Loihi and Loihi 2 compute and communicate asynchronously within a core, yet both platforms advance a single, global algorithmic timestep across the whole chip through a barrier synchronization handshake, and TrueNorth and Tianjic each advance time through their own chip-wide synchronization signal . Asynchronous circuitry inside a core is therefore compatible with, and on these platforms coexists with, a synchronized global tick across cores. This is the reason asynchronous execution and independent per-layer horizons appear on the list of equivalences this survey will not assert without a specific derivation or measurement. A platform being internally asynchronous is not evidence that it can host the static per-layer schedule discussed above.

The fourth mechanism is timestep-free event-driven execution, and it is the one case among the four with a physical result. On a substrate such as Speck2e there is no vector of discrete per-layer horizons to execute at all, because state updates occur strictly on event arrival. Temporal Flexibility trains a single weight set with Mixed Time-step Training, an augmentation that samples a vector of timestep counts and assigns them to network stages during each training iteration, and it deploys the resulting weights to a physical Speck2e device, reporting 99.16 percent training-time accuracy, 98.56 percent event-driven simulator accuracy, and 98.57 percent on-chip accuracy for N-MNIST, together with a 3.92 percent simulator-to-chip spike-pattern difference . This is genuine physical-hardware evidence, but it is evidence for a claim distinct from the one PASCAL, QAC, and MT-SNN make. The chip execution is fully event-driven and timestep-free; the mixed-stage timestep vector trains a temporally robust weight set, and it is not scheduled on the chip as a set of distinct discrete per-layer horizons . No source examined for this section demonstrates a static, heterogeneous, discrete per-layer timestep schedule executing on physical synchronous hardware. That narrower claim, and the two-population evidence that surrounds it, is deferred to Section 16; the point established here is only the vocabulary needed to keep the four mechanisms separate.

4.2 Encoder, Channel, and Decoder

A neuron model, fixed in Section 3, specifies how one unit's state evolves and when it emits an event. A neural code is a separate choice. It specifies what a source variable \(x\) is turned into before it enters the network, what that transformation causes to travel through the network, and what a receiver must compute to recover an estimate of the original quantity. The same integrate-and-fire update can carry a rate code, a timing code, or a graded payload depending only on how its input is prepared and how its output is read, so the neuron equations already fixed do not by themselves determine the code in use.

The three roles are an encoder \(\mathcal{E}\), a channel \(\mathcal{C}\), and a decoder \(\mathcal{D}\). The encoder maps the source variable to a current, an event train, or another representation the network can consume; the channel is the network transformation the representation passes through, characterized by its neuron model, its code, its payload, and its time model; the decoder maps the channel's output at some observation window to a decoded estimate \(\hat{x}\) or a decision \(\hat{y}\). Symbolically,

\[ \hat{x} \;=\; \mathcal{D}\Big(\mathcal{C}\big(\mathcal{E}(x)\big)\Big), \]

where every stage is defined independently. Changing the decoder while holding the encoder and channel fixed changes what is recovered from the same spike pattern; changing the encoder while holding the channel and decoder fixed changes what the same downstream mechanism is being asked to reconstruct. A frequent source of confusion is that an encoding convention is silently treated as a code. Applying a real-valued input as a constant current at every step, discussed as direct coding in Section 4.3, is an input convention. It is not automatically a neural code, because it does not by itself define an encoder that produces a spike-based representation, a channel that carries one, or a decoder that recovers a value from one.

Two quantities recur across the code comparison in Section 4.3. The spike count in a finite horizon, \(N_i^l(T) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} z_i^l[t]\), sums the same binary event \(z_i^l[t]\) that Section 3's update order emits at each step \(t\), over the closed window from the first step to the declared horizon \(T\); it is defined only for a binary discrete-time event variable, and it is not itself a rate until it is normalized. The discrete firing fraction \(\rho_i^l(T) = N_i^l(T)/T\) performs that normalization within the simulation, but it is not a physical rate unless it is further divided by a stated physical duration. Both quantities are decoder inputs, not code definitions on their own; what makes a code a rate code is the choice to read \(N_i^l(T)\) or \(\rho_i^l(T)\) as the encoded value, as Section 4.3 makes explicit for the first entry in the comparison.

4.3 Code Families

Eight codes recur in the ANN-to-SNN conversion and direct-training literature reviewed for this survey. Each is described below through the same five questions established in Section 4.2. What variable does it represent. How does the encoder produce a representation. What travels through the channel. What does the decoder compute. What horizon, precision, spike cost, and hardware requirement does the comparison in Table 3 record for it. The codes are not interchangeable ways of carrying the same information at the same cost; each trades a different resource, principally time, neurons, payload width, or absolute amplitude, for its stated cost.

Rate coding. The represented variable is a scalar, typically a non-negative activation value. The encoder is either a Poisson process whose instantaneous rate is set by the value, or a deterministic integration of a constant input current; the channel carries a sequence of binary events \(z_i^l[t]\); the decoder is the spike count \(N_i^l(T)\) or firing fraction \(\rho_i^l(T)\) defined in Section 4.2, or, in several conversion frameworks, the pre-threshold membrane potential of a non-spiking final layer read out directly . With a fixed threshold, the achievable spike counts over a \(T\)-step window are the integers from zero to \(T\), so the achievable output levels are spaced uniformly at intervals of \(1/T\); this uniform grid is a consequence of the counting operation itself, not a design choice made by any particular method . Deterministic count coding's quantization error scales as \(1/T\), while stochastic Poisson coding's variance instead scales as \(1/\sqrt{T}\), so Poisson input requires quadratically more steps for the same precision, which is why essentially all low-latency conversion work now uses deterministic input . The deployment consequence is that rate coding's cost is paid in steps, one spike per representable level in the worst case, and that cost is what Sections 8 and 9 later show low-latency conversion methods attempting to reduce without abandoning the underlying rate-matching correspondence.

Time-to-first-spike coding. The represented variable is again a scalar, but the encoder maps it to the latency of a single spike relative to a reference onset, typically with larger values producing earlier spikes; each neuron fires at most once. The channel carries one spike-timing event per neuron, and the decoder is a winner-take-all over first-spike time or a learned temporal-weighting decoder that favors earlier spikes . A single spike's continuous-valued timing carries, in principle, arbitrary precision, which is the code's appeal, but a physical substrate must quantize firing time to its own discrete tick, reintroducing a precision floor the idealized formulation does not have . The single-spike constraint also concentrates a layer's entire informational content into precise timing, which produces sparse, hard-to-train gradients relative to rate coding . Quartz is a converted, physically deployed instance of this code. Its activation representation is time to first spike with a balancing event per neuron rather than a spike count, and because each neuron fires at most once, its neurons need no soft-reset mechanism of the kind Section 3 makes load-bearing for rate coding . Quartz reports on-chip static power, dynamic power, latency, energy per inference, and energy-delay product on physical Loihi 1, at 65 timesteps for MNIST and 1211 timesteps for CIFAR-10, error rates the paper itself frames as an accuracy-energy trade against rate-coded results on the same dataset rather than an unconditional improvement .

Phase coding. The represented variable is a scalar. The encoder assigns each spike a weight given by its position within a repeating cycle of \(K\) timesteps, typically the powers of two \(2^{K-1}\) down to \(2^0\), analogous to binary place value . The channel carries binary events whose phase within the cycle is meaningful; the decoder sums the weights of the phases at which a spike occurred. Because the weights are powers of two, the representable outputs are subset sums of a geometric sequence, an exponentially weighted grid of \(2^K\) levels rather than the uniform \(1/T\) grid rate coding produces, so precision grows exponentially in cycle length rather than linearly in step count. The cost is bounded by the cycle length shared by the whole network rather than by any single spike; one report finds phase coding alone requiring on the order of three thousand simulation steps for a 32-layer network on CIFAR-100, and a related bistable-neuron method combining phase coding with a bistability mechanism reports the same order of magnitude for CIFAR-100 . Phase coding is documented as a software or training-time convention layered on an ordinary binary-spike substrate in the sources examined for this section rather than as a dedicated hardware decoding primitive, which is an absence in the material reviewed and not a demonstrated impossibility.

Burst coding. The represented variable is a scalar and the encoder is the same spike-count mechanism as rate coding, with one change. The neuron is permitted to emit more than one spike within a single timestep when its residual post-threshold membrane potential remains large, lifting the one-spike-per-step ceiling ordinary rate coding assumes . The channel therefore carries the same unit-spike count as rate coding, but that count can be packed into fewer timesteps because several unit spikes may occur in one step. The decoder is unchanged from rate coding, a count over the observation window. The hardware requirement is a neuron capable of multi-spike emission within one step; Speck documents such a mode, but the specific deployment cited alongside it in fact used a plain, single-spike-per-step neuron rather than the multi-spike variant, which is a documented capability that this section does not treat as an exercised route for that deployment .

Population coding. The represented variable can again be a scalar, but the encoder distributes it across the activity pattern of a population of \(N\) neurons, for example through per-neuron tuning curves, rather than through any single neuron's rate or timing. The channel carries the joint activity of the population, and the decoder is an ensemble readout over that activity. Population coding is best understood as an orthogonal, spatial axis rather than a fifth primitive alongside rate and temporal codes, since each neuron inside the population can itself be rate coded or time coded internally . Its cost is paid in neurons rather than in steps, which is the opposite trade from every temporal code above. Adaptive Fission illustrates the trade concretely. It replaces additional temporal integration with a group of binary neurons carrying unequal fixed weights, proposing a population code layered on top of existing converted or directly trained networks rather than a change to rate coding itself, and it reports physical execution on a Lynxi HP201 accelerator with per-epoch memory, time, and system energy measured from board sensors, a system-level rather than a core-only measurement boundary .

Sigma-delta coding. The represented variable is again a scalar activation, typically one that changes slowly from step to step. The encoder is a delta transmitter that emits a spike only when the change in activation since the last transmission exceeds a threshold, and that spike carries the size of the change as its payload rather than a count of unit events. The channel therefore requires a payload wider than one bit; a sigma-delta spike's precision is not equivalent to an ordinary binary spike, and reading it as one discards the information the code depends on. The decoder is a running sum that reconstructs the activation from the sequence of transmitted changes. Loihi 2 is the confirmed native case, supporting graded spikes that carry up to 24 bits of signed integer magnitude directly in the spike message . Brehove et al. build a sigma-delta conversion pipeline on this feature and report latency and energy-delay product measured on physical, multi-chip Loihi 2 hardware against a Jetson Xavier baseline for a video object-detection workload, transmitting signed graded activation deltas rather than a firing rate estimated from binary counts. Loihi 1 lacks a native graded-spike channel and approximates the mechanism with a two-compartment adaptive neuron at twice the compartment cost, which one group uses to reach a reported state of the art for on-chip speech classification with three-bit quantization-aware weights . Lava-DL documents sigma-delta blocks as a first-class, trainable layer type; this is a documented software capability, and this section records it as such rather than as an independently exercised deployment .

Rank order coding. The represented variable is not a magnitude at all. Each neuron in a population fires at most once, modeled as an analog-to-delay converter in which a more strongly driven neuron crosses threshold sooner, and the encoded content is purely the relative order in which the population's neurons fire during one feedforward wave . The channel carries that ordering; the canonical decoder is a shunting-inhibition mechanism in which each arriving spike progressively desensitizes the postsynaptic neuron, so an afferent's influence decays with its arrival rank . With \(N\) neurons the code's channel capacity is on the order of \(\log_2(N!)\) bits, obtained with roughly \(N\) spikes in a single feedforward wave rather than the count a rate code would need over a multi-step window . Rank order coding is contrast-invariant, because rescaling an input's intensity changes every latency but not their relative order, and this robustness is purchased by discarding absolute amplitude entirely; the ordering alone cannot recover how large the original values were . This is also why the code sits outside the rate-matching correspondence formalized in Section 7. Every conversion proof surveyed for this article, matching a per-layer firing rate between an artificial network and a spiking network, is stated over a magnitude, and an ordinal object drawn from \(N!\) possible permutations has no natural target for that kind of convergence argument . The unifying framework of Deneve et al. places the code precisely among its relatives; time-to-first-spike coding reads a continuous timing value, rank order coding discards timing and keeps only order, and N-of-M coding discards order as well in exchange for needing only binary synapses. The clearest measured hardware comparison is MorphIC, a 65-nanometer four-core digital processor on which rate coding reaches 97.8 percent accuracy at 205 microjoules per classification on the same chip and the same MNIST task where rank order coding reaches 95.9 percent accuracy at 21.8 microjoules per classification, a measured tenfold energy reduction for a 1.9-percentage-point accuracy cost . BrainChip's Akida processor carries a genuine rank-order-coding lineage; the company acquired Simon Thorpe's rank-order-coding research program in 2016, and an independent trade-press account corroborates a design pivot from an earlier rate-coding approach to rank coding . BrainChip's own documentation and an independent community summary describe the native input-encoding stage as rank-order-coded . The standard QuantizeML-to-CNN2SNN deployment route that essentially all published Akida results use, however, maps a uniformly quantized integer model to the runtime without a user-exposed multi-step firing-rate window, and independent physical-hardware studies and direct inspection of the toolkit's source both describe the converted path as a single-pass, order-agnostic threshold comparison rather than an order-sensitive mechanism operating inside hidden layers . This converted route must not be classified as ordinary multi-step rate coding simply because the vendor calls the result a spiking network; it is a one-pass evaluation whose relationship to either rate coding or rank order coding is narrower than either label suggests. A dedicated application-specific rank-order-coding circuit with unsupervised local plasticity separately reports 90.2 percent accuracy at 6.79 microjoules per classification on MNIST on fabricated 65-nanometer silicon .

Direct coding. The represented variable is again a scalar, typically a normalized pixel intensity or another real-valued input feature. What Section 4.2 calls direct coding here is, strictly, an input convention rather than a spike code. Instead of an encoder that produces a spike train, a real-valued input is applied as a constant analog current to a layer's neurons at every step, and only that layer's output is spiking. Rueckauer et al. introduce this alongside soft-reset semantics and weight normalization, reporting that switching a first layer from Poisson spike-train input to constant analog current, with all other settings fixed, raised CIFAR-10 accuracy substantially in a controlled comparison. There is no channel-level spike code and no decoder at the input stage; the cost is instead architectural. The layer receiving direct current performs a genuine multiply-accumulate on real-valued input rather than an accumulate over binary events, forfeiting for that one layer the accumulate-only efficiency the rest of a spiking network is built to exploit, an overhead reported as small relative to the network as a whole precisely because it is confined to a single layer .

4.4 Cross-Code Trade-Offs

The eight codes divide along the resource each one spends to buy precision. Rate and burst coding spend steps, filling a fixed \(1/T\) grid one unit at a time; phase coding spends a shared cycle length to buy an exponential grid, so its precision grows faster in cycle length than rate coding's grows in step count, at the price of a per-network cost that scales with the deepest cycle any layer requires. Time-to-first-spike coding spends timing resolution instead of step count, which is why it can in principle reach high precision in a single spike, but a physical tick grid puts a floor back under that precision once the code leaves the idealized continuous-time formulation. Population coding spends neurons rather than time, which makes it attractive precisely where neuron budget is cheaper than step count, and unattractive where it is not. Sigma-delta coding spends payload width, exchanging one wide graded spike for the many binary spikes a rate code would need to carry the same change, but only where the underlying activation is well behaved enough that changes are infrequent and a graded-spike channel exists to carry them. Rank order coding spends none of these and instead gives up amplitude entirely, trading a magnitude for an ordering that a single wave can transmit.

These trades interact with robustness in ways a cost count alone does not show. A rate code degrades gracefully under small perturbations to individual events, because the decoder only needs an approximately correct count. A rank order code is comparatively fragile to timing jitter when several neurons' latencies cluster closely, because the decoder depends on their relative order rather than on any single event's exact position . A sigma-delta code is only efficient when the represented signal changes slowly enough that few deltas cross threshold; a rapidly varying input erodes exactly the temporal redundancy the code is built to exploit. None of these trade-offs is visible from a spike count or a step count in isolation, which is the reason Table 3 records represented variable, decoder, and hardware requirement as separate fields rather than collapsing the comparison to a single cost number.

A second interaction concerns training rather than execution. Codes that concentrate information into few events, most sharply time to first spike and rank order coding, correspondingly concentrate the gradient signal available to a learning rule into few events, which is part of why time-to-first-spike coding trains comparatively slowly despite its fast inference convergence . A code choice is therefore not independent of the learning route that will be used to obtain the weights that exploit it, a dependency Section 5 develops once both learning branches are on the table.

5 Learning Paradigms and the Two Branches

Sections 2 through 4 fixed what a spiking model computes and what a code represents once weights and thresholds are already given. Obtaining those weights and thresholds is a separate problem. A network can be optimized directly in its spiking form, or a conventional artificial neural network (ANN) can be trained first and its parameters re-expressed as spiking dynamics afterward. This section states why the first option is harder than ordinary backpropagation, introduces the resulting fork into direct training, conversion, and hybrid methods, and names the point at which every route must again agree. Section 6 bounds the direct-training branch to what later sections need. Sections 7 through 9 derive and extend conversion, the survey's principal branch, in full.

5.1 The Spike Learning Problem

An ANN unit's output \(a^l\), fixed in Section 2.1, is a differentiable function of its input by construction. A static nonlinearity such as a rectifier has a well-defined derivative almost everywhere, and the chain rule propagates a loss gradient back through every layer without further assumption. A spiking unit instead emits \(z_i^l[t]=\mathcal{H}\!\left(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]-\theta_i^l[t]\right)\), the threshold stage fixed in Section 3.1, where \(\mathcal{H}\) is a Heaviside step function. Its derivative with respect to the pre-reset candidate is zero everywhere except at the crossing point, where it is undefined, \[ \frac{\partial z_i^l[t]}{\partial \tilde{u}_i^l[t]} = \mathcal{H}'\!\left(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]-\theta_i^l[t]\right) = 0 \ \text{almost everywhere.} \] The semantic reason is the one Section 2.1 already gave for why a single event carries almost no information on its own. A spike is a binary decision, not a smoothly varying quantity, so the local sensitivity ordinary backpropagation needs at every stage does not exist for it in the mathematical sense used above. The consequence is not that a gradient is merely small, as happens for a saturated sigmoid. Automatic differentiation applied literally to \(\mathcal{H}\) returns zero at every non-crossing step, which stops a learning signal from reaching an earlier layer or an earlier step through this path entirely.

The severity of this obstruction is not the same for every code fixed in Section 4.3. A code that spreads a represented value across many events over a window, such as rate coding, gives a learning rule many chances across the window for a small parameter change to move some event closer to or further from its threshold. A code that concentrates a value into very few events, most sharply time-to-first-spike and rank order coding, correspondingly concentrates whatever local gradient signal survives into those same few events, part of why time-to-first-spike coding trains comparatively slowly despite its fast inference convergence, a dependency Section 4.4 already flagged from the coding side. The two branches below are best read as two different responses to the one obstruction stated above, not as competing claims about which produces the more accurate network.

5.2 Direct Training, Conversion, and Hybrid Methods

Direct training keeps the network in spiking form throughout optimization and works around the obstruction in Section 5.1 rather than avoiding it. One family changes only what happens during the backward pass, replacing \(\mathcal{H}'\) with a smooth surrogate function so that a gradient exists to propagate while the forward pass still emits the exact binary \(z_i^l[t]\). A second family drops the requirement for a loss gradient propagated backward through the whole network and instead updates each synapse from signals already available locally to it. Section 6 takes up both to the depth this survey needs and refers outward for the remainder.

ANN-to-SNN conversion avoids the obstruction rather than working around it. A conventional ANN is trained by ordinary backpropagation, entirely in the value-based representation of Section 2.1, and only afterward is each unit's activation re-expressed as an integrate-and-fire neuron's spike count or timing under a stated correspondence. No spiking unit exists during training, so the non-differentiable threshold of Section 5.1 is never encountered by the optimizer. Section 7 derives the correspondence this depends on and one complete conversion pipeline. Section 8 and Section 9 locate and address the error that correspondence accumulates once the simulation horizon is short.

Hybrid methods sit between the two and close a gap that neither route resolves alone. One pattern trains an ANN component and a spiking component separately, each in its own native representation, and joins them at inference through a toolchain built for that purpose. NengoLoihi has been used in exactly this way to deploy a hybrid ANN-plus-SNN model to physical Loihi 1. A second pattern trains a network directly by gradient descent and then applies a hand-designed correction before deployment rather than during training. The mapless-navigation policy of Tang et al. is trained end to end by gradient descent in simulation and is then passed through a Loihi-specific eight-bit integer weight-rescaling step, closing the gap between the training-time numeric format and the chip's native one before the corrected weights are measured on physical Loihi. A hybrid method is therefore not a third optimization principle alongside the two above. It is a stated sequence of steps drawn from them, and what each step preserves or changes has to be stated explicitly rather than left to the label alone. Conversion-specific calibration and fine-tuning, which correct a converted network's spike statistics after the fact, are a further instance of this same pattern and are addressed at their intervention point in Section 9 rather than here.

5.3 Where the Branches Converge

Whichever route produces the weights, the result is still a spiking network of the form fixed in Section 3, carrying a code of the form fixed in Section 4. It must be representable in software, mappable onto a target's neuron cores, and executable within a stated timing model before a deployment claim about it means anything. A network obtained by direct training and a network obtained by conversion do not automatically arrive at this point the same way. Direct training frequently fixes the neuron model, code, and precision to a specific target from the outset, since the gradient or the local rule is computed on the same dynamics the target will run. Conversion fixes these choices afterward, as a separate mapping step from an ANN that was never told during its own training what hardware would eventually execute it. Both are statements about how a given network was obtained, not about which targets it can eventually reach.

What the two branches share is the requirement itself, not a common set of values for it. Section 10 makes that requirement explicit as a single contract that a directly trained network and a converted network are both asked to satisfy before either is compared on a target. No field of that contract is needed here. What matters at this point is only that direct training, taken up next, and conversion, taken up beginning in Section 7, are read against the same eventual requirement rather than as two unrelated literatures.

5.4 Evidence Guide

The single requirement named in Section 5.3 is not graded paper by paper. A deployment claim about a spiking network is graded by what was actually executed and measured to support that specific claim, whether the claim concerns latency, energy, accuracy, or a route that has merely been documented. One paper routinely carries claims of several different classes at once, an accuracy figure measured on physical silicon alongside a route that was only ever described and never run. Grading at the claim level keeps a strong result reported in one part of a paper from lending unearned credibility to a weaker claim stated nearby in the same publication. The five classes below sort claims along that axis, and every deployment claim in the sections that follow this one is read against them rather than against the reputation of the paper that reports it. They are not a quality ranking of the work that produced them.

6 Direct SNN Training

This section explains direct training only as far as a deployment claim requires. It is not a substitute for a dedicated treatment of spiking-network learning algorithms, and it says so explicitly at the boundaries in Section 6.7. The organizing question throughout is not which credit-assignment mechanism trains most accurately, but what each mechanism commits a resulting network to before that network reaches a target, and what the audited evidence in this survey actually shows about that commitment being exercised on physical hardware.

6.1 Local Learning and STDP

A local learning rule updates a synaptic weight \(w_{ij}^l\) from quantities already available at that synapse, typically the relative timing or recent history of the presynaptic and postsynaptic events \(z_j^{l-1}\) and \(z_i^l\), rather than from a loss gradient propagated backward through every later layer and step. Spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP), the best known instance, strengthens or weakens a synapse according to whether the presynaptic spike arrives shortly before or shortly after the postsynaptic one. The mechanism requires no global error signal and no unrolled trajectory of the kind Section 6.4 returns to, which is exactly what makes it attractive for a device that cannot afford to store one.

The audited deployment evidence for local learning is dominated by a narrower case than a general STDP training pipeline for an arbitrary task. Its strongest confirmed instances are hand-designed spiking circuits, in which the designer fixes the dynamics to solve one specific problem and plasticity, where present, adapts a bounded part of that fixed structure online rather than learning the structure itself. Imam and Cleland deploy an olfactory-bulb-model circuit that learns odor identity online through one-shot local plasticity on physical Loihi silicon of a generation the record does not specify, reaching E1 measurement. The Locally Competitive Algorithm, a sparse-coding circuit whose dynamics provably converge to a target optimum by construction rather than by training, reaches the same evidence class on physical Loihi 2 in the implementation by Parpart et al., and on Loihi, generation not given in the record, in the implementation by Tang et al.. A constraint-satisfaction solver built the same way reaches the same class on Loihi, generation likewise unspecified, in the implementation by Yakopcic et al.. Robotics contributes a further cluster at the same evidence class. Kreiser et al. report an iCub head-pose estimation circuit with on-chip plasticity on physical Loihi, a Kapoho Bay board, with the chip generation unspecified in the record. The NeuroPod hexapod central pattern generator and a goalkeeper robot's perception-to-action mapping from Cheng and Nikolić both run on SpiNNaker, generation unspecified in the record. A head-direction simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) circuit from Tang et al. runs on Loihi, generation likewise unspecified.

Because each of these circuits is designed for one task rather than learned from a labeled dataset by a general-purpose rule, the resulting system does not generalize the way a trained classifier does, and the design effort does not transfer automatically to a new task the way retraining a fixed architecture does. The deployment-relevant consequence is a distinction this survey will not blur. Local learning reaching hardware, in the evidence audited here, is largely a claim about hand-designed circuits with bounded local plasticity, not a demonstration that a general STDP training procedure reaches physical silicon for arbitrary tasks the way the gradient-based mechanisms in the next two subsections do. Absence of that broader demonstration in the audited corpus is not evidence that it is impossible, only that it was not found.

6.2 Surrogate Gradients and Backpropagation Through Time

A surrogate-gradient method keeps the exact binary forward pass \(z_i^l[t]=\mathcal{H}\!\left(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]-\theta_i^l[t]\right)\) fixed in Section 5.1 and changes only the backward pass, substituting a smooth surrogate derivative \(\sigma'(\cdot)\) for the Heaviside derivative at the same argument, \[ \frac{\partial z_i^l[t]}{\partial \tilde{u}_i^l[t]} \;\approx\; \sigma'\!\left(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]-\theta_i^l[t]\right), \] so that automatic differentiation has a nonzero local sensitivity to propagate at every step. Because the reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\) at step \(t\) determines the membrane state \(u_i^l[t]\) that stage \(\mathcal{M}^l\) reads at step \(t+1\), the network is unrolled across the full horizon \(T\) and treated as a deep, weight-tied recurrent graph, an application of backpropagation through time (BPTT) to the staged update of Section 3.1. The surrogate derivative is an approximation to the true one everywhere except exactly at the crossing point, so the resulting weight update is a descent direction for the surrogate loss landscape rather than an unbiased estimate of the gradient of the true, non-differentiable one. Backpropagating through this recurrence also means backpropagating through whichever reset rule, reset-to-zero or reset-by-subtraction, was assumed at Section 3.4, a choice a direct-training method must state rather than leave implicit, since it changes what the surrogate gradient is computed against.

Surrogate-gradient training is the mechanism behind most of this survey's audited direct-training deployments that reach physical hardware with an event-native workload and a vendor-supplied training-to-deployment pipeline. On event-based vision, Richter et al.'s own head-to-head comparison on physical Speck1 trains an integrate-and-fire convolutional network directly by BPTT and finds it reaches higher on-chip accuracy than an ANN-to-SNN converted baseline measured on the identical chip, 98.50 percent against 86.17 percent, at a higher energy cost per inference, 180 microjoules against 141 microjoules. Related BPTT-trained networks reach physical Speck and DYNAP-CNN silicon and the CRI field-programmable gate array (FPGA) platform built at UC San Diego, both at E1. On biosignal decoding, gradient-trained recurrent networks for electromyography (EMG) gesture classification and a graded-spike network for electroencephalography (EEG) seizure prediction are measured on physical Loihi and Loihi 2. On robotics, the mapless-navigation policy of Tang et al. and an industrial peg-in-hole force-control policy are trained end to end by gradient descent and measured on physical Loihi. Both robotics policies feed continuous sensor or proprioceptive state as a constant current to the first layer during training, the direct input convention already fixed in Section 4.3, rather than pre-encoding it into a spike train.

The consequence carried forward is not that surrogate-gradient training outperforms conversion in general. Speck1's own comparison, the only same-chip, head-to-head measurement of the two routes found in this survey's audited evidence, reports a specific accuracy advantage at a specific energy cost for one workload on one chip, not a general ranking. The evidence boundary is exactly that narrow, and Section 6.4 returns to the memory cost this training method pays for that result.

6.3 Online and Hardware-in-the-Loop Learning

Two further mechanisms respond to the surrogate-gradient family differently rather than replacing it outright. Exact spike-time gradient methods, including SLAYER, EXODUS, and EventProp, differentiate analytically through the timing of the spike-generation event itself rather than substituting a surrogate slope for the same crossing, an alternative formulation rather than an approximation to the mechanism in Section 6.2. A SLAYER-trained convolutional network reaches physical Loihi 1 through the NxTF compiler for DVS Gesture classification, with latency and energy measured on-chip. EventProp, trained through mlGeNN and exported through NetX, deploys a leaky integrate-and-fire network with learned synaptic delays to physical single-chip Loihi 2 for keyword spotting on the Spiking Heidelberg Digits and Spiking Speech Commands benchmarks, reported eighteen times faster and two hundred fifty times lower in energy than an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano baseline at batch size one, both at E1.

Hardware-in-the-loop (HITL) training is the most literal sense of online learning in the audited evidence, because the forward pass executes on the physical target device at every training iteration rather than on a simulator standing in for it, and only the backward pass runs on a host, using either an approximation to the device's true derivative or a measured trace injected into automatic differentiation. On BrainScaleS-1, a naive rate-coded transfer of an MNIST classifier without HITL correction collapses accuracy from 97 percent in software to 72 percent on the physical wafer-scale chip, recovering only to 95 percent, still short of the software figure, once the HITL correction is applied. On BrainScaleS-2, hxtorch.snn inserts measured membrane traces sampled from the physical chip directly into PyTorch's automatic-differentiation graph and trains with surrogate gradients computed from that injected physical trace rather than from a simulated one; the published Yin-Yang classification demonstration reports 94.63 plus or minus 0.7 percent accuracy over fifteen seeds on physical silicon, an E2 claim scoped to that one demonstration rather than a general capability statement about the platform.

Both mechanisms trade one cost for another rather than eliminating cost outright. Exact spike-time gradients still require the full trajectory needed to evaluate the analytic spike-time derivative, so they inherit the memory profile Section 6.4 describes for BPTT despite avoiding the surrogate approximation. Hardware-in-the-loop training substitutes a physical forward pass for a simulated one, but the collapse-and-recovery result above shows that substitution is not free either; it still needs a hardware-specific backward model or an instrumented physical readout, engineering built for one target that does not transfer to a different chip without being rebuilt.

6.4 Training Cost, State Unrolling, and Memory

Because the reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\) at step \(t\) determines the membrane state \(u_i^l[t]\) that stage \(\mathcal{M}^l\) reads at step \(t+1\), computing a gradient at any step depends on every earlier step through this recurrence, exactly as in a recurrent neural network. Backpropagating through that dependency chain, whether by the surrogate derivative of Section 6.2 or the exact spike-time derivative of Section 6.3, requires retaining every intermediate pre-reset and post-reset state across the whole horizon \(T\) before the backward pass can run. Training memory therefore grows with \(T\) and network depth jointly, unlike the single, memoryless evaluation of an ANN unit fixed in Section 2.1.

This is the reason truncated backpropagation windows, checkpointing, and gradient approximations recur across the direct-training literature, and it is also the reason local and eligibility-trace mechanisms trade exactness for a memory profile that does not grow with \(T\). The eligibility-trace formulation of Bellec et al., already used in Section 3.2 for the adaptive-threshold neuron, keeps one running per-synapse trace instead of the full trajectory, at the cost of approximating the credit a distant past step should receive rather than computing it exactly. 6.5 Hardware-Aware and On-Chip Learning

A hardware-aware training procedure incorporates a specific deployment target's numeric or temporal constraints into the objective or the gradient computation itself, rather than training a target-agnostic network and relying on a later mapping step to preserve its behavior. Arfa et al. combine hardware-aware fine-tuning with eight-bit quantization to deploy spiking Q-networks, reinforcement-learning policies rather than classifiers, to physical SpiNNaker2, preserving a rate-coded, subtract-reset network evaluated over ten or twenty ticks and reaching E1 measurement. This is direct Q-learning with surrogate-gradient fine-tuning for one target, not ANN-to-SNN conversion.

On-chip learning is a narrower and distinct claim from hardware-aware training. It means the weight update itself executes on the neuromorphic device, rather than on a host machine that trains offline and later downloads a fixed weight set for inference. Whether a platform's documented on-chip learning machinery is exercised by a given deployment route is a capability question, and it uses the capability vocabulary fixed for the rest of this survey, native, transformed, emulated, host-assisted, unsupported, and undocumented. 6.6 What Direct Training Fixes for Deployment

Across every mechanism audited in this section, from hand-designed local plasticity to hardware-in-the-loop gradients, direct training's defining deployment property recurs. The neuron dynamics, code, precision, and timing model used during optimization are, in the audited evidence, generally the ones executed at inference, because the gradient or the local rule is computed on that exact representation rather than on a separate proxy. A directly trained network's parameters are consequently often tied to the specific assumptions used to obtain them, a platform's particular reset rule, its exact quantization, its exact timing model, more tightly than a converted network's are, since conversion has not yet made those choices at the point its ANN is trained, the asymmetry Section 5.3 already previewed.

This tying is a property of how a given network was obtained, not a general claim that direct training cannot produce a portable network, and no absolute claim to that effect is made here. What direct training contributes to the deployable contract formalized in Section 10 is a network whose neuron model, code, and precision fields are typically settled jointly with its weights, at the cost of the training-time memory and hardware-specific engineering documented in Section 6.4 and Section 6.5. Section 7 turns to the branch that inverts this order, training in a representation with none of these commitments and choosing them only afterward.

7 Classical ANN-to-SNN Conversion

Section 5 introduced conversion as the second branch of the fork, training a conventional network with ordinary backpropagation and only afterward re-expressing it in spikes. This section makes that re-expression precise. It fixes one derivation, built directly on the staged update and reset term of Section 3 and the firing fraction and horizon vocabulary of Section 4, and one end-to-end pipeline that every later comparison in this survey refers back to rather than re-deriving. The pipeline has five stages, named once here and used identically for the remainder of the article. Normalization removes network elements that do not fit the staged update of Section 3.1 as they stand, chiefly batch normalization, max pooling, and unbalanced residual branches. Parameter mapping chooses the integrate-and-fire weights, thresholds, and biases that make the mapped network's firing fractions track the trained network's activations. Encoding places the network's input onto the mapped network's first layer. Temporal execution runs the staged update for a finite horizon \(T\). Readout decodes a class score from the final layer. Sections 8 and 9 later locate specific error mechanisms and specific interventions at these same five named stages, so a claim there that names "the encoding stage" or "the normalization stage" refers to exactly the stage fixed here.

7.1 Purpose and Architectural Assumptions

Conversion exists because training a spiking network directly, through the surrogate-gradient machinery of Section 6, remains harder to scale and slower to converge than training a conventional rectified linear unit (ReLU) network with backpropagation and the mature tooling built around it. Conversion instead asks a narrower question. Given a network already trained to convergence with ordinary methods, can its computation be re-expressed exactly, or with a bounded and characterized error, using only integrate-and-fire units, binary events, and the reset rule fixed in Section 3.4. The earliest instances of this question were asked of shallow or fully connected networks and hand-built spiking equivalents , and Stromatias et al. answer a related version of it for a deep belief network mapped onto SpiNNaker before the convolutional, ReLU-specific pipeline this section derives was formalized. The derivation below is specific to that later, ReLU-specific pipeline.

The derivation and the pipeline built on it hold only under a stated set of architectural assumptions, and the failure modes catalogued in Section 7.8 are exactly the consequences of violating one of them. First, every nonlinearity the source network applies between affine layers is a ReLU, so every target activation is non-negative and, on the calibration data the mapping is built from, bounded by a finite per-layer value. Second, every connection is feedforward with zero delay between layers, \(d_{ij}^l=0\) in the notation of Section 3.1, deferring configurable synaptic delay to the platform-specific treatment in Section 12. Third, batch normalization, where present, uses fixed running statistics rather than batch statistics computed at inference time, so that it is a fixed affine map rather than a data-dependent one. Fourth, pooling and residual connections are restricted to the forms Section 7.5 shows fit the derivation, or are rewritten into those forms before mapping. Fifth, the per-step synaptic drive into every unit is bounded, so that the membrane potential defined in Section 3.1 stays within a fixed range independent of the horizon \(T\); Section 7.2 uses this bound directly, and Section 7.8 returns to what happens when it is violated.

7.2 The ReLU-to-Firing-Fraction Correspondence

Specialize the staged update of Section 3.1 to the IF instance fixed in Section 3.2, with the feedforward, zero-delay synaptic stage \(q_i^l[t]=\sum_j w_{ij}^l z_j^{l-1}[t]\) and the fixed threshold \(\theta_i^l\), and apply reset-by-subtraction from Section 3.4. Writing the three stages as one recurrence for the post-reset state, \[ u_i^l[t] = u_i^l[t-1] + q_i^l[t] + b_i^l - z_i^l[t]\,\theta_i^l , \] and summing both sides over \(t=1,\ldots,T\) telescopes the \(u_i^l[t-1]\) terms and leaves \[ u_i^l[T]-u_i^l[0] = \sum_{t=1}^{T} q_i^l[t] + T b_i^l - \theta_i^l \sum_{t=1}^{T} z_i^l[t] = \sum_{t=1}^{T} q_i^l[t] + T b_i^l - \theta_i^l N_i^l(T), \] using the finite-horizon spike count \(N_i^l(T)\) fixed in Section 4.2. Because \(q_i^l[t]\) is linear in the presynaptic events, \(\sum_{t=1}^{T} q_i^l[t] = \sum_j w_{ij}^l N_j^{l-1}(T) = T\sum_j w_{ij}^l \rho_j^{l-1}(T)\), where \(\rho_j^{l-1}(T)=N_j^{l-1}(T)/T\) is the discrete firing fraction of Section 4.2. Substituting this and dividing by \(T\theta_i^l\) gives \[ \rho_i^l(T) = \frac{1}{\theta_i^l}\left(\sum_j w_{ij}^l \rho_j^{l-1}(T) + b_i^l\right) - \epsilon_i^l(T), \qquad \epsilon_i^l(T) := \frac{u_i^l[T]-u_i^l[0]}{T\,\theta_i^l}. \]

This identity is exact for every finite \(T\ge 1\). It is not an approximation, and nothing in its derivation has yet invoked a limit; \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) is defined so that the identity holds by construction, and the entire content of the correspondence is in how \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) behaves. Under the bounded-drive assumption of Section 7.1, standard arguments in the conversion literature bound the membrane excursion \(|u_i^l[T]-u_i^l[0]|\) by a constant independent of \(T\) whenever the neuron's asymptotic firing fraction sits strictly inside \((0,1)\), the regime away from the ReLU's own zero and saturation edges, which gives \(\epsilon_i^l(T) = O(1/T)\), the discretization error \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\) of the notation . At the two edges the identity still holds exactly but for a different reason. A unit whose net drive stays negative for the whole horizon never crosses threshold, so \(\rho_i^l(T)=0\) at every \(T\), matching \(\mathrm{ReLU}\) at zero without the residual needing to shrink at all; a unit whose drive would require firing on every step saturates at \(\rho_i^l(T)=1\) for the same reason. Section 7.7 turns this per-neuron identity into a statement about the whole network as \(T\to\infty\), and Section 8 names the regime where the bound on \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) is not yet small at the \(T\) actually used as one of its finite-time error mechanisms.

7.3 Input Encoding and Output Readout

The identity above already fixes what happens at every hidden layer once its input is a spike train with a well-defined firing fraction. The first and last layers are boundary cases, because the network's actual input is not itself the output of an IF neuron and its actual output is read before any further layer would consume it as spikes.

At the input, the classical pipeline uses direct or analog current injection rather than a Poisson process, treating each input value as a constant per-pixel current \(q_i^1[t]=\sum_j w_{ij}^1 x_j\) held fixed for the whole horizon, where \(x_j\) is the corresponding entry of the trained network's own input tensor. This is a stronger claim than merely being one option among the code family of Section 4.3, because it changes which error term dominates at the first layer. Constant injection makes \(q_i^1[t]\) exactly time-invariant, so the bound on \(\epsilon_i^1(T)\) from Section 7.2 applies with no additional variance term. A Poisson-rate encoding of the same input instead adds the code's own stochastic variance, which Section 4.3 gives as scaling with \(1/\sqrt{T}\) against the deterministic count's \(1/T\), a quadratically worse rate for the same horizon. Rueckauer et al. report this difference directly on a controlled ablation at matched \(T\), in which switching the first layer from Poisson-rate input to direct analog injection reduces the same converted network's classification error on CIFAR-10, with every other setting held fixed E5, a software-simulation result rather than a hardware measurement. This is why essentially all classical and later conversion work encodes its input this way, and why the derivation in Section 7.2 is written for a deterministic drive.

At the output, the classical pipeline does not require the final layer to spike at all. Setting the final layer's threshold high enough that it never fires, or equivalently never applying the threshold and reset stages to it, turns \(\mathcal{M}^L\) into a pure accumulator, and summing its recurrence over \(T\) steps the same way as in Section 7.2, but with no reset term to track, gives \(u_i^L[T] - u_i^L[0] = \sum_{t=1}^{T} q_i^L[t] + T b_i^L\), with the initial potential retained exactly as Section 7.2 retains it. This section adopts the convention that the readout accumulator starts from a zero initial membrane potential, \(u_i^L[0]=0\). Under that convention, dividing by \(T\) gives \(u_i^L[T]/T = \sum_j w_{ij}^L \rho_j^{L-1}(T) + b_i^L\) exactly, with no residual \(\epsilon\) term at all; a nonzero initial value would instead carry the extra term \(u_i^L[0]/T\) into the right-hand side, a term that vanishes as \(T\) grows but is not itself zero at any finite \(T\). This is the class-accumulator decoder named among the options in Section 4.2, and it is why the final layer's decoded score is not subject to the same finite-count error that a spiking hidden layer carries. The deployment consequence is that only the hidden layers between input and output pay the discretization cost derived in Section 7.2; the choice to decode logits from an unreset accumulator rather than from a spike count is a design choice available because the final layer's output never needs to be re-encoded as an event train for a further layer to consume.

7.4 Parameter Mapping

Section 7.2 leaves the weights, thresholds, and biases of the mapped network unspecified beyond linearity; it holds for any values, which means it does not by itself tell a practitioner what to enter into \(w_{ij}^l\), \(\theta_i^l\), or \(b_i^l\). Parameter mapping is the separate question of which values make the mapped network's decoded activations reproduce the trained network's own pre-activations rather than an arbitrary rescaling of them.

Fix a per-layer calibration constant \(\lambda^l\), the scale symbol of the notation, chosen from the trained network's own activation statistics on a calibration set, either the observed maximum or a more outlier-robust percentile of it ; Sengupta et al.'s SPIKE-NORM instead estimates the same constant online, from spikes produced by a short simulation of the partially mapped network rather than from the source network's own activation statistics directly. Define the decoded value at layer \(l\) as \(\hat a_i^l(T):=\lambda^l \rho_i^l(T)\), read directly from a hidden layer's firing fraction, or, at the final layer, from the unreset accumulator of Section 7.3. Setting the mapped network's threshold to the calibration constant, \(\theta_i^l=\lambda^l\), and its weight to the trained weight rescaled by the previous layer's constant, \(w_{ij}^l=\lambda^{l-1}\tilde w_{ij}^l\), with the bias carried over unchanged, \(b_i^l=\tilde b_i^l\), turns the identity of Section 7.2 into \[ \hat a_i^l(T) = \sum_j \tilde w_{ij}^l\, \hat a_j^{l-1}(T) + \tilde b_i^l - \lambda^l \epsilon_i^l(T), \] which is the trained network's own affine pre-activation, evaluated at the previous layer's decoded value rather than its true activation, minus the same bounded residual carried forward from Section 7.2. Different papers place the ratio \(\lambda^{l-1}/\lambda^l\) on the weight, on the threshold, or on an explicit rescaling of the activation itself; these are algebraically equivalent packagings of the same normalization, and a reader comparing two papers' stated weights or thresholds directly, without checking which packaging each uses, will compare numbers that are not on the same footing.

Because the firing fraction \(\rho_i^l(T)\) is bounded in \([0,1]\) by construction, a spike count cannot fall below zero or exceed the horizon, the decoded value \(\hat a_i^l(T)=\lambda^l\rho_i^l(T)\) is confined to \([0,\lambda^l]\) regardless of how large or negative the right-hand side of the equation above becomes. This is the origin of the clipping this literature repeatedly reports, and it is a structural consequence of representing a value as a bounded count, not an artifact of a particular calibration choice. A calibration constant set too small clips activations the source network actually produces; one set too large wastes the achievable \(1/T\) resolution over a range the activations never use. Choosing \(\lambda^l\) well is exactly the estimation problem the parameter-mapping methods above solve, and refining that estimate under a fixed, small \(T\) is the subject of the low-latency interventions Section 9 covers; this section fixes only the mapping's structure, not its optimization.

7.5 Architecture Preparation

Three components of a trained convolutional network do not fit the staged update of Section 3.1 as trained, and each must be rewritten before parameter mapping applies. Table 8 collects the three together with the mechanism each preparation step preserves.

Batch normalization in evaluation mode is a fixed per-channel affine map built from running statistics, \(\hat y = \gamma(y-\mu)/\sqrt{\sigma^2+\delta}+\beta\), which composes algebraically with the affine map of the convolution or linear layer immediately before it, \[ w' = w\cdot\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\delta}}, \qquad b' = b\cdot\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\delta}} + \beta - \frac{\gamma\mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2+\delta}}, \] folding the two layers into one affine layer with weight \(w'\) and bias \(b'\) ahead of the ReLU. A batch-normalization layer computed from batch statistics at inference time is not this fixed map and does not fold this way, which is why the assumption in Section 7.1 restricts to the evaluation-mode case.

Average pooling is a fixed linear operator on its input window, so it commutes exactly with the substitution in Section 7.2's derivation; forwarding an averaged spike count rather than a single binary event changes the payload carried on that connection from binary to graded, in the sense of Section 3.2, but introduces no new error term. Max pooling is not linear in its inputs, and no exact analog of the firing-fraction identity holds for it; an instantaneous "any input fired" gating rule approximates the operation but can overestimate the pooled unit's rate relative to the single highest-rate input channel, which is why Rueckauer et al. document this approximation explicitly and why the great majority of classical and low-latency conversion pipelines replace max pooling with average pooling before mapping rather than attempting the approximation.

A residual or skip connection sums two branches that were calibrated independently, an identity path and a residual branch, each with its own \(\lambda^l\). Summing their raw spike trains at the merge point implicitly assumes both branches are expressed on the same scale; when they are not, the merged drive entering the shared post-add threshold no longer corresponds to the sum the trained network actually computed. Sengupta et al.'s extension of the pipeline to VGG and residual architectures handles this by rescaling both branches to a shared calibration constant before the merge, which is the architectural preparation this survey groups under normalization for residual networks specifically.

7.6 Walkthrough

Assembling Sections 7.3 through 7.5 in execution order rather than exposition order gives the pipeline named in the opening paragraph, normalization, parameter mapping, encoding, temporal execution, readout, applied here to one trained network end to end. Normalization first rewrites the trained graph, folding every batch-normalization layer into its preceding affine layer, replacing any max-pooling layer with average pooling, and rescaling every residual branch to a shared calibration constant, leaving a graph of affine layers, ReLU nonlinearities, and linear pooling. Parameter mapping then estimates a calibration constant \(\lambda^l\) for every layer from a held-out calibration batch and sets each layer's integrate-and-fire threshold and rescaled weight from Section 7.4's identity, carrying every bias over unchanged. Encoding injects the network's actual input tensor as a constant per-pixel current into the first layer for the entire horizon, as fixed in Section 7.3, rather than sampling any stochastic process. Temporal execution then runs the staged update of Section 3.1 for \(T\) discrete steps at every hidden layer, with reset-by-subtraction at every threshold crossing, producing a firing fraction \(\rho_i^l(T)\) at every hidden unit that Section 7.2 already characterizes. Readout accumulates the final layer's membrane potential without ever resetting it and divides by \(T\), giving the class scores from which the argmax decision is taken.

\(T\) here is the algorithmic horizon fixed in Section 4.1, a count of discrete update steps, not a wall-clock duration and not a proxy for it. A smaller \(T\) shortens the sum each unit accumulates and enlarges the bound on \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) from Section 7.2; it does not by itself establish a shorter inference latency on any particular target, because what one step of temporal execution costs in wall-clock time depends on the execution model that Sections 4.1 and 12 treat separately from the step count itself.

7.7 Asymptotic Equivalence and Finite-Time Behavior

Composing the identity of Section 7.4 across the \(L_{\mathrm{net}}\) layers of the mapped network gives, for the output layer, a sum of the finite-\(T\) residuals \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) accumulated at every hidden layer the output depends on, each weighted by the path of trained weights connecting that layer to the output. Two statements follow from this composition, and this survey states them separately rather than as one claim, because only one of them is exact at every finite \(T\) actually used.

The asymptotic statement is a limit. As \(T\to\infty\), with the calibration constants, weights, and thresholds of Section 7.4 held fixed, every per-layer residual \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) in the interior regime of Section 7.2 shrinks toward zero, and the network's decoded output converges to the trained network's own forward pass evaluated through a clipped ReLU, \(\mathrm{clip}(\cdot,0,\lambda^l)\), at every layer. This is the content of Rueckauer et al.'s convergence result for the reset-by-subtraction rule, and Ding et al. state and prove a version of the same layer-by-layer firing-rate correspondence, matching a magnitude between a source network and a converted network, for general feedforward and residual architectures. Both results are asymptotic; neither author claims the limit is reached at any finite \(T\), and this survey makes the same restriction explicit rather than leaving it implicit.

The finite-time statement is what Section 7.2's identity gives directly, and it does not vanish at the horizons any deployed system actually runs. Each hidden layer carries a residual bounded on the order of \(1/T\) in the interior regime, exact at the two clipped edges, and these residuals do not stay confined to the layer where they arise. A hidden layer's own residual enters the next layer's synaptic drive as part of \(\rho_j^{l-1}(T)\), so a shallow network's error at some finite \(T\) is not simply the single largest per-layer term but a composition of per-layer terms along every path from input to output, weighted by the trained weights on that path. This composition has no universal closed form or growth law derivable from Section 7.2 alone, an instance of the prohibition, in the notation's terms, against assuming additivity or independence of named error mechanisms without a specific cited derivation. Section 8 is where this survey names and separately bounds the individual mechanisms, clipping, discretization, and the mismatch between a reset-to-zero and reset-by-subtraction trajectory, that this compounded finite-\(T\) residual decomposes into, rather than treating "the conversion error" as one undifferentiated quantity.

Neither statement licenses substituting one algorithmic quantity for another. Convergence as \(T\to\infty\) is convergence in the horizon defined in Section 4.1, not in wall-clock time; a claim that a smaller \(T\) achieves lower latency requires the separate, target-specific argument that Section 12 supplies for a named execution model, not an appeal to this section's asymptotic result.

7.8 Where the Derivation Does Not Apply

Each architectural assumption fixed in Section 7.1 has a corresponding failure case, and naming them here closes the loop the assumptions opened rather than leaving them as unenforced fine print.

A source network that uses a negative-valued or non-monotonic activation, rather than a ReLU, has no non-negative target for \(\rho_i^l(T)\in[0,1]\) to converge toward, since the derivation in Section 7.2 relies on the decoded value \(\hat a_i^l(T)=\lambda^l\rho_i^l(T)\) being confined to \([0,\lambda^l]\) by construction. Representing a signed target requires a signed or dual-channel emission scheme distinct from the single non-negative event this section assumes, an intervention Section 9 covers rather than a case this derivation extends to on its own.

A source network with recurrent connections, attention, or normalization layers computed from live batch or sequence statistics breaks the feedforward, fixed-weight linearity that Section 7.2's substitution \(\sum_{t=1}^T q_i^l[t]=T\sum_j w_{ij}^l \rho_j^{l-1}(T)\) depends on; if \(w_{ij}^l\) or the set of contributing units varies with \(t\) or with the input itself, no analogous closed-form telescoping identity has been established by the sources this section relies on.

A per-step synaptic drive that is not bounded independent of \(T\), violating the fifth assumption of Section 7.1, leaves the membrane excursion \(|u_i^l[T]-u_i^l[0]|\) unbounded in \(T\) as well, so the \(O(1/T)\) rate for \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\) no longer follows; a sustained, strongly negative drive without a floor on the membrane potential is the clearest instance, and it is a modeling and implementation detail this section flags rather than resolves.

Rank order coding, defined among the code families of Section 4.3, lies outside this correspondence for a reason traceable directly to Section 7.2's own construction rather than to a general property of non-rate codes. The derivation needs a magnitude per neuron, a spike count \(N_i^l(T)\) that can be normalized into a firing fraction; a rank-order channel carries no such magnitude, because each neuron fires at most once per wave and the encoded content is that neuron's rank among the population, not any count attached to it in isolation . There is accordingly no \(\rho_i^l(T)\) to define for a single rank-order unit, and every conversion proof this survey locates, including Rueckauer et al.'s and Ding et al.'s , is stated over a matched magnitude rather than a matched ordering, consistent with Section 4.3's statement that this is why the code sits outside the rate-matching correspondence.

These are the assumptions Section 8 traces as the entry points of finite-time conversion error, clipping from a mis-calibrated \(\lambda^l\), discretization from the bound on \(\epsilon_i^l(T)\), and the reset-rule mismatches Section 3.4 already anticipates, and they are the targets Section 9 organizes its interventions around, most of which relax exactly one of the assumptions fixed here while leaving the rest of this section's pipeline unchanged.

8 Finite-Time Error and Low-Latency Conversion

Section 7 derives the correspondence between a ReLU-activated ANN and a converted SNN under an asymptotic assumption, an inference horizon \(T\to\infty\) at which the discrete firing fraction \(\rho_i^l(T)\) defined in Section 4 exactly reproduces the corresponding activation. Every claim in this article that describes a converted network running with a finite, deliberately small \(T\) is a claim about what that asymptotic derivation leaves out. This section names each thing it leaves out, states where in the pipeline it enters, whether it is a fixed structural consequence of the architecture or a random consequence of the input encoding, and how it interacts with the reset semantics fixed in Section 3. Section 9 organizes its intervention methods by which of these named mechanisms they target; this section exists so that organization has fixed referents.

8.1 A General Finite-Time Error Model

Write the finite-window deviation of a converted neuron's discrete firing fraction from its asymptotic target as \[ \delta_i^l(T) \;=\; \rho_i^l(T) \;-\; \lim_{T'\to\infty}\rho_i^l(T'), \] evaluated at the layer index \(l\), unit index \(i\), and closed window \([1,T]\) fixed by Section 4's definition of \(N_i^l(T)\) and \(\rho_i^l(T)\). This survey organizes \(\delta_i^l(T)\) into four named contributions using the symbols \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\), \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\), \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{reset}}\), and \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{time}}\) reserved for this purpose, \[ \delta_i^l(T) \;\approx\; \epsilon_{\mathrm{clip},i}^l \;+\; \epsilon_{\mathrm{disc},i}^l(T) \;+\; \epsilon_{\mathrm{reset},i}^l[T] \;+\; \epsilon_{\mathrm{time},i}^l[T]. \] The approximate sign is deliberate. This is an organizational device for exposition, not a derived identity. The literature proves an additive bound for a narrower claim than the one written above, decomposing the error of a specific converted activation function into a clipping term and a discretization term under a stated reset rule and a stated finite window ; that proof does not cover the timing term introduced in Section 8.4, and no source reviewed for this survey proves that all four terms are independent, additive, or governed by one growth law across architectures and reset rules. Treat the equation as a labeling scheme for the rest of this section, not as a formula to evaluate.

Where does \(\delta_i^l(T)\) come from mechanically. The classical pipeline traced in Section 7 runs five named stages in order, normalization, parameter mapping, encoding, temporal execution, and readout. Each of the four error terms above enters at one of these stages, and a layer's total deviation is whatever the temporal execution stage has accumulated by the time it reaches that layer's own contribution. Section 8.2 locates \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\) and \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\) at parameter mapping and at the counting operation itself. Section 8.3 locates \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{reset}}\) at the reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\) fixed in Section 3.1. Section 8.4 separates a dispersion component of \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{reset}}\), called unevenness error, from \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{time}}\), a structurally distinct term tied to propagation across depth rather than to reset. Section 8.5 and Section 8.6 then trace how normalization, pooling, residual paths, depth, encoding, and readout change how much of each term a given layer inherits.

Where in this pipeline finite-time error visibly enters, and how it can grow, shrink, or interact as a signal passes through the stages a real converted network uses, batch normalization folding, average or max pooling, a residual summation, and a non-spiking readout, is a structural question rather than a numeric one; no single measured trace generalizes across architectures, reset rules, and horizons. 8.2 Clipping and Discretization Error

Clipping error, \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\), is a mechanism of the parameter mapping stage, not of the neuron dynamics that follow it. Before conversion, that stage fixes a firing threshold \(\theta_i^l\), or equivalently an activation scale \(\lambda^l\) in the sense Section 7 assigns that symbol, so that the neuron's finite range of achievable spike counts stands in for the ANN activation's range. Any input drive that would have produced an ANN activation above the value that scale represents is truncated at the maximum achievable count, exactly as a hard upper clip truncates a real-valued activation. The semantic effect is a value-dependent, one-sided bias, present only for inputs at or above the chosen scale, that does not shrink as the simulation horizon \(T\) grows, because it is a property of the chosen scale rather than of the counting resolution. The trade-off this creates is between two threshold-setting failure modes on opposite sides, a scale set too low clips frequently and biases every affected unit downward, while a scale set too high wastes achievable count levels on activation values the network rarely produces, which is itself a discretization cost. Early parameter mapping methods, the weight and threshold balancing procedures of the first conversion papers, exist specifically to choose this scale from a calibration pass over training data, an unresolved tension the field returns to repeatedly rather than a solved constant . This is a mechanism claim about where and why the bias appears, not a measured accuracy result, and it is unconditional on the reset rule in Section 3.4, since a spike count cannot exceed the finite window \(T\) regardless of which reset stage is chosen.

Discretization error, \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\), is a mechanism of the counting operation itself, present even for an input drive that a correctly scaled threshold would not clip. Section 4 establishes that a fixed threshold restricts the achievable values of \(N_i^l(T)\) to the integers from zero to \(T\), so the achievable firing fractions \(\rho_i^l(T)\) form a uniform grid spaced at intervals of \(1/T\) . Whatever real-valued activation the ANN side would produce, the SNN side can only represent the nearest point on that grid, a flooring operation whose worst-case magnitude is bounded by the grid spacing itself and therefore shrinks as \(T\) grows. The semantic effect is a bounded, deterministic rounding error rather than the unbounded, value-dependent bias clipping produces. The trade-off is direct, halving the grid spacing means doubling \(T\), so discretization error is the term that most directly ties accuracy to simulation length, and it is the term every low-latency conversion method in Section 9's activation-shaping family is built to compress without simply enlarging \(T\) . The deployment consequence is that clipping and discretization are separable mechanisms that a single calibration step cannot both fix at once, choosing a scale narrow enough to keep the achievable grid fine costs clipped range, and choosing a scale wide enough to avoid clipping costs grid resolution at the same fixed \(T\); a formal treatment of this trade-off as a joint optimization over both quantities exists and is itself an inference claim about the shape of that trade-off, not a hardware measurement .

8.3 Residual Membrane Potential and Reset Error

Reset error, \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{reset}}\), is a mechanism of the reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\) that Section 3.4 already makes load-bearing for the asymptotic derivation. Recall the reset term \(r_i^l[t]=\tilde{u}_i^l[t]-\theta_i^l[t]\), the residual charge left over the instant a neuron crosses threshold. Section 3.4 shows that reset-to-zero discards \(r_i^l[t]\) outright and reset-by-subtraction retains it for the membrane stage to integrate again at the next step, and that only the second rule removes a persistent, input-dependent bias as \(T\to\infty\) . Reset error is what remains at any finite \(T\) even under reset-by-subtraction, because the window closes before every unit of residual charge has necessarily been converted into an output spike. At the final step \(t=T\), whatever membrane potential \(u_i^l[T]\) sits below threshold is charge the neuron accepted but never spent, and it is discarded by the readout the moment the window ends regardless of which reset rule governed the steps inside that window. The semantic effect is a mean-level bias in \(N_i^l(T)\) that depends on the neuron's own charge history over the closed window, not a random fluctuation that a resimulation would erase.

Naming this quantity residual membrane potential rather than folding it silently into discretization error matters because its size is not fixed by the grid spacing alone. A neuron's leftover charge at cutoff depends on where in its own integration cycle the window happened to end, which depends in turn on every upstream layer's own finite-time behavior, so the bias compounds with depth rather than staying at one layer's fixed grid resolution. This is exactly the failure mode a line of work formalizes for deep and residual architectures under uniform, hard reset, terming it a residual membrane potential problem and reporting worse degradation as network depth increases . A closely related line explicitly targets a converted network's initial membrane condition, \(u_i^l[0]\), as the free parameter that determines how much residual charge a finite window can accumulate before it starts producing this bias, and a separate line targets the same bias by explicitly modeling and subtracting the expected residual term at conversion time . A third diagnostic line observes that the resulting output spikes are not merely fewer or more numerous than the target but offset from the value the discretization grid alone would predict, naming this an offset-spike phenomenon rather than treating it as ordinary discretization noise . All four are mechanism and inference claims about where this bias originates and how it scales with depth, not hardware measurements; none of the source papers audited for this survey report execution on physical neuromorphic silicon, a gap Section 15 returns to for the low-latency conversion literature generally. The deployment consequence is that reset error cannot be fixed by lengthening \(T\) alone in a deep network, because added depth adds new residual-charge accumulation points faster than a longer window drains any single one of them.

8.4 Unevenness Error and Timing Error

Unevenness error and timing error are not the same mechanism, and this survey does not use them interchangeably. Unevenness error is a dispersion of residual charge across units that share a layer and a target rate. Timing error is a structural propagation delay tied to network depth. A network can suffer from one without the other.

Unevenness error is the part of \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{reset}}\) that a single-neuron, mean-value account of residual charge misses. Two units with the same target firing fraction \(\rho_i^l(T)\) over a shared window need not carry the same instantaneous input history inside that window, because the individual arrival times of their presynaptic events, set by whatever unevenness of upstream firing already exists, differ even when the two windows' totals agree. Since the reset stage \(\mathcal{R}^l\) and the membrane stage \(\mathcal{M}^l\) are stateful rather than simple accumulators, two input trains with an identical mean rate but a different within-window arrival pattern leave different amounts of residual charge \(r_i^l[t]\) at the window's end. This is the mechanism the QCFS line of analysis isolates as a third, separate contribution to finite-time error beyond clipping and discretization, one that a value-independent scale correction and a value-independent count-resolution improvement do not reach . The semantic effect is that two neurons converted from the same ANN activation value can carry different amounts of finite-time error depending on where in the network they sit and what firing pattern their inputs happen to produce, so unevenness error is heterogeneous across a layer rather than a single per-layer constant. The deployment consequence is that a per-layer average error estimate, the kind a calibration pass over a validation set naturally produces, can understate the worst-affected units in that same layer.

Timing error, \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{time}}\), is a different mechanism located at the temporal execution stage rather than at the reset stage. Under the layer-synchronous update order Section 3.1 fixes, a unit at layer \(l\) cannot register an event fired at layer \(l-1\), step \(t\), before step \(t+d_{ij}^l\), where \(d_{ij}^l\) is the synaptic delay Section 3.3 reserves that symbol for, at minimum one step when no explicit delay is modeled. A signal entering the network at step 1 therefore cannot influence a unit at depth \(l\) before step \(l\), so the deepest layers of a network with many layers spend part of any short window simply waiting for the first wave of events to arrive, a fixed structural cost that a wider threshold scale or a finer discretization grid cannot remove. This cost is why depth interacts with a small \(T\) even when clipping, discretization, and reset error are all individually small, a constraint the literature on very deep converted networks documents directly by needing larger simulation windows and dedicated reset treatments specifically as convolutional depth increases . Where timing error is deliberately made unequal across layers, by assigning a different simulation length to each layer rather than treating it as an unavoidable propagation cost, the resulting per-layer realignment is a Section 9 intervention rather than a Section 8 mechanism, and this section stops at naming the underlying cost without describing that remedy.

8.5 Architecture-Dependent Accumulation

The four mechanisms above are defined for a single unit and a single layer. What a specific architecture experiences is how many times, and in what combination, each unit's output is passed through another unit that adds its own contribution. Section 7's normalization stage assumes a specific set of transformations, batch normalization folded into the preceding weight and bias, average pooling, and an explicit prescription for residual summation, precisely because these are the transformations the finite-time account below needs to hold as claimed.

Batch normalization folding is exact in the asymptotic derivation, the folded scale and bias reproduce the trained network's normalized activation as \(T\to\infty\). At finite \(T\) this exactness does not carry over unchanged. The folded bias becomes an added constant current that the membrane stage integrates over the same closed window as every other input, so it is clipped by the same threshold, discretized by the same grid, and left with the same residual charge at cutoff as any other contribution to \(\tilde{u}_i^l[t]\). Folding a normalization layer into the weight and bias removes a separate computational stage, it does not remove that stage's finite-time error, and treating a folded network as automatically equivalent to its unfolded ANN counterpart at low \(T\) is an inference this survey does not make without a cited derivation covering the folded case specifically.

Pooling divides along exactly the line Section 4's linearity properties predict. Average pooling is a linear combination of its inputs, so it commutes with the spike-count readout the same way it commutes with a real-valued sum, and a pooled unit's finite-time error is a direct, unweighted composition of its inputs' own \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{clip}}\), \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\), and \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{reset}}\) terms rather than a new error source. Max pooling is not linear in spike counts, the maximum of several finite, integer-valued counts over a shared window is not obtainable from a weighted sum of those counts by any fixed set of synaptic weights, so a converted network built with max pooling needs a separate approximation of the pooling operation itself, and that approximation is a fifth, architecture-specific error source outside the four named above, one this survey does not attempt to characterize in general because its magnitude is a property of the specific approximation chosen rather than of the pooling operation itself . The deployment consequence is that Section 7's architecture-preparation assumption of average pooling is not a stylistic simplification, a network trained with max pooling either needs retraining with average pooling before the rest of this section's error account applies to it, or it carries an additional, separately characterized error term that this taxonomy does not cover.

Residual summation compounds the timing term specifically. A residual block sums an identity shortcut path against a transformed path that has passed through additional convolutional, normalization, and activation stages, so the two operands entering that summation have unequal depth measured from the network's input, and by the timing mechanism in Section 8.4, unequal depth means unequal minimum propagation delay before either operand's events can meaningfully reflect the input driving them. The two operands also carry unequal accumulated clipping, discretization, and residual error, since one has passed through strictly fewer stages than the other. Two documented lines of work address exactly this combination, one by tracking how conversion accuracy degrades specifically as convolutional depth and residual connections are added , and one by introducing a reset treatment aimed at the resulting residual-charge accumulation in deep and residual architectures specifically . Depth is therefore not merely a multiplier on a single-layer error rate, each additional layer contributes its own instance of all four mechanisms and its own minimum propagation delay, and a residual architecture adds the further complication of summing two branches whose accumulated error and delay are not equal to begin with.

The readout stage inherits whatever the deepest layer before it has accumulated rather than adding a fixed increment of its own. A spike-count or firing-fraction readout applies one more instance of \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\) on top of everything upstream. Reading the pre-threshold membrane potential of a non-spiking final layer directly, a decoder choice Section 4.2 already names among \(\mathcal{D}\)'s options, removes that one final discretization stage, since the readout no longer counts events at all, but it does not remove or correct any clipping, discretization, reset, unevenness, or timing error already embedded in the real-valued input that final layer receives from the layer below it . The deployment consequence is that a non-spiking readout is a bound on where error can still be introduced, not a correction of error already present.

8.6 Encoding and Readout Error

Sections 8.2 through 8.5 treat every mechanism as deterministic given a fixed input trajectory, which is the correct treatment for the direct or analog encoding convention, injecting a real-valued input as a constant current rather than as a Poisson process, that Section 7's own walkthrough and the great majority of the low-latency conversion literature adopt at the encoding stage . Under that convention, rerunning the same input through the same converted network with the same finite \(T\) reproduces the same \(\delta_i^l(T)\) exactly, because nothing in the encoder \(\mathcal{E}\) is random. Every bound and every mechanism named above is a deterministic-encoding claim in this sense, a fixed function of the input and the finite window, not a distribution over repeated runs.

A Poisson-rate encoder changes this at the source rather than downstream. Section 4.3 already establishes that deterministic count coding's quantization error scales as \(1/T\) while stochastic Poisson coding's variance instead scales as \(1/\sqrt{T}\) . The mechanism reason is that a Poisson encoder makes the input event train \(z_i^{0}[t]\) itself a random variable with the correct value only in expectation, so every downstream quantity built from it, \(N_i^l(T)\), \(\rho_i^l(T)\), and every error term in Table 10, becomes a random variable rather than a deterministic function of the input, and its spread does not close as fast as a deterministic encoder's bias does for the same increase in \(T\). The semantic effect is that a stochastic-encoding result reported at a given \(T\) describes a distribution over repeated draws, and a single reported accuracy number at that \(T\) is one sample from that distribution rather than a bound. The trade-off is why deterministic direct encoding, not Poisson encoding, is the near-universal convention in the low-latency conversion literature specifically, quadratically more steps are needed under Poisson encoding to match a deterministic encoder's precision at the same \(T\) , and the deployment consequence is that a stochastic-encoding and a deterministic-encoding result at the same nominal \(T\) are not directly comparable claims about accuracy at that horizon.

The decoder side carries a parallel, non-equivalent distinction. Choosing which of \(\mathcal{D}\)'s readout options, count, rate, first-spike time, membrane state, or a class accumulator over several output units, to use does not by itself introduce or remove stochastic variance; that property is fixed at the encoder. What the decoder choice changes is which of the deterministic terms in Table 10 the readout is exposed to, a count or rate readout adds one more instance of \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\), as Section 8.5 already states, while a membrane-state readout instead exposes whatever residual and unevenness error sits in that final layer's pre-threshold potential without the count-related bounding property Table 10 gives \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{disc}}\). Conflating a decoder's exposure to deterministic finite-window error with the encoder's stochastic variance is exactly the confusion this subsection exists to prevent, the two are governed by different mechanisms, enter the pipeline at opposite ends, and do not trade off against each other by adjusting \(T\) alone.

8.7 The Design Problem Finite-Time Error Creates

No single mechanism in this section is the barrier to low-latency conversion. Clipping, discretization, residual membrane potential, unevenness, and timing are five structurally distinct terms, entering at different pipeline stages, with different growth behavior in \(T\), compounded further by normalization, pooling, residual paths, and depth. A method that only targets one of these terms leaves the others in place.

This section has deliberately stopped short of describing how any of these mechanisms is corrected. That is Section 9's question, and Section 9 organizes its answer by which of the mechanisms named here a given intervention targets, rather than by chronology or by author. The design problem Section 9 answers is not simply how to reduce \(T\). A method that reduces the discretization grid's spacing at a given \(T\) without addressing residual membrane potential or timing error leaves those terms exactly as large as before, and a smaller \(T\) obtained this way is a claim about the number of algorithmic steps a network requires, not, on its own, a claim about wall-clock inference latency \(\tau_{\mathrm{inf}}\), a distinction Section 4.1 already makes and this survey does not relax for the interventions that follow. The problem Section 9 must solve is therefore which mechanism, or combination of mechanisms, a given intervention can address without silently reintroducing another, at a stated finite \(T\), under a stated reset rule, for a stated architecture.

9 Advanced Conversion Methods

Section 8 traces five error mechanisms to the pipeline stage where each one enters: clipping and discretization error, residual and reset error, unevenness and timing error, architecture-dependent error, and encoding and readout error. Each mechanism marks a point where a finite, discrete simulation departs from an assumption Section 7's classical derivation holds fixed. This section organizes the methods that intervene against those errors by the point in the pipeline where each intervention acts, not by publication venue or year. Activation shaping intervenes before conversion, on the ANN's own training objective. Potential and reset calibration and neuron interventions intervene on the neuron itself, changing what Section 3 holds fixed as the reset rule or the neuron-model family for the specific case of a classically converted network. Temporal allocation and adaptive inference intervene on the schedule, replacing Section 7's single global horizon with one that varies by layer or by input. Alternative-code conversion and hardware-aware conversion intervene on the correspondence itself, replacing rate matching with a mapping onto a different code or a specific target platform. Hybrid fine-tuning and emerging architectures intervene after conversion or across the boundary between the two learning branches introduced in Section 5. Evidence class and hardware status are stated per method below, never assumed from the group a method belongs to.

9.1 Activation Shaping

A rate-coded IF neuron under reset-by-subtraction accumulates a spike count that is, by construction, a uniform \(T\)-level quantizer, as Section 4.3 establishes: with a fixed threshold, the achievable output levels over a \(T\)-step window are the integers from zero to \(T\), spaced at intervals of \(1/T\). Classical conversion, described in Section 7, trains the ANN independent of this grid and imposes it only afterward, which is the mechanism Section 8's clipping-and-discretization error attributes error to. Activation shaping intervenes earlier, training the ANN's own activation function to anticipate the grid before conversion happens. This is an ordinary quantization-aware training step applied to the ANN, not a hardware intervention, and it should be read as such rather than as a claim about the SNN's own execution. Bu, Fang, Ding, Dai, Yu, and Huang's Quantization Clip-Floor-Shift (QCFS) activation replaces the ANN's ReLU with a clip-floor-shift nonlinearity trained to anticipate the target IF neuron's step response, reporting 93.96 percent CIFAR-10 accuracy at \(T=4\) and further results from \(T=8\) up to 512 and beyond, all in GPU simulation. Read this way, the clip-floor-shift function is an activation fake-quantizer of the kind used throughout conventional quantization-aware training. This survey's claim index does not carry a verified claim establishing that the two constructions are bit-identical, so that correspondence is stated here as an interpretive reading of the published mechanism, not as a verified finding.

Three later papers keep the same intervention point and vary what the trained nonlinearity looks like. Yang, Yang, Zhang, Dou, Shen, and Zhao's CS-QCFS gives each channel its own per-channel, softplus-shaped, guaranteed-positive threshold, reporting a \(T=1\) headline accuracy on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100. Li, Ma, and Furber's QFFS makes the quantization framing explicit rather than implicit, training with an LSQ-style quantization-aware quantizer before conversion and stating an explicit analog-coded input convention, reaching \(T=4\) on CIFAR-10 and \(T=8\) or 10 on ImageNet. Jiang, Anumasa, De Masi, Xiong, and Gu's SlipReLU generalizes the activation to a weighted-ReLU-plus-step form with a shift term and reports a claimed one-step, \(T=1\), conversion, with further results at \(T=2\) and \(T=4\). All three, like QCFS itself, report GPU simulation accuracy only; none names a target chip inside an experiments section, and all four papers in this paragraph and the one above are E5.

Datta, Liu, Diffenderfer, Kailkhura, and Beerel's When SNN Meets ANN keeps the same intervention point, a modified IF neuron that postpones firing and reset until after input-current accumulation together with a batch-normalization bias adjustment, reporting a \(T=4\) headline on ImageNet. Its own appendix, titled Deployment of Proposed SNN on Loihi, is the clearest instance in this literature of a documented-looking route that stops short of an exercised one. The appendix reports a PyTorch-versus-Lava-DL accuracy comparison, which is E5 evidence for the proposed method, and separately asserts a Lava-DL-to-Loihi path without naming a Loihi generation, a physical board, or a device measurement, which keeps that route at E3 rather than a deployment claim . Section 9.7 returns to this paper as the negative case against which genuinely hardware-aware conversions are compared.

None of the four activation-shaping papers above reports its own hardware execution. A later, unrelated paper does place one of them on physical silicon: Adaptive Fission's hardware table includes an unmodified, no-Fission QCFS configuration measured on a physical Lynxi HP201 accelerator, reporting per-epoch time and system energy from board sensors, an E1 result for a baseline the QCFS paper itself never deployed . Section 9.2 records the parallel case for SRP.

9.2 Potential and Reset Calibration

A second family keeps the classical IF neuron and the rate code intact and instead changes what happens to membrane potential at the start of inference and at reset, intervening directly against the residual-and-reset error Section 8 attributes to reset-by-subtraction's carried-over charge and to an implicit zero initial condition. Bu, Ding, Yu, and Huang's Optimized Potential Initialization (OPI) sets a non-zero initial membrane potential, one half of threshold, for IF neurons under reset-by-subtraction, targeting a horizon of 32 steps or fewer with further results reported from 8 up to 512 and beyond. Hao, Bu, Ding, Huang, and Yu's Residual Membrane Potential method (SRP) is built directly on the QCFS activation and adds a second, calibration-only pass that reads out the residual membrane potential left after ordinary inference, reducing the reported headline to as few as 4 steps, with 8, 16, and 32-step alternatives. Both papers report GPU simulation accuracy only and are E5.

As with QCFS in Section 9.1, a later hardware paper places SRP's own unmodified baseline configuration on physical silicon. Adaptive Fission's hardware table includes a no-Fission SRP row measured for per-epoch time and system energy on a physical Lynxi HP201 accelerator, an E1 result the SRP paper itself did not report, using board sensors that closed interfaces prevent from separating into core-only figures . Read together, the QCFS and SRP rows in that table establish that rate-coded QCFS-lineage networks have reached silicon, while also confirming that neither proposing paper reported that deployment itself, a distinction this survey preserves rather than collapsing into a single verdict about the lineage.

9.3 Neuron Interventions

A third family leaves the ANN's activation function and the reset rule largely alone and instead redesigns the IF neuron itself, changing the neuron-model assumption Section 3 holds fixed for the specific case of a classically converted network and intervening against Section 8's clipping-and-discretization error by changing what a single step's spike budget can represent. Li and Zeng's burst-spike IF neuron lifts the one-spike-per-step ceiling ordinary rate coding assumes, letting a neuron emit several unit spikes within one step when its residual post-threshold potential remains large, paired with a pooling layer the paper calls LIPooling, and reaches sub-100-step headlines with ablations extending to 32 steps and beyond; the burst-coding row in Section 4.3's Table 3 already fixes the code this neuron implements. Wang, Zhang, Chen, and Qu's Signed Neuron with Memory (SNM) permits a compensating negative spike alongside neuron-wise normalization, targeting headlines at or below 128 steps with tables extending to 1024. Hu, Zheng, Jiang, and Pan's Fast-SNN converts a quantized ANN into a signed IF neuron that resets by adding the threshold on a negative spike, fine-tuned layer by layer, at \(T\) of 3, 7, or 15 across classification, detection, and segmentation workloads. Liu, Zhao, Chen, Wang, and Jiang's SpikeConverter introduces an inverse-leaky IF neuron with a temporal-separation scheme, reducing the reported headline to 16 steps. All four report GPU simulation accuracy only and are E5. Fast-SNN cites Loihi's documented support for signed spikes as design justification for its own signed-IF neuron; the paper reports no execution on that or any other chip, and the citation should be read as motivation for a design choice, not as a deployment claim .

9.4 Temporal Allocation

Section 4.1 already fixes the vocabulary a mixed-timestep paper needs to be read correctly: a static per-layer horizon \(T_l\) is a different control variable from wall-clock latency, and the average or \(T_{\mathrm{eff}}\) each paper reports is a derived layer statistic, not a measured duration. What that discussion leaves for this section is each method's target and its hardware evidence. PASCAL, QAC, and MT-SNN intervene against Section 8's unevenness-and-timing error by discarding Section 7's assumption of one global \(T\) shared by every layer, assigning instead a layer-specific quantization level and therefore a layer-specific unrolled timestep count, reconciled between adjacent layers by an explicit temporal-alignment step . The accuracy results for all three are E5, software simulation reported against an averaged \(T_{\mathrm{eff}}\) rather than a measured duration. A separate reproducibility finding narrows how PASCAL's own headline should be read: the published ResNet-18/CIFAR-10 \(T_{\mathrm{eff}} = 3.14\) is reproducible from the released script only when its 17 listed per-layer horizons are silently truncated to the 10 energy coefficients the script's zip() call actually consumes, a mapping neither the paper nor the code documents.

Two later accelerator papers do synthesize hardware for the neuron this family introduces, though neither synthesizes the mixed-horizon schedule itself. Ramesh and Srinivasan's own PASCAL paper separately reports a register-transfer level (RTL) synthesis and cycle-accurate simulation of its PASC-IF neuron at one uniform \(T=4\) design point, in modified LoAS and SparTen accelerator models synthesized with Synopsys Design Compiler at 560 MHz in a 40-nanometer library, an E4 result that tests only the neuron's overhead relative to a standard IF neuron and does not exercise Adaptive Layerwise mixed-horizon execution. Venkatesh, Radhakrishnan, Rakshit, and Srinivasan's APEX integrates the same PASC-IF neuron natively into the LoAS accelerator's dataflow as a fully combinational circuit, synthesized across a \(T \in \{4, 8, 16\}\) and INT4/INT8 design-space sweep at 400 MHz in 40-nanometer CMOS, reporting 69.23 millijoules per inference at \(T=8\) against a 181.76-millijoule LoAS baseline at \(T=32\), also E4 rather than a fabricated chip. QAC's own hardware-efficiency appendix, by contrast, gives only a qualitative discussion, warning that a pipelined multicore accelerator can stall while a downstream layer waits for an upstream layer to finish a different number of steps; this survey's claim index keeps that discussion below the documented-toolchain-path bar it uses for E3 rather than promoting a feasibility argument to deployment evidence . MT-SNN, in both its withdrawn ICLR manuscript and its later accepted Frontiers abstract, which names a revised alignment mechanism the two records should not be read as documenting identically, contains no compiler, RTL, simulator, or physical run at all, and both versions remain E5 . Manjunath, Ramesh, and Srinivasan's NeuroFlex extends the same PASC-IF equivalence to a column-level granularity rather than a per-layer one; Section 9.9 discusses it as an architectural intervention rather than a temporal one.

9.5 Adaptive Inference

SEENN and Dynamic Confidence intervene against the same finite-window error along a different axis than Section 9.4's static per-layer allocation. Rather than assigning a fixed \(T_l\) to each layer in advance, both vary a single, whole-network terminal horizon \(T_{\mathrm{stop}}(x)\) per input, stopping every layer together once a runtime criterion is satisfied, exactly the distinction Section 4.1 already establishes between per-input early exit and static per-layer allocation. Li, Geller, Kim, and Panda's SEENN offers a confidence-threshold variant, SEENN-I, and a reinforcement-learned policy variant, SEENN-II, built on a QCFS or TET base network, reaching an average of 1.08 timesteps on CIFAR-10 for SEENN-II against full baselines of up to \(T=6\). Li, Jones, and Furber's Dynamic Confidence decodes a confidence signal from the SNN's output over time and applies a Pareto-calibrated threshold on top of a QCFS or QFFS backbone, reducing an average from baselines of \(T=4\) or \(T=14\) to roughly 1.28 to 4.71 timesteps depending on configuration. Both report GPU latency and a spike-count-based energy proxy rather than a chip measurement, and both are E5. Neither paper treats the hardware question as settled: SEENN's latency figure is explicitly measured on an NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPU, and Dynamic Confidence's own text states directly that the real power saving from running on neuromorphic hardware is unknown to its authors . Because every layer in both methods still advances on one shared running step count, a pipelined multi-core accelerator could in principle cut a uniform per-layer \(T\) short once its confidence criterion is met, without the stall failure mode Section 9.4 describes for a genuinely heterogeneous per-layer schedule; no source examined for this section demonstrates that behavior on a physical part.

9.6 Alternative-Code Conversion

The interventions in Sections 9.1 through 9.5 all keep Section 7's rate-matching correspondence and change what feeds it. A separate family instead changes the correspondence itself, replacing the encoder, channel, and decoder Section 4.2 defines with a different code entirely, intervening against Section 8's encoding-and-readout error by changing the code the encoder and decoder use rather than the statistics carried through a fixed rate code. Section 4.3 already fixes what each of these codes represents, how its encoder and decoder work, and what it costs in steps, neurons, or payload width; this section records only where each conversion algorithm intervenes and what evidence supports the result it reports.

Lenz, Orchard, and Sheik's Quartz converts to a time-to-first-spike code using counterweight and rectifier synapses rather than the dynamic-threshold machinery earlier TTFS conversions required, and because each neuron fires at most once, the conversion needs no soft-reset mechanism at all, unlike every rate-coded method in Sections 9.1 through 9.5. Brehove, Tumpa, Kyubwa, Menon, and Narayanan convert a quantized ANN to a Sigma-Delta Neural Network built on Loihi 2's native, up to 24-bit signed graded-spike payload, transmitting activation deltas rather than a spike count. Jiang, Chen, Li, Liu, Gao, Zhang, and Fang's Adaptive Fission applies a population-coding transformation after training, on top of an already converted or directly trained network, trading additional temporal integration for a group of binary sub-neurons carrying unequal fixed weights, which Section 4.3 records as a spatial axis orthogonal to rate and temporal coding rather than a fifth primitive. All three abandon rate coding at the point of conversion itself, rather than adjusting the activation function, the neuron, or the timestep schedule around a rate code, which distinguishes them structurally from every method in Sections 9.1 through 9.5. Section 9.7 records the hardware evidence for all three together, since in every confirmed case in the audited corpus, abandoning rate coding for a platform-native code is also what carries a conversion method to physical silicon.

Rank order coding is the code family for which no conversion theorem exists among the sources reviewed for this survey, for the reason Section 4.3 develops in full: every conversion proof surveyed matches a per-layer magnitude, and an ordinal object drawn from \(N!\) permutations has no natural target for that convergence argument. BrainChip's Akida carries a genuine rank-order-coding lineage at its input-encoding stage, but the standard QuantizeML-to-CNN2SNN route nearly all published Akida results use collapses to a single-pass, order-agnostic threshold comparison inside hidden layers . That converted route is therefore neither a new rate-coded low-timestep conversion algorithm nor an order-sensitive one, and this survey does not count it toward either family's evidence base. MorphIC's same-chip, same-task comparison of rate and rank-order coding, and the resulting tenfold measured energy difference at a 1.9-percentage-point accuracy cost, is developed fully in Section 4.3 and is not repeated here .

9.7 Hardware-Aware Conversion

Through the falsifier search this survey conducted into 16 September 2026, no audited source both introduces a recent, low-timestep, spike-count or firing-rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion algorithm and reports that same proposed algorithm measured on named physical neuromorphic silicon . This is a bounded search result over a stated population of paper-level conversion algorithms; it is not a claim that rate-coded conversion cannot reach silicon, and it says nothing about direct training, classical conversion, or later application papers that reuse an existing converter. Every confirmed instance in the audited corpus of a paper that both proposes a conversion method and reports its own physical-silicon measurement abandons rate coding for a code native to the target platform, which is why this section and Section 9.6 describe the same four methods from two directions.

Quartz's own comparison is head-to-head against a rate-coded NxTF and SNN Toolbox baseline on the same Nahuku 32 Loihi 1 board using NxSDK 1.0.0, reporting 0.77 percent MNIST error against a 0.73 percent ANN baseline, and separately a considerably higher 25.14 percent CIFAR-10 error than the rate-coded NxTF result of 8.52 percent on the same dataset, which the paper itself frames as an energy-accuracy trade rather than an unconditional improvement . Brehove et al. measure their conversion on an in-house 16-chip Loihi 2 VPX board running nxkernel 0.3.0 with modified microcode, reporting chip-probe latency, throughput, power, energy per frame, and energy-delay product against a Jetson Xavier baseline whose whole system-on-chip (SoC) power is not boundary-identical to Loihi's chip-side measurement. Adaptive Fission's population-coding conversion is deployed and measured on a physical Lynxi HP201 accelerator, reporting on-chip memory, per-epoch time, and system energy from board sensors that closed interfaces prevent from being separated into core-only figures . Andrei, Drăguțoiu, Béna, Akl, Li, Lohrmann, Mönich, and Boche extend Few-Spikes conversion to complex-valued layers for an unfolded multidimensional harmonic-retrieval network and map the result to a physical single-chip SpiNNaker2 board, reporting board power against a Jetson Xavier baseline; the underlying representation is a serialized temporal Few-Spikes code, not a spike-count rate estimate. All four reach E1.

APEX and NeuroFlex, discussed for their temporal and architectural contributions in Sections 9.4 and 9.9, synthesize RTL for the PASC-IF neuron rather than fabricating silicon, an E4 hardware model rather than a measured chip in either case . Datta et al.'s asserted Lava-DL-to-Loihi route, by contrast, names no board, generation, or measurement and remains E3, the clearest example in this section of a documented-looking route that stops short of an exercised one. Read across this section, a hardware-aware conversion's evidence class tracks how specifically it names a generation, a toolchain version, and a measurement boundary, not how confidently its introduction or appendix invokes a chip's name.

9.8 Hybrid Fine-Tuning

A third strategy neither reshapes the ANN's training objective, as Section 9.1 does, nor redesigns the neuron, as Sections 9.2 and 9.3 do, but instead corrects a network's activation statistics after conversion, calibrating against Section 8's residual-and-reset error and Section 8's clipping-and-discretization error that an earlier conversion step already introduced, without altering the underlying correspondence theorem. Li, Deng, Dong, Gong, and Gu's calibration method adjusts bias, weight, and initial-membrane-potential statistics on a network already converted at up to 256 steps, explicitly keeping the first layer's input continuous rather than binary because generating binary spikes there degrades accuracy, and computes its energy from Rathi and Roy's published per-operation constants rather than from a measured value. Hao, Ding, Bu, Huang, and Yu's Calibrating Offset Spikes (COS) iteratively detects and corrects the offset between an SNN's spike output and the value its source ANN activation would produce, reaching a headline six-step configuration, four calibration steps plus two inference steps. Wang, Fang, Cao, Ren, and Xu's Adaptive Calibration (AdaFire) introduces a training-free calibration procedure paired with an adaptive-firing burst neuron, reaching a \(T=8\) headline against \(T=32\) competitors at equal energy, with its energy figures again computed from an appendix-stated theoretical method rather than measured. All three report GPU simulation only and are E5; where a chip is named at all in these three papers, it is cited as motivation for a design or calibration choice, never as an executed deployment.

A second, structurally different hybrid pattern fine-tunes across the branch boundary itself rather than statistically. One record in this survey's route index describes a hybrid SNN-ANN network, conventional PyTorch or TensorFlow layers alongside SLAYER- or Lava-trained spiking layers, mapped through NengoLoihi to physical Loihi 1 hardware, with power, core count, and spike-propagation-delay latency measured on-chip . This record is retrieved from structured metadata rather than the paper's full text and carries a provisional verification status in this survey's source registry; its reported E1 measurement should be read with that limitation attached until the underlying paper is independently checked against its full text. Section 9.9 returns to the architecture this method builds, a network that is neither wholly ANN nor wholly SNN.

9.9 Emerging Architectures

Every method in Sections 9.1 through 9.8 keeps one assumption of Section 7's architecture preparation intact: a network is prepared once, as a whole, into a wholly artificial or a wholly spiking form before it runs. Two records in the audited corpus intervene on that partition itself, executing part of a network as an ANN and part as an SNN within a single inference, which targets Section 8's architecture-dependent error at the level of the network's structure rather than at a layer's activation, neuron, or timestep count. Manjunath, Ramesh, and Srinivasan's NeuroFlex extends PASCAL's PASC-IF neuron and its underlying integer-exact equivalence proof from a layer-wide granularity to a column-level one, in which individual output columns within a single layer independently execute in ANN mode or PASC-IF SNN mode, chosen offline by a profiling-driven scheduler tied to each column's own QCFS quantization level. Because the equivalence proof it inherits depends only on local, per-neuron integer arithmetic, the paper argues it survives being applied column by column rather than layer by layer, and reports 98.6 to 99.3 percent processing-element-array utilization on VGG-16, ResNet-34, GoogLeNet, and BERT against 71.0 to 83.9 percent for coarser, layer-wise hybrid scheduling, synthesized in RTL at 560 MHz in a 40-nanometer library rather than fabricated, an E4 result .

The hybrid SNN-ANN network introduced in Section 9.8 makes the same kind of partition at a coarser, layer-wide granularity instead of a column-wide one, and is the one record in this group reporting execution on physical rather than synthesized hardware, subject to the provisional status Section 9.8 already attaches to it . Both records are early enough, and few enough, that this survey states their existence as a design direction rather than as a demonstrated deployment pattern. Section 17 returns to algorithm-hardware co-design as an open research problem, and the column-level and layer-level granularities distinguished here are exactly the design axis that discussion resumes.

10 The Deployable SNN Contract

Section 9 closed by observing that every conversion method it reviewed reports its result against an implicit agreement about which fields of the network a comparison holds fixed, code, precision, reset, time model, and target hardware, and which it silently lets vary. Nothing in Sections 5 through 9 has forced that agreement to be written down. This section writes it down. It fixes eleven fields that a directly trained network and a converted network must both make explicit before either is compared against a target, or against each other, at all. The five terms Section 9 named are not a shorter, competing list. Code and precision name two of the eleven fields directly, reset and time model name two more under fuller names, and target hardware names the object every field is evaluated against rather than a twelfth field of the network itself.

10.1 Why a Shared Contract

Section 5.3 already stated the requirement this section formalizes. Whichever route produces the weights, direct training by the mechanisms of Section 6 or conversion by the derivation of Section 7, the result is a spiking network of the form fixed in Section 3, carrying a code of the form fixed in Section 4. It must be representable in software and executable on a target before a deployment claim about it means anything. What was missing at that point was not a new fact about either branch. It was a single, named list of the semantic commitments both branches make, so that a reader can ask the same question of a paper regardless of which branch produced its network.

The contract answers three separate questions for each of its eleven fields, and this survey keeps the three separate throughout the remainder of the article. Is the field mathematically defined? A neuron equation, a reset rule, or a code can be written down precisely, as Section 3 and Section 4 do, independent of any software or hardware that will later realize it. Can a software framework represent it? A training or simulation framework, an interchange format, or a compiler must accept the mathematical object without silently rewriting its meaning, the question Section 11 answers edge by edge. Can the target execute it without changing what the network computes? A chip, accelerator, or emulator must run the represented object under its own timing, precision, and routing constraints, the question Section 12 and Section 13 answer per platform and per named route. A field can pass one question and fail the next. Section 3.4 already gave the clearest instance available in this survey's audited evidence. Reset by subtraction is mathematically defined without difficulty. It is not representable in the NIR 1.0.8 schema, which encodes only a fixed-value post-spike reset . Where it is executed at all on Intel Loihi 1, it is emulated with a second compartment rather than run on a native single-compartment primitive . The same field is defined, unrepresentable in one interchange format, and emulated on one platform, three different answers for one field.

10.3 Definition, Representation, and Execution

The three questions introduced in Section 10.1 are not stages of one pipeline that a field passes through in order. They are independent tests, and a field can fail any one of them while passing the other two. Restating them against the reset example makes the independence concrete rather than abstract.

Mathematical definition asks whether an equation exists. Section 3.4 writes reset by subtraction as \(u_i^l[t]=\tilde{u}_i^l[t]-z_i^l[t]\,\theta_i^l[t]\), a complete, unambiguous definition that requires no software or hardware to state. Software representation asks whether a framework, an interchange format, or a compiler can accept that definition and hand it downstream unchanged. SpikingJelly 2.0.0rc1's own rate-coding conversion recipe instantiates exactly this reset rule by default, so the framework that produces the network represents the field correctly at the point of production . The same network's own NIR and Lava exporters both reject that default output outright, because neither downstream schema has a field for anything other than a fixed-value reset . The field is representable at the point where the network is defined and unrepresentable one export step later, inside the same toolchain. Faithful execution asks whether a target that does accept some representation of the field runs it as specified. NxTF's Loihi 1 pipeline recovers the mathematical behavior of subtractive reset, but only by allocating a second compartment per affected neuron and using a recurrent inhibition trick to reproduce the subtraction, an emulated rather than a native capability with an explicit resource cost . One field, one clean mathematical definition, three different answers depending on which of the three questions is asked and at which boundary of the pipeline it is asked.

This three-way independence is the reason a single adjective, portable, supported, or compatible, is not an adequate description of any field in this contract. Section 11 develops the representation question into a typed schema applied to every audited software boundary. Section 12 develops the execution question into a comparison across execution families and a capability matrix scoped to specific, versioned platforms. Section 13 then composes both answers across a complete chain of edges, from a training framework to a physical target, and assigns the resulting route a single validated state precisely because no earlier section in this survey has assumed that a route's state can be read off any one boundary in isolation.

10.4 Conformance and the Capability Vocabulary

Evaluating a field against Table 15 requires evidence of three specific kinds, and a field is triangulated only when at least the software and platform kinds can be checked against each other. Algorithm sources are the papers that propose a training method or a conversion algorithm and state, explicitly or by omission, what their own network assumes about each field. Software sources are the documented behavior of a training framework, an interchange format, or a compiler, checked wherever possible against the framework's own source code rather than its prose description alone, exactly as Section 3.4 did for SpikingJelly's exporters. Official platform sources are versioned vendor manuals, SDK and development-kit documentation, mapper and compiler release notes, and official worked examples, the same evidentiary standard this survey's platform-capability records apply throughout Sections 12 and 13.

A field's status against a target is recorded using the capability vocabulary already fixed for this survey, native, transformed, emulated, host-assisted, unsupported, and undocumented . These labels answer a different question than the route-state vocabulary, exact, approximate, blocked, obsolete, unexercised, and physical, which Section 13 assigns to a complete chain of edges rather than to one field. A field can be natively supported on a chip whose overall route to that chip from a specific training framework remains blocked at an earlier edge, exactly the case Section 12 and Section 13 document for Loihi 2's threshold and reset field under the audited SpikingJelly-to-Lava chain. Undocumented is its own distinct status and carries no inference either way. This survey has not located a documented route for custom, non-public Loihi 2 microcode to implement subtractive reset outside the public Lava and NetX toolchains; Loihi 2's neuron engine is documented as fully microcode-programmable, so an undocumented route is a gap in the sources this survey could locate, not a demonstrated impossibility .

Every platform-capability record this survey draws on for Sections 12 and 13 carries a verification status of its own, separate from the capability status it reports. At the time of the evidence audit underlying this survey, every one of the nine platform-capability records assembled for the detailed comparison in Section 12.7 remains provisional, because the canonical vendor manual behind each record is registered in this survey's source registry as reviewed from structured metadata or partial excerpts rather than promoted to a fully inspected, stably located full-text source. A physically exercised route can therefore coexist with a provisional field-level record, when the official technical source documenting that field has not itself been promoted, or when the exercised example uses a different upstream route than the one a specific field claim describes. This survey states that limitation once here rather than qualifying every cell of the tables that follow, and the distinction between a physically exercised route and a fully promoted documentary source remains active throughout Section 12 and Section 13.

The representation question comes first in every remaining section of this survey, because a field that cannot survive a software boundary never reaches a target for the execution question to be asked of it at all. Section 11 takes up that question next, following the software path from a training framework through serialization, an intermediate representation, compilation, and a runtime, and applying the input, output, preserved, transformed, rejected, and evidence schema to every edge the audited evidence supports.

11 Software, Interchange, and Compilation

Section 10.4 established that a contract field's representability must be checked before its execution is asked about at all. This section checks it, following the software path a network travels in the order it is actually traveled, a training or simulation framework, serialization or export, an intermediate representation where one is used, compiler lowering and mapping, a runtime, and the host input and output work that surrounds every stage. Every edge in that path is typed using the schema Table 16 fixes below, input, output, preserved, transformed, rejected, and evidence, so that a claim about one boundary is never confused with a claim about a different one.

11.1 The Framework Boundary

The sixteen training and simulation frameworks this survey's audited evidence covers do not form one category. They divide by what representation they commit to at the point a network is defined and by which hardware family, if any, their own maintainers built them to reach. Grouping by this commitment, rather than listing sixteen names in alphabetical or chronological order, is what lets the rest of this section state which boundary a given framework's output must cross next.

A first group trains a network natively in PyTorch and commits to a spiking representation from the first line of model code. SpikingJelly, snnTorch, and Norse all expose PyTorch-native spiking layers trained by the surrogate-gradient or exact spike-time methods of Section 6, and all three differ in their default reset rule at the point of definition, exactly the divergence Section 3.4 already traces for SpikingJelly. snnTorch's snn.Leaky defaults to a subtractive reset, matching SpikingJelly's own converted-network default, while Norse's LIFCell and LIFRecurrentCell default to reset-to-zero . Neither snnTorch nor Norse has a first-party path to a specific physical target audited in this survey; Norse instead exports to NIR, with its own documentation stating a caveat that a round-trip export and import can produce a different spike pattern on identical input, a framework-level instance of the representation risk this section develops in full . This group's frameworks are therefore evaluated by this survey chiefly as sources of the reset-semantics divergence itself, not as members of an audited hardware route.

A second group descends from biological point-neuron simulation rather than from a PyTorch training loop. Brian2 and NEST are differential-equation and point-neuron simulators whose native application is large-scale biological modeling rather than deep-network conversion; each reaches a converted convolutional network only through SNN Toolbox acting as an external output backend, not through its own native import path . Nengo instead implements the Neural Engineering Framework, analytically solving decoding weights over spiking tuning curves rather than training by backpropagated error, a construction distinct enough from the rate-matching correspondence of Section 7.2 that its NengoDL bridge and ReLU-to-spiking converter should not be read as an instance of the same theorem . PyNN sits above several of these simulators as a shared front end, letting one described model target NEST, BrainScaleS-2, or SpiNNaker without being rewritten per backend, though sPyNNaker, PyNN's own SpiNNaker-specific implementation, is the concrete backend this survey's audited routes actually exercise rather than the generic PyNN interface itself .

A third group is a vendor toolchain built around one hardware family from the outset. Lava and Lava-DL target Loihi 2, Sinabs targets SynSense's DYNAP-CNN and Speck family, Rockpool targets SynSense's Xylo family, QuantizeML targets BrainChip's Akida family, hxtorch targets BrainScaleS-2, and BIDL targets Lynxi's KA200 lineage . Because each of these frameworks is co-designed with its one target, the framework-boundary edge for this group is typically the smallest source of divergence in the entire chain; Section 11.5 shows the larger transformations for this group happen one or two edges downstream, at the mapper or the runtime rather than at model definition. mlGeNN occupies a narrower version of the conversion-specific role SNN Toolbox occupies more broadly, adding explicit deep-CNN conversion support to the GPU code-generation library GeNN and reporting large-scale conversion results at ImageNet scale, but as GPU simulation only, without a documented route to neuromorphic silicon audited in this survey .

SNN Toolbox is the fourth group's sole confirmed member in this survey's audited evidence, a conversion-specific tool whose entire purpose is producing a rate-coded, reset-by-subtraction IF network from a Keras, PyTorch, Caffe, or Lasagne source model and routing that network toward one of several output backends, NxTF, sPyNNaker, Brian2, or Cerebron among them . Section 13.2 traces two of these output paths to physical silicon in full.

11.2 Serialization and the Intermediate Representation

Serialization turns a framework's in-memory graph into a file or object another program can consume. Most vendor toolchains in this survey's audited evidence serialize into a format specific to that vendor's own downstream mapper, Sinabs's internal DynapcnnLayer representation and Lava-DL's HDF5 network-exchange schema being two audited instances . NIR is the one cross-vendor exception this survey's audited evidence covers in depth, an interchange format that multiple frameworks, SpikingJelly, Sinabs, Rockpool, and Norse among them, can both write to and read from, aiming to let one trained network reach several otherwise unrelated backends through a single schema rather than a bespoke pairwise exporter for every framework-to-framework combination .

NIR's own documentation states directly that its primitives are idealized and that the schema does not constrain a backend's integration order, spike timing, reset timing, or numerical quantization, so accepting a NIR graph never by itself establishes that two different backends execute it identically . Section 3.4 already gives the sharpest instance of this idealization becoming a representational limit rather than a merely stylistic one. NIR 1.0.8's IF, LIF, and CubaLIF primitives encode only a fixed-value post-spike reset, and a missing or null reset value is materialized as reset-to-zero, so a schema that has no field for reset by subtraction cannot silently misrepresent it. It rejects it structurally, at the level of what the format is capable of expressing at all . This is a property of NIR 1.0.8 specifically, introduced into the schema in 2025, so a source describing an earlier version of the format should not be read as describing the same reset contract .

11.4 Runtime Scheduling and Host Input and Output

A runtime is the software layer that actually executes a compiled graph on a target, whether that target is physical silicon or a CPU-based simulation standing in for it. NxSDK is Loihi 1's register-level runtime, superseded for Loihi 2 by the Lava runtime's Magma layer, whose Loihi-targeting components remain proprietary and gated to members of Intel's Neuromorphic Research Community . samna is SynSense's cross-chip developer interface, applying a configuration object to physical Speck or DYNAP-CNN silicon and streaming event data through a chain of source, filter, and sink nodes . SpiNNaker's runtime splits across the on-chip SpiN1API operating system and the host-side SpiNNTools pipeline that loads per-core parameter blocks, signals synchronized execution start across every core, and reads results back through the PyNN API after the run completes . In every one of these cases, the runtime that executes the graph and the host process that prepares its input and reads its output are two different pieces of software crossing a boundary that the deployable contract's host-operations field, fixed in Section 10.2, exists to make explicit.

That boundary is not a peripheral detail this survey's audited evidence lets a platform avoid. Every reviewed digital platform still depends on a host-side step that converts an input into the target's native event or packet format before injection and converts output spikes or counters back into a readable result once execution finishes. Rockpool's Xylo mapper illustrates the same boundary from the accelerated-simulation side of a runtime rather than the physical side, deploying through either XyloSamna or XyloMonitor in accelerated-time or real-time streaming mode, with on-chip power recorded by current monitors sampled at 1280 hertz, while parameter quantization, sample-duration configuration, and application-level decoding all remain the host's responsibility rather than the chip's . This is the concrete instance behind the host-operations status recorded uniformly across every platform in Section 12.7's comparison table, and it is also a direct consequence of the boundary Section 2.3 already drew between an event-driven execution model and an end-to-end event-driven system. A platform whose neuron cores execute without a shared clock can still depend on a host that is neither event-driven nor spiking to prepare its input and interpret its output, and no platform audited in this survey removes that dependency entirely.

11.5 What Transforms, What Is Rejected

Reading Table 16 as a whole rather than edge by edge surfaces three patterns that recur across the software stack independent of which vendor built which piece of it.

The first pattern is that reset semantics are transformed or rejected far more often than they are preserved natively. Of the eleven typed edges in Table 16, two reject the source model outright, four transform the reset rule into a different one, one is undocumented, one concerns the sample boundary rather than the reset rule, and only two preserve subtractive reset, NxTF's route to Loihi 1 only through an emulation that doubles compartment allocation, and Sinabs's route to Speck natively. A third preserving row, Lava-DL NetX HDF5 to the Lava LIF process, preserves the opposite case, a hard-zero reset, not a subtractive one. No audited edge in this survey's evidence base preserves subtractive reset through a native, unmodified, single-compartment hardware primitive on a chip whose own default reset behavior is reset-to-zero. Norse's own documented reset-to-zero default and its separately documented round-trip NIR re-import discrepancy extend this same pattern one layer further back, into a framework's export and import of its own graphs rather than only its export toward a different framework .

The second pattern is that numeric precision is quantized at nearly every boundary this survey's audited evidence documents, and the specific bit widths chosen differ from edge to edge rather than converging on one industry default. NxTF's soft-reset workaround preserves 9-bit weight precision on Loihi 1. Sinabs's mapper quantizes to 8-bit weights and 16-bit membrane state for Speck. SpiNNaker2's NIR importer preserves 8-bit weights alongside 32-bit floating-point state. QuantizeML's uniform symmetric integer quantization for Akida commonly uses one, two, four, or eight bits depending on the layer and the configured precision . A precision field stated as bits alone, without naming which of weights, thresholds, or state that count applies to, is therefore an incomplete report against the contract fixed in Section 10.2.

The third pattern is that maintenance status and access conditions are themselves facts about a software boundary, not a footnote outside the contract. Intel archived the entire Lava, Lava-DL, and NetX stack in 2026, stating that all Lava repositories are archived with no successor software development kit yet announced, which freezes every edge in Table 16 that terminates at Loihi 2 through that stack at whatever state it held at the point of archival . Loihi access more generally remains gated to members of Intel's Neuromorphic Research Community rather than openly available . Lynxi's BIDL, Lyngor, and LynSDK toolchain is documented in the sources this survey reviewed as effectively unavailable outside mainland China . A route that is otherwise exact or physical by every other test in this section can still fail a practitioner who cannot obtain the access it requires, a distinction Section 17 returns to as an open problem rather than resolving it here.

12 Hardware Execution Models

Section 11.6 closed by noting that software representation is necessary but not sufficient for a deployment claim. This section takes up the remaining question, whether a target that accepts some representation of a contract field executes it as specified. It organizes hardware by execution model rather than by vendor, because two chips from different manufacturers that both advance a barrier-synchronized global tick share more with each other, for the purposes of the deployable contract, than either shares with a chip from the same lineage built around a fundamentally different timing model. Platform examples throughout this section carry a stated generation, toolchain version, and capability status rather than a bare vendor name, and every capability record drawn from this survey's generated platform-capability data remains provisional in the sense Section 10.4 already fixes, pending full-text promotion of the underlying vendor manual.

12.1 Synchronous Digital Execution

A synchronous digital system advances computation through a global or barrier-based progress signal that every core or region of the chip must reach before the next step begins, regardless of whether the circuitry beneath that signal is itself clocked uniformly. Section 4.1 already established the sharpest instance of this pattern for Intel's Loihi family. Loihi 1 and Loihi 2 compute and communicate asynchronously within a core, yet both advance a single, global algorithmic timestep across the whole chip through a barrier synchronization handshake, and IBM's TrueNorth and Tsinghua's Tianjic each advance time through their own chip-wide synchronization signal in the same structural sense . TrueNorth evaluates its entire network once per one-kilohertz global tick, encoding activation magnitude spatially across redundant, trinary-weighted neuron copies within that single tick rather than through multi-timestep temporal accumulation, a code-and-timing pairing distinct from the rate-coded correspondence Section 7 derives . SynSense's Xylo family sits in the same family from the opposite direction, a genuinely clocked, synchronous digital application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC) running an internal clock in the tens of megahertz with a fixed recurrent CuBa-LIF core and a global timestep duration configured by the host .

The mechanism this family shares is a chip-wide agreement on step boundaries, whatever happens inside one step. The semantic effect is that every layer of a mapped network advances at the same rate as every other layer within one inference, which is exactly the assumption the static per-layer horizons of Section 9.4 would need a barrier-synchronized target to relax, a demonstration Section 4.1 already states no audited source in this survey provides on physical synchronous hardware. The trade-off is that a barrier's own wall-clock duration is not fixed by the model and stretches with the slowest core's spike traffic in a given step, so a reported timestep count on this family of hardware is never automatically a fixed physical duration . The deployment consequence, developed field by field in Section 12.7, is that Loihi's own neuron dynamics field is native while its threshold and reset field on the audited route to Loihi 1 is only recoverable through the two-compartment emulation Section 11 already traces in full, a divergence that a single verdict about Loihi's synchronous execution model would not reveal.

12.2 Asynchronous Digital Execution

An asynchronous event-driven digital pipeline has no chip-wide barrier at all. A core or a processing stage advances only when an event demands it, and the chip has no shared clock signal for a barrier to be built on top of. SynSense's Speck and its predecessor core DYNAP-CNN are the confirmed instances in this survey's audited evidence, a fully asynchronous, clockless system-on-chip integrating a dynamic vision sensor with nine fixed convolution, integrate-and-fire, and pooling cores, reporting a single event crossing all nine layers in under four microseconds regardless of how many timesteps a software model believed it was simulating . This is Section 4.1's fourth mechanism, timestep-free event-driven execution, and it is the one mechanism in that discussion with a confirmed physical result behind it, Temporal Flexibility's deployment of a Mixed Time-step Training network to a physical Speck2e device .

The mechanism is that state updates occur strictly on event arrival, with no vector of per-layer discrete horizons for a scheduler to execute at all. The semantic effect is that a reported timestep count for a network destined for this family of hardware describes only the training-time simulation, not a quantity the chip itself schedules; Section 4.1 already states this distinction and this section does not relax it. The trade-off is that removing the barrier removes with it any single, chip-wide notion of when one inference ends and the next begins, which is why Section 11.4 records sample-duration configuration and output aggregation as host responsibilities for this family rather than native chip behavior. The deployment consequence, confirmed directly in Section 12.7's comparison table, is that Speck's time-and-termination field is recorded as host-assisted rather than native, the one point in that table where the asynchronous family's own defining property, no chip-wide tick, produces a different status than the barrier-synchronized family's native time-and-termination field, even though both families ultimately depend on a host to declare when a result should be read.

12.3 Software-Programmable Many-Core Systems

A software-programmable many-core system executes a user-supplied program on each of many general-purpose or near-general-purpose cores rather than realizing one fixed neuron circuit in hardware. SpiNNaker 1 and SpiNNaker 2 are this survey's confirmed instances, an ARM-core-based digital many-core architecture that simulates arbitrary software-defined neuron models rather than one built-in dynamical primitive, at a default software convention near one millisecond per tick that Section 3.5 already identifies as a scheduling convention rather than a hardware limit . This is also the family in which Table 2's CUBA-LIF row places sPyNNaker's own documented default cell type, IF_curr_exp, a current-based synapse paired with an exponentially decaying current rather than the instantaneous sum IF and LIF assume .

The mechanism is that neuron and synapse behavior is compiled C code running on a general-purpose core rather than a fixed circuit, so this family's capability status for a given contract field tracks what a user is willing to implement rather than what the silicon fixes in place. The semantic effect is direct for the reset-and-threshold field. SpiNNaker's built-in cell types use PyNN-conventional hard-reset semantics by default, and reset by subtraction is achievable only by writing a bespoke C neuron model with a custom spike-detection callback, not by selecting a built-in option, which is why Section 11 records the SNN Toolbox to sPyNNaker route's threshold-and-reset field as unsupported for the audited default configuration even though the route as a whole reaches physical silicon . The trade-off is a place-and-route resource budget rather than a silicon-area one; SpiNNTools compresses multicast routing tables toward a roughly 1024-entry-per-chip router budget and partitions the application graph at close to 256 atoms per core given per-core memory limits, a mapping constraint distinct from anything a barrier-synchronized or fully asynchronous chip needs to satisfy . The deployment consequence is that this family's programmability makes an absolute claim about what it cannot do particularly risky. A capability recorded as unsupported for this family describes the audited default configuration and toolchain, not a hardware ceiling a custom kernel could not in principle raise.

12.4 Analog and Mixed-Signal Execution

An analog or mixed-signal system realizes neuron and synapse dynamics as physical circuit behavior evolving in continuous time, typically at a fixed acceleration factor relative to biological or wall-clock time, rather than as a discrete-time numerical recurrence. Heidelberg's BrainScaleS-1 and BrainScaleS-2 are the confirmed instances developed in depth in this survey's audited evidence, wafer-scale and single-chip analog systems physically emulating adaptive-exponential or leaky-integrate-and-fire circuits at roughly one thousand to ten thousand times accelerated analog time, requiring per-neuron calibration to correct manufacturing mismatch before a mapped network reaches usable accuracy . SynSense's DYNAP-SE2 and Innatera's Pulsar extend the same execution family to smaller, commercially oriented mixed-signal parts; this survey's audited evidence located no rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion pipeline for DYNAP-SE2, and every quantitative performance figure it located for Innatera Pulsar is vendor-sourced without an independent peer-reviewed benchmark, a gap this survey states rather than resolves .

The mechanism is that threshold crossing and reset are properties of a physical neuron circuit rather than a digital comparison against a stored number, so the threshold-and-reset field is native by construction on hardware in this family, exactly the status Section 12.7's comparison table records for BrainScaleS-2. The semantic effect is that numeric precision is not a bit width chosen by a compiler but a resolution set by circuit mismatch and calibration quality, which is why the same table records BrainScaleS-2's precision field as transformed rather than native, a mismatch-and-calibration process standing in for what a digital platform would call quantization. A caution belongs here, not deferred to a later section, because the audited physical route for hxtorch, the PyTorch interface to BrainScaleS-2, targets its non-spiking hagen mode, executing analog matrix multiplication directly rather than the spiking, leaky-integrate-and-fire hxtorch.snn mode . This survey's own source notes this distinction explicitly, and the physical latency and energy measurement Stradmann et al. report for a mobile electrocardiogram application must not be read as a measurement of the spiking execution of hxtorch.snn . The separate hardware-in-the-loop evidence for BrainScaleS-2's own spiking mode, hxtorch.snn's injection of measured membrane traces into automatic differentiation for the Yin-Yang classification task, is Section 6.3's E2 result, a genuinely spiking, genuinely physical claim, but a distinct one from the non-spiking route this section's platform-capability comparison audits . The trade-off for the whole family is that accelerated time is a design feature for some workloads and a representational obstacle for others; a network trained assuming discrete algorithmic steps must be re-expressed against continuous circuit dynamics before either the timing or the precision fields can be evaluated as this survey's contract requires.

12.5 FPGA and Custom-ASIC Implementations

An FPGA or custom-ASIC implementation realizes a fixed or reconfigurable digital circuit purpose-built for one execution model rather than adapting a general-purpose many-core architecture or a continuous analog substrate to the task. BrainChip's Akida family is this survey's most thoroughly audited instance, a commercial neuromorphic system-on-chip whose standard QuantizeML-to-CNN2SNN deployment route, developed in full in Section 9.6 and traced again as a named route in Section 13.2, collapses the multi-step firing-rate window the classical correspondence assumes into a single feedforward pass over quantized, integer-only activations, folding batch normalization and activation quantization into a per-neuron static threshold computed once at conversion time . UCLouvain's MorphIC is the clearest controlled same-silicon comparison available in this survey's audited evidence between two neural codes, reporting rate coding reaching 97.8 percent accuracy at 205 microjoules per classification against rank order coding reaching 95.9 percent accuracy at 21.8 microjoules per classification on the identical MNIST task and chip, a result Section 4.3 already develops in full . A broader FPGA family, including Cerebron, SyncNN, and other accelerators surveyed for their code-fidelity properties in earlier sections, implements the classical IF-plus-reset-by-subtraction-plus-rate-coding pipeline with varying fidelity per design; SyncNN and a subset of these designs expose reset by subtraction as an explicit, user-selectable configuration, while others do not document their reset polarity at all .

The mechanism this family shares is that the execution model is fixed into the circuit at design time rather than negotiated by a general-purpose core's software or a continuous physical process. The semantic effect is that a custom ASIC or a synthesized FPGA design can realize almost any of the neuron models Section 3.2 derives natively, at the cost of committing that choice before fabrication or synthesis rather than after. The trade-off is exactly the one Section 9.6 and Section 9.7 already establish for Akida specifically: every confirmed instance in this survey's audited corpus of a conversion method reaching physical silicon through this family abandons the classical rate-coded correspondence for a code native to the target rather than adapting the target to carry an unmodified rate code. The deployment consequence is that a capability status reported for this family is a statement about one fabricated or synthesized design, not about what a different FPGA bitstream or a different ASIC tapeout targeting the same neuron family could in principle achieve; Section 9.4's APEX and NeuroFlex accelerator models, synthesized in RTL rather than fabricated, are E4 hardware-model evidence for exactly this reason, a documented design rather than a measured chip.

12.6 Conventional Processors and Emerging Devices

A conventional processor executing an SNN workload is a general-purpose CPU or GPU running a simulator rather than any dedicated spiking substrate, and it is, by a wide margin, the execution model behind the majority of the evidence this survey classifies as E5 throughout Sections 7 through 9. mlGeNN's own GPU-only deep-CNN conversion results, reaching close to matched accuracy between a converted VGG-16 and its ANN baseline on ImageNet, are a direct instance, useful evidence about the classical correspondence's numerical behavior at scale and not evidence about any neuromorphic target at all . BIDL's multi-backend design, compiling through Lyngor to either the Lynxi KA200 many-core, compute-in-memory chip or a conventional CPU or GPU fallback, makes the same processor a documented option inside one vendor's own toolchain rather than only an implicit default outside every dedicated toolchain .

IBM's NorthPole belongs in this family for a reason worth stating precisely rather than assuming from its lineage. NorthPole is IBM's own described successor to TrueNorth, but it is a purely digital, low-precision matrix-multiply accelerator with no spiking or event-driven neuron model and no membrane-potential dynamics; its reported efficiency derives from memory locality and low-bit multiply-accumulate execution, not from event-driven temporal sparsity of the kind this survey's deployable contract is built to describe . NorthPole is therefore outside the eleven-field contract's intended scope in the same sense a conventional accelerator is, and naming it a neuromorphic chip on the strength of its lineage alone would be exactly the kind of vendor-name-driven overclaim Section 2.3 already warns against.

In-memory and emerging-device systems are covered here only to deployment-relevant depth, consistent with the referral Section 2.5 already gives to the underlying device physics. Lynxi's KA200, a compute-in-memory, many-core, heterogeneous-fusion chip built on the Tianjic lineage and natively supporting both deep-learning ANNs and spiking networks with mixed-precision compute, is this survey's clearest confirmed instance, and its own access limitation, a deployment path effectively unavailable outside mainland China per the sources reviewed, is itself a deployment-relevant fact rather than a detail this survey sets aside . The present discussion stops after this deployment-relevant depth. For the underlying device-level physics of brain-inspired and analog or emerging-device neuromorphic substrates, see Zolfagharinejad et al. , already referred to at the same boundary in Section 2.5.

13 Complete Deployment Routes

Section 12.7 closed by distinguishing what a platform can do in principle from what a specific published chain from training framework to silicon has actually done. This section closes that gap for seven named candidate routes, chosen because each pairs a training or conversion framework already introduced in Section 11 with a hardware family already introduced in Section 12, and because each is a route a reader of this literature is likely to assume exists in something like its named form. Each route is traced edge by edge against this survey's generated route index, and a route absent from that index, however plausible its two named endpoints look together, is not treated as a route at all.

13.1 What Counts as a Named Route

A named route in this section is a chain of two or more edges, each typed by the schema Section 11.3 already applies, connecting a training or conversion framework to a physical or intended hardware target. A route's overall state is not asserted independently of its edges. It is read off the platform-capability record that names the specific chain of route identifiers behind it, exactly as Section 10.4 already distinguishes a field's capability status from a route's state. Six states are available for this purpose, exact, approximate, blocked, obsolete, unexercised, and physical, fixed once in this survey's evidence vocabulary and applied here without redefinition. A route can fail at its first edge, in which case every downstream edge is conditional on a hypothetical model that never reaches it, or it can succeed through every edge to a documented physical measurement. Both outcomes are reported in what follows with the same care, because a blocked route is exactly as informative to a practitioner choosing a deployment path as a physical one.

One naming caution applies before any route is traced. The plainest name for a candidate route is not always the exact chain the generated route index records. Where this survey's evidence traces a different, more specific intermediate node than the name a reader might expect, this section states the discrepancy explicitly rather than silently substituting the expected name for the audited one.

13.2 Physical Routes

Four of the seven candidate routes reach a physical target under this survey's audited evidence, and a fifth reaches physical hardware for a different execution mode than the one its name suggests.

SNN Toolbox → NxTF → Loihi 1. SNN Toolbox's Keras, PyTorch, Caffe, and Lasagne-ingested, percentile-normalized, reset-by-subtraction IF conversion bridges into NxTF's Keras-derived compiler through a dedicated interface module, and NxTF compiles the result down through NxSDK's register-level NxCore API onto physical Loihi 1 silicon . Every edge in this chain is E1. The result is, in this survey's audited evidence, the clearest published rate-coded conversion result on physical neuromorphic silicon, an 8.52 percent CIFAR-10 error on a 413,000-neuron, 3-million-parameter network spanning 1,753 cores across 14 chips . The route's threshold-and-reset field is emulated rather than native, recovered only through the two-compartment workaround Section 11.3 already traces, and NxTF's own authors list a native soft-reset primitive as a desired future hardware feature specifically to remove that cost . This chain is also a closed one. NxTF and NxSDK were never ported to Loihi 2, and Intel's own 2022 Neuromorphic Research Community guidance redirects members to Lava for that generation instead . This survey's platform-capability record accordingly assigns the route state physical to this chain specifically for Loihi 1.

SNN Toolbox → sPyNNaker → SpiNNaker 1. The audited chain is more specific than a name built around the generic PyNN interface would suggest. SNN Toolbox's own SpiNNaker output module builds PyNN-based convolutional and dense layers from a parsed model and drives sPyNNaker, PyNN's SpiNNaker-specific backend, directly, rather than passing through the generic, backend-agnostic PyNN interface as an intermediate stage in its own right . sPyNNaker's own SpiNNTools stage then partitions the graph, places it, compresses routing tables to the chip's router budget, and signals synchronized execution start across physical SpiNNaker 1 silicon . An earlier, independent deep-belief-network conversion on a single physical SpiNNaker 1 chip reports 95.01 percent MNIST accuracy at 0.3 watts and roughly 20 milliseconds latency, all measured on silicon, and a later result credits SNN Toolbox's own conversion methodology at 98.20 percent MNIST accuracy on a larger SpiNNaker-103 machine, though the exact code path is not confirmed to be this specific module in every source consulted . As Section 12.3 already states, this route's threshold-and-reset field is unsupported for the audited default configuration, because sPyNNaker's built-in cell types use PyNN-conventional hard-reset semantics and reset by subtraction requires a bespoke C neuron model outside the audited chain. This survey's evidence also locates no CIFAR-10-scale or larger convolutional network run on physical SpiNNaker silicon through any documented path; every physical result located is digit-recognition scale or a small number of convolutional layers, a scale limit distinct from the route's own state.

Sinabs → DynapcnnNetwork → samna → Speck. Sinabs's from_torch.from_model call replaces ReLU activations with subtractive-reset integrate-and-fire layers by default, and its DynapcnnNetwork mapper restructures the result into convolution, spike, and pooling blocks, quantizing weights to 8 bits and membrane state to 16 bits . samna then applies the resulting configuration object to physical Speck silicon and streams event data in real time . Multiple physical results confirm this chain, a dynamic-SNN DVS-Gesture result reporting 81.0 percent accuracy at 9.5 milliwatts for a fixed-rate configuration against 90.0 percent accuracy at 3.8 milliwatts for a dynamic configuration on the same chip, and an official NIR-to-Speck tutorial reporting 90.00 percent on-chip accuracy on N-MNIST . Unlike the two routes above, this chain's threshold-and-reset field is native rather than emulated, because Speck's own hardware default, return_to_zero=False, already implements subtraction and Sinabs's own default reset function matches it without a workaround . The route still transforms other fields on the way, average pooling becomes sum pooling because spikes cannot be meaningfully averaged, a linear layer becomes a 1x1 convolution, and Speck imposes one uniform threshold per spiking layer rather than a per-neuron threshold, so native status for one field does not extend to the graph-operators or precision fields on the same chain.

Rockpool → Xylo. A trained Rockpool computational graph passes to a mapper that extracts a hardware specification and checks it against Xylo's fixed CuBa-LIF architecture through a design-rule check, and a quantization stage then fixes weights to 8 bits and thresholds to 16 bits before XyloSamna or XyloMonitor deploys the result to physical Xylo Audio 2 silicon . A published micro-power keyword-spotting benchmark on the physical XyloAudio 2 development kit, with on-board current monitors sampled at 1280 hertz, is this chain's E1 result . Subtractive reset is preserved as a fixed hardware property on this route rather than a software switch, matching the native threshold-and-reset status Section 12.7's comparison table already records for Xylo. The route also departs from the single-spike-per-timestep assumption most conversion theory relies on, because Xylo's core supports up to 31 events per neuron per step, a capability this survey's audited evidence does not find exploited by the specific keyword-spotting benchmark cited here.

hxtorch → BrainScaleS-2, a physical route to a different target than its name suggests. This chain is not one of the seven originally listed candidate routes, but it belongs in this subsection as a caution against a mistake this survey's own audited evidence makes concrete rather than hypothetical. hxtorch's perceptron primitives execute non-spiking analog matrix multiplication on BrainScaleS-2 in its hagen mode, and a physical measurement exists, latency and energy for a mobile electrocardiogram application measured on the physical system . This measurement applies to the non-spiking analog execution mode alone. It must not be read as a measurement of hxtorch.snn's spiking, leaky-integrate-and-fire execution mode, a distinct route with its own separate, E2, hardware-in-the-loop evidence already developed in Section 6.3 and Section 12.4 . A route can be physical and still not be the route a name suggests it is.

13.3 Approximate and Transformed Routes

QuantizeML → CNN2SNN → Akida. QuantizeML's uniform, symmetric integer quantization output is consumed directly by CNN2SNN's convert() call, which maps the result to Akida's .fbz runtime format, folding batch normalization and activation quantization into a per-neuron static threshold computed once at conversion time . This is a documented, first-party BrainChip pipeline order, and the Akida runtime executes the resulting model on physical AKD1000, AKD1500, or Akida Pico silicon, an E1 chain for the Akida 1.0 generation . This survey nonetheless places the route in this subsection rather than treating it as an unqualified physical instance of rate-coded conversion, because the same evidence that establishes it as physical also establishes that it transforms away essentially every primitive the rate-coded correspondence of Section 7 assumes. There is no user-exposed multi-timestep simulation horizon; the mapped network collapses to a single feedforward pass. No membrane potential carries across timesteps, and there is no reset by subtraction, an iterative subtract-and-carry mechanism, only a static threshold computed once. Rate coding of an activation as a spike count over a horizon \(T\) is explicitly replaced by a step-wise quantized ReLU evaluated in one pass, a collapse two independent physical-hardware studies confirm, Lunghi et al. and Ziegler et al. . A reading of the toolkit's own source code, which this survey records but has not independently verified, supports the same conclusion . The route is scoped to BrainChip's standard converted-CNN execution and does not establish the semantics of every sequential, recurrent, or otherwise undocumented Akida mode. For the Akida 2.0 generation specifically, this survey's platform-capability record states the route state as unexercised rather than physical, because the same software chain establishes a generic Akida target without demonstrating a measured run on Akida 2.0 FPGA or production silicon specifically . Reaching physical silicon and preserving the contract fields a reader might assume a rate-coded conversion route preserves are, in this one chain, two separate and partially conflicting claims.

13.4 Blocked and Unexercised Routes

Two of the seven originally listed candidate routes are blocked at their first edge, and neither reaches the hardware target its name promises.

SpikingJelly → Lava → Loihi 2. SpikingJelly's own rate-coding ANN-to-SNN conversion recipe instantiates subtractive-reset IF neurons with v_reset=None by default, exactly as Section 3.4 already establishes, and its lava_exchange module raises an explicit error at five separate call sites whenever v_reset is not exactly zero . This is a blocked route at the first edge for the default, published conversion output, not an unexercised or merely undocumented one; the exporter fails with a stated, deliberate error rather than silently miscompiling or simply lacking an example. A separately configured hard-zero model can enter the restricted downstream chain, lava_exchange to NetX's HDF5 schema to the Lava runtime, but that is a different model than the one the classical conversion recipe this survey has developed throughout Sections 7 through 9 actually produces, and no official example exercising that alternative, hard-zero-configured chain end to end to physical Loihi 2 was located in this survey's audited evidence . The downstream edges this alternative chain would use, lava_exchange to NetX and NetX to Loihi 2, are independently recorded as E3, documented but unexercised for this specific source contract, and the entire Lava, Lava-DL, and NetX stack was archived by Intel during 2026 with no successor toolchain yet announced, freezing whatever state each downstream edge held at the point of archival .

SpikingJelly → NIR → available backends. The same default reset configuration blocks this second candidate route at the same first edge for a related but distinct reason. SpikingJelly's nir_exchange module traces the model graph but raises NotImplementedError whenever v_reset is not set, because NIR 1.0.8's own IF, LIF, and CubaLIF primitives specify only a fixed-value reset and have no field capable of representing reset by subtraction at all . This is, in the terms Section 11.2 already uses, the format correctly rejecting a semantics it cannot express rather than a bug in SpikingJelly's own exporter. Once this first edge is blocked, the phrase available backends in the candidate route's name cannot be evaluated for SpikingJelly's own converted output, because there is no NIR graph for those backends to receive. NIR's fan-out to other frameworks remains real for other sources that do produce a representable graph, Sinabs to NIR is approximate and E2-evidenced through a physical Speck tutorial, and NIR to an official Lava or Lava-DL example is approximate through a documented but narrower zero-reset transformation, but neither of these is the SpikingJelly-authored chain the candidate route names, and NIR's own onward route to SpiNNaker 2 is independently blocked for the identical reset reason, at the identical first edge, before py-spinnaker2's importer is ever reached .

A further, more specific route belongs in this subsection precisely because its absence is evidentially informative rather than an oversight. This survey located no documented route, inside the public Lava or NetX toolchains or outside them, for implementing subtractive reset through custom Loihi 2 microcode. Loihi 2's neuron engine is documented as fully microcode-programmable, which makes impossibility an unwarranted inference from this absence . This survey accordingly records the edge as undocumented, a distinct route state from blocked, obsolete, or unexercised, and one that leaves the question open for a future audit rather than closing it in either direction.

13.5 Choosing a Route

The seven routes traced in this section do not rank neatly from best to worst, because the field each one preserves, transforms, or fails to reach at all is not the same field from route to route. A practitioner whose deployment claim depends specifically on reset by subtraction executing without a resource-doubling workaround has, in this survey's audited evidence, exactly three physical options, native subtraction on Speck through Sinabs, native subtraction on Xylo through Rockpool, or emulated subtraction on Loihi 1 through NxTF at the cost of a second compartment per neuron; SpiNNaker's two generations and the QuantizeML-to-Akida chain fail this specific requirement even though three of those four chains are otherwise physical. A practitioner whose claim depends only on reaching some physical target with a rate-coded, multi-step, feedforward convolutional network, without a further requirement on reset semantics, has a wider physical option set, but should read the QuantizeML-to-Akida route's own transformation of the timestep and reset fields as a warning against assuming that physical execution certifies the specific correspondence Section 7 derives. A practitioner drawn to SpikingJelly for its PyTorch-native training convenience should read this section's two blocked routes as a concrete cost of that convenience at the export boundary, not a permanent one, since a hard-zero-configured model can enter the restricted Lava-DL chain that a subtractive-reset model cannot, but a cost that changes what the exported network computes relative to the one actually trained.

14 Deployment Measurement

Sections 8 and 9 already flagged the gap this section closes. A finite-time error term or an intervention against it is a mechanism or inference claim until it is tied to a number obtained from a stated experiment, and Section 4 (Section 4.5) fixed latency and energy as quantities that require a stated boundary without yet naming what the candidate boundaries are. This section names them, states what a comparable report of accuracy, time, power, energy, memory, and communication must contain, and explains why a Pareto claim across several of these axes is only as trustworthy as its weakest axis. Section 15 applies this vocabulary case by case; this section defines it once.

14.1 Deployment Metrics

A deployment claim mixes several quantities that this survey keeps separate rather than folding into one headline number. Accuracy or task quality states what was measured, on which dataset split, with which preprocessing, and whether the reported figure came from software evaluation or from the deployed device itself. Decision time \(\tau_{\mathrm{decision}}\) is the time until a valid output becomes available, and it can fall short of a declared maximum horizon \(T\) when a stopping rule intervenes, the distinction Section 4.1 already fixes for \(T_{\mathrm{stop}}(x)\). Wall-clock latency \(\tau_{\mathrm{inf}}\) is a duration between a stated start event and a stated stop event, and, as Table 4 already warns, a lower step count is not evidence of a lower \(\tau_{\mathrm{inf}}\) without that pair of events, a measurement boundary, and a batch size all stated together. Throughput \(R\) reports decisions per unit time at a stated batch size, and a peak-throughput figure at a large batch answers a different question than an interactive batch-one latency. Static and dynamic power, \(P_{\mathrm{static}}\) and \(P_{\mathrm{dyn}}\), require a stated subtraction method and boundary before they can be compared. Energy per inference \(E_{\mathrm{inf}}\) is either measured directly or computed from measured power and latency, and the product \(E_{\mathrm{inf}} \cdot \tau_{\mathrm{inf}}\), the energy-delay product (EDP), is a single combined figure of merit only when both factors share the same boundary. Memory, on-chip weight storage, and routing or spike-packet communication cost round out the set, and, as Table 19 records, they are the fields most often left unstated in the audited evidence. Finally, a proxy metric, a spike count \(N_{\mathrm{spk}}\) or a synaptic-operation count \(N_{\mathrm{synop}}\), estimates one of the quantities above under an assumed per-operation energy constant rather than measuring it, and this survey treats a proxy as informative only when the source states the constant, the population it is applied to, and that the result is an estimate rather than a measurement.

14.2 Measurement Boundaries

A metric from Table 19 is only comparable to another instance of the same metric when both share a measurement boundary. This survey fixes six. A chip-only boundary includes only the neurocore or die itself, excluding host input and output traffic, off-chip memory access, and any board-level peripheral. A board-level boundary adds the chip's own board, its power supply, memory, and I/O controllers, while still excluding a driving host machine. A host-inclusive boundary adds that driving host's own encode, decode, or streaming overhead on top of the board. A sensor-to-decision boundary spans the full path from physical sensor acquisition to a decision or actuation signal, with no host-side frame accumulation inserted along the way. A modeled boundary is a register-transfer-level (RTL) synthesis, a cycle-accurate simulation, or an analytical or SynOps-style estimate, with no fabricated silicon executed at all. A physical boundary, finally, is a fabricated device measured directly and reported without further decomposition into the finer boundaries above, typically a bare die or packaged part on a bench rather than a system with its own board and host layers. 14.3 Baseline Matching and Batching

A ratio against a baseline is only informative when the baseline shares the workload, precision, and, where relevant, the boundary of the result it is compared against. Kelber et al.'s own benchmarking table illustrates the failure mode directly. Within one comparison, SpiNNaker's and Spikey's energy figures are measured with real power meters, a physical boundary, while the BrainScaleS figure for the same comparison is estimated from previously published per-event data, a modeled boundary, so a reader who treats the table's three numbers as three measurements of the same kind is instead comparing two physical results against one modeled estimate. Ortone et al.'s tactile localization system reports the reverse pairing deliberately, contrasting its physical DYNAP-SE measurement against an explicitly estimated milliwatt-class conventional implementation of the same dynamics, stating the comparator's own boundary rather than presenting it as an equivalent measurement. Batch size carries the same requirement and is more often left unstated. Adaptive Fission's board-level throughput and energy figures name batch size 32 explicitly, one of the few records in the audited evidence to do so; most of the other cases surveyed in Section 15 report a single-sample or unspecified batching condition, which leaves the usual throughput-versus-latency trade unresolved for a reader trying to compare across them. A force-control policy's own comparison against a named CPU and GPU baseline states the comparator platforms explicitly without stating their numeric precision, a documentation gap this survey records rather than fills in.

14.4 Event Rate and Static Power

Dynamic power \(P_{\mathrm{dyn}}\) scales with the rate of state-changing events a substrate processes within its declared boundary, while static power \(P_{\mathrm{static}}\) is drawn continuously whenever the device is powered, independent of that rate. The tactile localization system above reports both terms directly on physical, mixed-signal hardware, a resting power an order of magnitude below its median inference power, the two figures separated rather than folded into one number. Yao et al.'s event-driven dynamic-computing chip ties the same distinction to an explicit intervention, reporting real-time power reduced through input masking that lowers the incoming event rate itself, a measured link between event rate and power on physical silicon rather than an assumed proportionality. Because a synchronous digital substrate keeps its static term running for the full duration it is powered and mapped, a schedule that shortens the algorithmic horizon \(T\) reduces only the dynamic contribution to \(E_{\mathrm{inf}}\), and the size of that reduction depends on what fraction of the reported energy was dynamic in the first place, a fraction this survey cannot state as a fixed proportion because none of the records above disclose it as one.

14.5 Uncertainty and Pareto Analysis

A single reported number carries no information about how much it would vary on a second run, a second die, or a second chip of the same design. Du et al.'s Temporal Flexibility reports two such figures for the identical physical deployment, a simulator-to-chip spike-pattern difference and a separate repeated-chip difference, the second obtained by comparing the same trained weights across more than one physical unit. ODIN's and ReckOn's own results are drawn from nine and five fabricated chips respectively, a die-to-die replication rather than a single-sample measurement, though neither source states the resulting spread as an explicit uncertainty figure. A Pareto claim, a reported frontier across two or more of the axes in Table 19 rather than one operating point, inherits the measurement boundary of its weakest axis. SEENN's and Dynamic Confidence's own accuracy-versus-average-timestep frontiers, already placed at E5 in Section 9.5, are constructed entirely in software simulation with a spike-count energy proxy standing in for the energy axis, and Dynamic Confidence's own text states that the real power saving on neuromorphic hardware is unknown to its authors. An accuracy-timestep curve built this way is not an accuracy-energy-latency Pareto surface measured on hardware, and this survey does not read it as one.

14.6 Measurement Checklist

The fields above recur often enough across Section 15's cases that a short checklist is more useful here than repeating the argument once per case. A comparable deployment report states each of the following, and Section 15 records where a given source leaves one unstated rather than treating the omission as a hidden zero.

  • The measurement boundary from Table 20, and, where a source reports more than one condition, which condition each number belongs to.
  • The baseline's workload, precision, and boundary, stated rather than implied by proximity in a table.
  • Batch size, or an explicit statement that the reported figure is a single-sample result.
  • Whether static and dynamic power are separated, and the subtraction method if so.
  • Whether energy or operation counts are measured or are a stated proxy, and, if a proxy, the assumed constant and the population it scales.
  • Any replication across chips, dies, or runs, and the resulting spread, where available.
  • For a frontier or Pareto claim, the boundary of every axis plotted, not only the axis the paper foregrounds.

15.1 Vision Workloads

Section 8 already noted a gap this subsection returns to. None of the finite-time-error mechanisms and none of the low-latency conversion interventions surveyed in Sections 8 and 9 report execution on physical neuromorphic silicon in their own papers; every case in this subsection that reaches silicon under a rate code does so through a separate, later engineering paper applying a documented toolchain to a classical or QCFS-lineage correspondence, not through the algorithm paper that first proposed the correspondence. The NxTF compiler of Rueckauer et al. illustrates the pattern from two directions within one paper. It carries a four-layer convolutional network trained conventionally and converted through SNN Toolbox's rate-coded, reset-by-subtraction pipeline to physical Loihi 1 for MNIST and, separately, an off-the-shelf MobileNet converted the same way for CIFAR-10, and it also carries a SLAYER-trained convolutional network for N-MNIST and DVS Gestures on the identical physical chip, a direct-training result rather than a converted one. Massa et al. deploy a gesture-recognition network to physical Loihi 1 through the same SNN Toolbox and NxSDK route, but their pipeline first accumulates DVS events into frames before conversion, so the reported accuracy and per-frame latency answer a question about a frame-accumulated input, not about native event input, a distinction Section 14.1's first row already flags. Patiño-Saucedo et al. carry the same two-route pattern onto SpiNNaker, converting a LeNet-style network through SNN Toolbox for MNIST and separately training a network directly by spatio-temporal backpropagation (STBP) for N-MNIST on the same 48-chip SpiNNaker-103 machine, predating the QCFS lineage by construction. Chen, Gao, and Fu's Cerebron, Panchapakesan, Fang, and Li's SyncNN, and Stromatias et al.'s spiking deep belief network extend the same rate-coded, converted-network pattern to physical field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) and to SpiNNaker respectively, each reporting accuracy alongside latency, throughput, or power measured on real boards rather than synthesis estimates. Li et al.'s FireFly v2 accelerator reports the highest clock frequency among the FPGA accelerators surveyed here, though its own record does not disclose whether the evaluated networks were converted or directly trained. Read together, this cluster is the clearest physical evidence in the audited corpus that a rate-coded, IF-neuron convolutional network, trained by ordinary means and converted through a documented toolchain, reaches physical silicon, and every instance of it that does so is an engineering paper built on top of an already-published correspondence rather than the correspondence's own proposing paper.

A second cluster abandons the rate code at the point of conversion itself, already established as a code-family and intervention-family case in Sections 4 and 9. Lenz, Orchard, and Sheik's Quartz converts to a time-to-first-spike code and reaches physical Loihi 1 for MNIST and CIFAR-10, reporting an accuracy-energy trade against rate-coded baselines on the same board rather than an unconditional improvement. Brehove et al.'s Sigma-Delta Neural Network conversion targets Loihi 2's native graded-spike channel for a frame-based workload, and Adaptive Fission's population-coding transformation reports, inside its own hardware table, unmodified QCFS and SRP baseline configurations measured on the same physical accelerator, the one documented instance in this survey's audited evidence in which an unmodified rate-coded QCFS-lineage output has been measured on physical silicon, achieved by a different paper's non-rate-coded contribution rather than by QCFS's or SRP's own authors. Section 16 returns to what this pattern implies about the two learning populations; the point recorded here is only that abandoning the rate code, in every confirmed vision case in this subsection, is what carried a low-latency conversion method to physical silicon.

A third cluster processes event-native vision directly, without a frame-accumulation stage, and is dominated by direct training. Amir et al.'s TrueNorth gesture-recognition system reads a live DVS128 camera feed straight through to a decision with no host-side frame reconstruction, the strongest sensor-to-decision case in this subsection. Yao et al.'s Speck-based dynamic-computing chip and Richter et al.'s Speck1, already introduced in Section 6 for their surrogate-gradient training route, both carry directly trained networks to physical event-native silicon, and Richter et al.'s own head-to-head comparison of BPTT training against an ANN-to-SNN converted baseline on the identical chip is the clearest same-chip route comparison this survey found for a vision workload. The HiAER-Spike and Compositional Reconfigurable Inferencer (CRI) project converts a SpikingJelly-trained network to physical FPGA-based neuromorphic hardware, one of a small number of SpikingJelly-trained networks in the audited evidence that reach any physical chip, cross-referenced from Section 6. Du et al.'s Temporal Flexibility deploys a single weight set trained with a mixed-timestep augmentation to a physical Speck2e device, reporting on-chip accuracy alongside a measured simulator-to-chip difference, an E2 result already introduced in Section 4 for its timestep-free, event-driven execution rather than a discrete per-layer schedule. Esser et al.'s EEDN trains a network directly under TrueNorth's own hardware constraints, binary neurons and trinary synapses via constrained backpropagation, mapping natively onto the chip rather than converting a separately trained ANN, and reports accuracy, throughput, and power measured on physical silicon across several frame-based vision benchmarks. uBrain's fabricated 40-nanometer application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC) is explicitly designed to ease ANN-to-SNN conversion, reporting power and energy per classification on a radar hand-gesture dataset and on a converted MNIST network from the same fabricated die, and Frenkel et al.'s ODIN reports on-chip local plasticity for a small MNIST network at measured energy per synaptic operation, also on fabricated silicon. 15.2 Audio and Temporal Workloads

Mészáros et al.'s EventProp pipeline trains a leaky integrate-and-fire network with learned synaptic delays through an exact spike-time gradient and deploys it to a physical single-chip Loihi 2 for keyword spotting on the Spiking Heidelberg Digits and Spiking Speech Commands benchmarks, already introduced in Section 6 for its training mechanism; the workload-relevant addition here is that both benchmarks are genuinely temporal classification tasks, not frame-accumulated vision relabeled as audio. Bos and Muir's Xylo Audio 2 deployment for the Aloha keyword-spotting benchmark follows a commercial, direct-training-first pipeline through Rockpool, reporting accuracy, dynamic power, and dynamic energy per inference on physical, purpose-built silicon compared against GPU, CPU, and other embedded baselines. Carpegna, Savino, and Di Carlo's Spiker+ framework, already noted in Section 15.1 for its configurable reset, evaluates the same generated accelerator on the Spiking Heidelberg Dataset and on AudioMNIST alongside its MNIST result, reporting accuracy, latency, and power from physical FPGA synthesis and deployment. Frenkel and Indiveri's ReckOn is a single fabricated processor evaluated across three different workloads, DVS Gestures, Spiking Heidelberg Digits, and a synthetic rodent-navigation task, using an eligibility-propagation rule for on-chip learning and reporting accuracy and energy per synaptic operation from five fabricated dies; its Spiking Heidelberg Digits result is the case recorded here, and its other two workloads recur in Table 22. Ortone et al.'s tactile-localization system belongs in this subsection rather than in vision because its represented variable is a continuously varying touch signal rather than a static image, and it is a hand-designed circuit, not a trained network in the direct-training or conversion sense; a Bragg-grating-based e-skin drives a population of DYNAP-SE analog neurons for tactile localization, reporting localization error against a software baseline alongside resting and median inference power measured on physical, mixed-signal silicon. Every case in this subsection is either directly trained or hand-designed with local plasticity; none is an ANN-to-SNN conversion. This is a narrower population than vision's, and this survey does not read the absence of a converted audio or temporal case here as evidence that no such conversion has ever been deployed, only that this survey's audited corpus did not locate one.

15.3 Control and Robotics

Robotics is the workload domain in which this survey's audited evidence is most heavily direct-trained or hand-designed, and in which a single case most often combines several evidence-relevant properties at once, closed-loop actuation, a non-rate code, and a comparison against a conventional embedded baseline. Arfa et al. combine hardware-aware fine-tuning with eight-bit quantization to deploy directly trained spiking Q-networks, reinforcement-learning policies for CartPole and Acrobot, to physical SpiNNaker2, already introduced in Section 6 for its quantization mechanism; the workload here is closed-loop control rather than classification, and the policy's action at each tick is itself the deployed output. Tang et al.'s mapless-navigation policy, introduced in Section 5 as a hybrid method that applies a Loihi-specific integer weight-rescaling step after gradient-based training, is measured on physical Loihi hardware at a reported reduction in energy per inference relative to a conventional deep-reinforcement-learning baseline on an embedded GPU. A separate, industrial peg-in-hole force-control policy, also introduced in Section 6, trains a spiking reinforcement-learning policy end to end in simulation and ports it to both physical Loihi 1 and Loihi 2 for a KUKA robotic arm, reporting latency and dynamic energy against named CPU and GPU comparators on the same insertion task. Cheng and Nikolić's goalkeeper robot, already placed in Section 6's hand-designed local-plasticity cluster, maps a live DVS camera stream through SpiNNaker to a servomotor in a closed loop disconnected from any host PC, reporting response latency, interception accuracy against balls moving up to one meter per second, and maximum system power, the clearest sensor-to-decision robotics case in the audited evidence. Tang et al.'s head-direction simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) circuit, from the same hand-designed cluster, runs on physical Loihi and reports dynamic power roughly two orders of magnitude below a GMapping baseline on a conventional CPU at comparable localization accuracy. Pei et al.'s Tianjic chip demonstrates a hybrid ANN and SNN architecture in a single physical chip driving a real-time robotics demonstration spanning object detection, tracking, voice recognition, obstacle avoidance, and balance control, reporting throughput and power-efficiency ratios against GPU baselines from a hardware-in-the-loop demonstration rather than a benchmark table. CLP-SNN, already discussed in Section 6's on-chip-learning survey (Table 7), performs online continual learning directly on physical Loihi 2 for a few-shot robotic-vision task, reporting latency and energy against a Jetson Orin Nano baseline; it sits at the boundary between vision and robotics because its workload is object recognition, but its deployment claim is about on-chip adaptation for a robotic platform rather than a fixed classifier.

Every closed-loop case in this cluster, the goalkeeper, the SLAM circuit, and Tianjic's demonstration, reports either a hand-designed circuit or a hybrid architecture rather than a network converted from a pretrained ANN by the classical or QCFS-lineage correspondence Sections 7 through 9 formalize. The reinforcement-learning cases, Arfa et al., Tang et al.'s navigation policy, and the force-control policy, are directly trained by gradient descent. No case in this subsection is a rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion of the kind Section 15.1 documents for vision, a gap this survey states rather than explains, since a causal account of why the two learning branches concentrate differently across workloads is not something the audited evidence itself supports.

15.4 Scientific and Industrial Workloads

This survey's audited evidence for scientific and industrial sensing outside vision, audio, and closed-loop control is thin, two confirmed cases, and this subsection states that population size honestly rather than padding it with cases that belong more naturally elsewhere. Imam and Cleland deploy a hand-designed olfactory-bulb-model circuit that learns odor identity online through one-shot local plasticity on physical Loihi silicon, with no backpropagation and no ANN-to-SNN conversion step anywhere in the pipeline, reporting latency and energy per sniff against a wind-tunnel chemosensor dataset. This survey records a caveat the audited evidence itself carries. A 2023 replication study by Dennler, van Schaik, and Schmuker found that the underlying chemosensor dataset suffers from sensor drift and a non-randomized acquisition protocol, and that a simple hash-table baseline matches or exceeds the circuit's reported accuracy once the data is split properly. The hardware latency and energy measurement itself is not invalidated by this finding, but the algorithmic and scientific claim attached to it is substantially undercut, and this survey reports the measurement at E1 while flagging the accuracy claim as unresolved rather than folding the two together into one verdict. Andrei et al. extend Few-Spikes conversion to complex-valued layers for an unfolded multidimensional harmonic-retrieval network, a signal-processing workload rather than a classification benchmark, and map the result to a physical single-chip SpiNNaker2 board, reporting board power against a Jetson Xavier baseline for a serialized temporal Few-Spikes code rather than a spike-count rate estimate, already introduced in Section 9 for its non-rate-coded conversion.

This survey's own research passes did not locate a physically or near-physically deployed case of industrial anomaly detection, large-scale neural simulation informing a deployment claim, or an emerging sequence or transformer-style SNN workload with a matching measurement-index record. Their absence here is a statement about what this survey's audited corpus contains, not a claim that such deployments do not exist.

16.2 The Rate-Coding Falsifier and Its Counterexamples

Section 9.7 already reported the search this subsection now returns to as the direct answer to Section 1's motivating question. Through the falsifier search this survey conducted into 16 September 2026, no audited source both introduces a recent, low-timestep, spike-count or firing-rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion algorithm and reports that same proposed algorithm measured on named physical neuromorphic silicon. This is a bounded search result over a stated population, not an impossibility claim, and it says nothing about direct training, classical conversion, or later application papers that reuse an existing converter.

Table 23's low-latency-conversion row makes the finding concrete. Of its 16 records, 13 are algorithm-only, every one of them rate, burst, or signed-rate coded, and every one E5, software simulation with no chip named in the experiments section. The remaining 3 are tagged both, meaning the proposing paper itself reports E1 physical hardware execution, and all 3 abandon rate coding at the point of conversion to do so. Quartz converts to a time-to-first-spike code and reaches physical Loihi 1. Brehove et al. convert to Loihi 2's native graded sigma-delta channel. Andrei et al. extend Few-Spikes conversion to a serialized temporal code on physical SpiNNaker2. Zero of the 16 records in this row are both rate-coded and E1 in their own paper. This is exactly the gap the falsifier names, read directly off Table 23 rather than asserted separately from it.

Three verified counterexamples widen this picture without weakening the narrow claim above, and each answers one of three separate questions this survey does not collapse into one. Did the proposing paper deploy its own contribution? Adaptive Fission's own hardware table includes unmodified no-Fission QCFS and SRP baseline rows measured on a physical Lynxi HP201 accelerator, an E1 result neither the QCFS nor the SRP paper itself reported. Rate-coded QCFS-lineage networks have therefore reached silicon, achieved by a later, different paper's population-coding contribution rather than by QCFS's or SRP's own authors. Is a rate-coded, low-timestep SNN reaching silicon confined to conversion at all? Arfa et al. report physical SpiNNaker2 latency, power, and energy for rate-coded spiking Q-networks run for 10 or 20 ticks, a genuine rate-coded, low-timestep result on named silicon that falls outside the falsifier's scope because the method is direct Q-learning with hardware-aware fine-tuning, not ANN-to-SNN conversion. Does a vendor's own product label settle the coding question? BrainChip's Akida deployments are physical and are called SNNs by the vendor, yet the standard QuantizeML-to-CNN2SNN route collapses the multi-step firing-rate window the classical correspondence assumes into a single feedforward pass over quantized activations, with no user-exposed simulation horizon and no membrane potential carried across steps. Akida's physical results therefore do not satisfy the falsifier's requirement that hidden activations retain a binary spike-count or firing-rate representation over a finite window, independent of what the runtime is named.

This falsifier's scope excludes direct-training methods, application papers that reuse an existing converter, classical conversion deployments audited under Section 7's earlier correspondence, non-silicon RTL or cycle-level synthesis, and later third-party baseline deployments of an existing method. A future paper that introduces a new low-timestep conversion algorithm whose hidden activations retain a rate or spike-count representation, and that itself reports at least one on-device measurement on named physical neuromorphic silicon, weakens this finding. None of the sources audited here does so.

16.3 Semantic Findings on Reset, Operators, and Time

Reset semantics are the field this survey has traced in the greatest cross-toolchain depth, and the corpus-level pattern is asymmetric. Of the eleven typed edges Table 16 applies the input, output, preservation, transformation, rejection, and evidence schema to, two reject the source model outright, four transform subtractive reset into a different rule, one is undocumented, one concerns the sample boundary rather than the reset rule, and only two preserve subtractive reset, one only through the two-compartment emulation NxTF requires on Loihi 1 and the other natively through Sinabs's route to Speck. A third preserving row, Lava-DL NetX HDF5 to the Lava LIF process, preserves a hard-zero reset instead. At platform level, Table 18's nine audited platforms range across every capability status this survey defines for the threshold-and-reset field, native on Speck, Xylo, and BrainScaleS-2, transformed on Loihi 1 and both Akida generations, unsupported on the audited routes to Loihi 2 and both SpiNNaker generations. No audited platform in Table 18 recovers subtractive reset through a native, single-compartment hardware primitive on a chip whose own default behavior is reset-to-zero.

Graph operators show the opposite pattern, uniform rather than spread. Every one of the nine platforms in Table 18 carries a transformed status for graph operators and supported operations, meaning no platform accepts an arbitrary computation graph without a mapper restructuring, splitting, or rejecting unsupported topology first. Encoder and decoder status is equally uniform, host-assisted at all nine platforms, confirming at corpus scale that no audited platform performs both input encoding and output decoding entirely on-chip.

Time model findings come from a different axis than either field above. Section 4.1 and Audit A distinguish four mechanisms that this survey's evidence keeps structurally separate, a static per-layer horizon set before inference, a per-input early-exit horizon set during inference, asynchronous within-core computation coexisting with a chip-wide barrier, and timestep-free event-driven execution with no discrete horizon at all. Among PASCAL, QAC, MT-SNN, and Temporal Flexibility, the four sources this survey audited for static heterogeneous per-layer scheduling, none demonstrates that schedule executing on physical or even cycle-simulated hardware; PASCAL's and APEX's own RTL synthesis evidence covers only a uniform-timestep neuron overhead check, not the mixed-horizon schedule itself. Barrier synchronization coexisting with asynchronous within-core computation is independently confirmed for Loihi and Tianjic-lineage systems, and this coexistence does not, on its own, establish that different layers hold independent discrete time coordinates, the non-equivalence Section 4.1 already states. Timestep-free execution is the one mechanism in this group with a confirmed physical result, Temporal Flexibility's deployment to a physical Speck2e device, and that result is event-driven and horizon-free at the point of execution, not a demonstration of the static mixed-horizon schedule the other three sources study only in software.

16.5 Evidence and Interpretation

Sections 16.1 through 16.4 report counts, verified claims, route states, and sourced facts. Nothing above this point assigns a cause to any of those findings. The paragraphs below state candidate explanations explicitly as interpretation, not as an extension of the evidence.

Why zero of the 16 low-latency-conversion records in Table 23 pair rate coding with the proposing paper's own physical hardware measurement admits several candidate explanations that the audited evidence does not adjudicate between. One reading is architectural, that a multi-step, binary, count-based representation is a genuinely poor match for the barrier-synchronized or fully asynchronous execution families Section 12 compares, so groups that abandon rate coding are responding to a real hardware constraint. A second reading is a matter of engineering cost, that building a hardware-specific pipeline for one platform is a separate, substantial effort from the software contribution a conversion paper is otherwise built around, so most conversion papers simply stop before that step regardless of the code's hardware suitability. A third reading is about publication incentives, that a venue rewarding a simulated accuracy curve across many timestep values gives an author little additional credit for the months of engineering a physical deployment would cost. A fourth reading is about access, that the platforms with the clearest documented capability, Loihi 2 and the archived Lava stack among them, are also the platforms gated behind community membership or now frozen without a successor toolchain, which would depress physical results independent of any property of the code itself. This survey's audited evidence is consistent with all four readings and does not distinguish among them.

Section 15.5 records a parallel, workload-level version of the same open question. Vision carries every confirmed rate-coded and non-rate-coded conversion case in the audited application evidence, while audio, temporal sensing, control, robotics, and the two scientific and industrial cases skew toward direct training and hand-designed circuits. Whether that skew reflects a property of the workloads themselves, a property of which research communities publish hardware results, or a property of which correspondence a given code admits for a given signal class is a question this survey's evidence does not answer, and it is restated here as an open interpretation rather than resolved by restating it.

One reading this survey does reject as unsupported by its own evidence is a strong impossibility claim, that rate-coded low-timestep conversion cannot reach silicon. Table 23 and Table 24 already falsify that stronger reading directly, through the Adaptive Fission baseline rows and through Akida's physical, if semantically transformed, route. The narrower, demonstrated finding is that no audited paper has yet closed the loop between proposing such a method and measuring that same method on hardware. 17.1 Failed and Unverified Contract Edges

Three edge-level facts anchor most of what follows. First, NIR 1.0.8 has no field capable of representing reset by subtraction at all, a structural rejection rather than an incomplete exporter, so SpikingJelly's rate-coding conversion output cannot enter that schema without first changing the network it represents. Second, the same triggering condition, SpikingJelly's default \(v_{\mathrm{reset}}=\mathrm{None}\), a subtractive reset, independently blocks SpikingJelly's own Lava exporter, which raises an explicit guard against a subtractive or nonzero fixed reset in its own code, so two of Table 16's eleven typed edges are blocked at the same first step by the same triggering condition meeting two different mechanisms, an explicit code-level guard in the Lava exporter and the absence of any field for subtractive reset in the NIR schema. Third, this survey located no documented route for subtractive reset through custom Loihi 2 microcode outside the public Lava and NetX artifacts, an edge recorded as undocumented rather than blocked, because Loihi 2's neuron engine is documented as fully microcode-programmable and an inference of impossibility from this absence would be unwarranted.

17.2 Algorithm-Hardware Codesign for Heterogeneous Schedules

Section 9.9 deferred a specific question to this subsection, the design tradeoff between NeuroFlex's column-level and a coarser layer-level granularity for mixing ANN and SNN execution modes within one inference. This survey's own audit of PASCAL, QAC, MT-SNN, and Temporal Flexibility gives that question a sharper, evidence-grounded form. None of the four sources demonstrates a static, heterogeneous, per-layer discrete-timestep schedule executing on physical or even cycle-simulated hardware. PASCAL's and APEX's RTL synthesis results are genuine E4 hardware-model evidence, but both cover only a uniform-timestep neuron overhead check, not the Adaptive Layerwise mixed-horizon schedule that produces PASCAL's own headline number. QAC states the precise mechanism a hardware scheduler for this class of method would need to resolve, that a pipelined multicore accelerator can stall while a downstream layer waits for an upstream layer to finish a different number of steps, but this is a qualitative warning in the paper's own text, not a documented toolchain path, a compiler invocation, or a measured result.

The open problem is therefore precise rather than diffuse. A temporal-allocation method that assigns a different static horizon to each layer has no audited evidence, at any evidence class, that the resulting heterogeneous schedule executes correctly and efficiently on a barrier-synchronized, asynchronous, or software-programmable target from Section 12. A concrete research direction is to synthesize or fabricate a scheduler that resolves QAC's own named stall mechanism for a stated execution family, and to report the result against at least the E4 hardware-model boundary this survey already applies to PASCAL and APEX, extending it to the mixed-horizon case those two papers do not cover. A second, related direction follows Section 9.9's column-level and layer-level distinction directly, testing whether a finer execution granularity changes the stall behavior QAC describes only at the coarser layer level.

17.3 Portable Reset and Precision Semantics

Table 16's eleven typed edges already show that reset semantics survive a software or compilation boundary in the minority of audited cases, two rejected, four transformed, one undocumented, one concerning the sample boundary rather than the reset rule, three preserved, one of the three preserved only through an emulation. Section 11.5's second pattern extends the same gap to precision, numeric bit width is quantized at nearly every audited boundary, and the specific widths chosen differ from edge to edge, 9-bit weights on Loihi 1's NxTF route, 8-bit weights and 16-bit state for Speck through Sinabs, 8-bit weights alongside 32-bit floating-point state for SpiNNaker 2's NIR importer, one, two, four, or eight bits depending on layer and configuration for Akida through QuantizeML. NIR's own documentation states plainly that a declarative schema does not by itself certify identical integration order, spike timing, reset timing, or numerical quantization across two backends that both accept the same graph.

No interchange format or compiler chain audited in this survey preserves reset semantics and precision jointly across a training framework to hardware chain without either transformation or outright rejection along the way. This is an open problem in the schema itself, not only in any one framework's implementation of it. A concrete research direction is an interchange extension, whether to NIR or to a successor schema, that encodes reset mode as a first-class field alongside a per-quantity precision declaration naming weights, thresholds, and state separately, verified by demonstrating that at least two of Table 16's audited edges preserve the extended field where they currently transform or reject it. Graph operators and supported operations compound this problem rather than sitting outside it. Every one of Table 18's nine audited platforms transforms an arbitrary input graph before executing it, so a portable semantics proposal that addresses reset and precision alone would still leave the operator-set boundary this survey finds uniformly restrictive.

17.4 Measurement and Reproducibility Gaps

Section 14's measurement checklist names seven fields a comparable deployment report must state, and Section 15's applications evidence documents specific, sourced gaps against each of them, memory and communication cost left unstated in most E1 records, static and dynamic power separated in a small minority, batch size named explicitly in only a handful of cases, and a Pareto claim inheriting the boundary of its weakest reported axis. Two further, more specific reproducibility failures surfaced during this survey's own audit rather than in its secondary literature. PASCAL's published ResNet-18 and CIFAR-10 headline of \(T_{\mathrm{eff}}=3.14\) is reproducible from the paper's own released script only when its 17 listed per-layer timestep values are silently truncated to the 10 energy coefficients a Python zip() call actually consumes, an undocumented cardinality mismatch neither the paper nor its code addresses. MT-SNN's withdrawn ICLR manuscript and its later accepted Frontiers abstract name different temporal-alignment mechanisms, average-and-repeat alignment in the former and a named Scaled Synaptic Current Accumulation mechanism in the latter, so the two records document the same layerwise-allocation lineage without being interchangeable evidence for how alignment is actually implemented.

Both cases point to the same open problem at different scales. A headline number in this literature is not reliably checked against the released artifact that is supposed to produce it, and a method's identity is not reliably stable across a manuscript's own revision history. A concrete research direction is procedural rather than technical, adopting Section 14.6's checklist as an artifact-review requirement before a deployment number is cited as reproduced, and requiring that a paper's released code be executed against its own stated headline before that headline enters a downstream comparison table, exactly the check this survey performed for PASCAL specifically and found to fail silently.

17.5 Access and Maintenance Risk

Three access and maintenance facts recur across this survey's platform and route evidence. Intel archived the entire Lava, Lava-DL, and NetX stack during 2026 with no successor software development kit yet announced, freezing every audited edge that terminates at Loihi 2 through that stack, including the blocked SpikingJelly-to-Lava route in Table 24, at whatever state it held at the point of archival. Loihi access more generally remains gated to members of Intel's Neuromorphic Research Community rather than openly available, a condition that governed access to the NxTF-to-Loihi 1 route while it was an open chain and continues to govern access to Loihi 2 today. Lynxi's BIDL, Lyngor, and LynSDK toolchain, the software behind the Adaptive Fission and QCFS-lineage baseline measurements in Table 23 and Table 24, is documented in the sources this survey reviewed as effectively unavailable outside mainland China. A fourth, structural fact compounds the first three, every platform-capability record behind Table 18 remains provisional pending full-text promotion of its underlying vendor manual, the limitation Section 10.4 already states once for the entire comparison.

The open problem here is distinct from ordinary software rot in reproducible machine learning research. A route this survey classifies as physical today, NxTF to Loihi 1 among them, can become a closed chain within the same toolchain's own generational transition, and a route this survey classifies as blocked can become permanently so once the stack it depended on is archived with no successor, as happened to the Lava-based routes during the audit period this survey itself spans. Reproducing or extending an audited result therefore depends on infrastructure access and continuity that this survey's own evidence shows is neither guaranteed nor evenly distributed across platforms. A concrete research direction is independent, vendor-neutral archival of reference toolchain versions, worked examples, and their exact commit identifiers, tied explicitly to a platform-capability record's own verification status rather than left to a vendor's own retention policy, so that a future audit can distinguish a route that has become genuinely obsolete from one that has merely become harder to access.

18 Researcher Entry Guide and Conclusion

The preceding seventeen sections build a dependency chain from computation and representation to open research problems. A reader entering the field at a specific point, rather than from Section 1 forward, needs a shorter path through that chain and a small set of practical questions rather than a restated outline. This section gives four reading paths, a route-choice guide built from the contract fields Sections 10 through 13 already fix, a minimum reporting checklist, and referrals to the specialist surveys this survey has bounded itself against throughout. It closes with a short conclusion that states this survey's deployment argument rather than summarizing what came before it.

18.1 Reading Paths

A reader arriving from conventional deep learning who wants to understand how a trained ANN becomes an SNN should read Section 3's neuron and synapse dynamics, then Section 4's codes and decoding, then Section 7's complete conversion derivation in full before sampling Section 9's interventions by name. Section 8's finite-time error taxonomy is optional on a first pass but load-bearing before evaluating any specific low-latency method's claimed timestep reduction.

A reader who already has a specific chip in hand and wants to know what it can execute should start from Section 10's eleven-field contract, read the execution family that chip belongs to in Section 12, and check Section 12.7's Table 18 for that platform's row before consulting Section 13 for whether a named route from a training framework to that chip has actually been traced. Section 18.2 below turns this reading path into a short list of questions rather than a chapter list.

A reader auditing a specific deployment claim, for a thesis, a peer review, or a competing method's stated baseline, should apply Section 5.4's five evidence classes to that specific claim rather than to the paper as a whole, check the claim against Section 14.2's six measurement boundaries and Section 14.6's checklist, and consult Section 16 for whether a similar claim in this survey's own audited corpus required a qualification the claim under review omits.

A reader designing a new low-latency conversion method, the population Section 16.2 and Section 17.6 both return to directly, should read Section 8 to identify which named error mechanism the new method targets, Section 9 to check whether an existing intervention already targets the same mechanism, and Section 17.1 before committing to a target platform, since the reset, precision, and operator gaps documented there apply to a new method exactly as they apply to every audited one.

18.2 Route Choice Guide

Section 13.5 already gives one worked instance of the general question this subsection generalizes, that a practitioner's actual requirement determines which of several physical routes is usable, not which route is most often cited. Before committing to a target, a practitioner should be able to answer four questions from the contract fields Section 10.2 fixes. Which of the eleven fields does the deployment claim actually depend on, and is that field non-negotiable, for example does the claim require reset by subtraction executing without a resource-doubling emulation, in which case Table 24 narrows the physical option set to Speck through Sinabs, Xylo through Rockpool, or Loihi 1 through NxTF at the stated compartment cost, and rules out both SpiNNaker generations and the QuantizeML-to-Akida chain even though three of those four chains are otherwise physical. Does the claim depend on the classical rate-matching correspondence Section 7 derives, in which case the Akida route's own transformation of that correspondence, documented in Table 24, disqualifies it regardless of how physical its execution is. Does the claim require on-chip plasticity rather than a fixed, host-trained weight set, in which case Table 7's native-versus-exercised distinction applies, native learning machinery on Loihi 1 and Loihi 2 is not evidence that a specific published route exercises it.

A second set of questions is about the chip and toolchain themselves rather than the field. What generation is being targeted, and does the cited capability record apply to that generation specifically, given that Akida 1.0 and Akida 2.0 already diverge on plasticity and reset status in Table 18. What toolchain version produced the cited result, given that NIR's reset field, Sinabs's mapper behavior, and Lava's built-in neuron semantics have each changed materially across the versions this survey audited. Is the toolchain still maintained, given that the entire Lava, Lava-DL, and NetX stack was archived in 2026 with no successor announced. Is access to the target gated or regionally restricted, given that Loihi requires Neuromorphic Research Community membership and Lynxi's toolchain is documented as effectively unavailable outside mainland China. A route that answers all four contract questions favorably but fails one of these four access questions is not a usable route for a practitioner without that access, independent of its technical merit.

18.3 Minimum Reporting Checklist

A deployment claim this survey would classify without qualification states each of the following. The first eleven items are the contract fields Table 15 fixes; the last three are the boundary, baseline, and evidence-class fields Section 14 and Section 5.4 fix separately from the contract itself.

  • The network graph and which operators it uses, and whether the target's mapper accepted, transformed, split, or rejected any of them.
  • The neuron equations and update order, named as IF, LIF, CUBA-LIF, ALIF, multi-compartment, or another explicitly stated form.
  • Synaptic dynamics and whether delay is a configured parameter, a fixed step, or absent.
  • Threshold and reset semantics, reset-to-zero or reset-by-subtraction, and whether that semantics is native, transformed, or emulated on the named target.
  • The neural code and spike payload, and how many bits or what magnitude range it carries.
  • The input encoder and output decoder, and which of encoding and decoding, if either, executes on-chip rather than on a host.
  • Precision for weights, thresholds, and state, reported separately rather than as one bit-width figure.
  • The time model and termination condition, a static horizon, a per-input stopping rule, a barrier-synchronized tick, or an unbounded event stream.
  • Connectivity, fan-in, fan-out, and routing, including whether the mapped graph fit the target's resource budget without splitting.
  • Plasticity and state persistence, whether a weight update executes on the device and whether state persists across inferences.
  • Required host-side operations, stated explicitly rather than left implicit in a system diagram.
  • The measurement boundary from Section 14.2, chip-only, board-level, host-inclusive, sensor-to-decision, modeled, or physical.
  • The baseline's workload, precision, and boundary, stated rather than implied by table proximity, and the batch size or an explicit single-sample statement.
  • The evidence class from Section 5.4, E1 through E5, assigned to the specific claim being reported rather than to the paper as a whole.

18.4 Specialist Referrals

This survey bounds several topics at the depth deployment claims require and refers a reader who needs more to a named specialist source, repeating here the referrals already placed at their point of use so that a reader who begins at Section 18 has them in one place. For learning theory and algorithms beyond what a deployment comparison needs, see Nunes et al., useful for its breadth across neuron models, coding, and learning, and Khan et al., useful for training methodologies developed considerably further than Section 6 attempts. For local learning rules and open benchmarking of STDP and related mechanisms, see Caviglia et al.. For event-based sensing, representation, and algorithms ahead of the point this survey treats a sensor as a source of a code rather than a device, see Gallego et al., particularly useful for the sensing and preprocessing detail Section 4 and Section 14 do not repeat.

For a data-science comparison of SNN training and simulation frameworks by usability and API design, see Manna et al.. For an extension of the software-implementation view to GPU and RISC-V accelerators and a discussion of incompatible cross-toolchain reporting practices, see Farsa et al.. For NIR's own account of the semantic disagreements it exposes across simulators and hardware back ends, beyond the reset-specific case Section 11 develops in depth, see Pedersen et al.. For hardware-platform surveys with a longer view of circuit and architecture families than Section 12's deployment-contract framing needs, see Schuman et al.'s early integration of models, algorithms, hardware, and applications, Bouvier et al.'s account of SNN algorithms and their hardware-implementation benchmarks, Davies et al.'s synthesis of measured Loihi results, and Shrestha et al.'s connection of model semantics to platform-specific compiler support.

For system-level deployment barriers and scaling challenges beyond the field-by-field contract this survey builds, see Schuman et al.'s later review connecting algorithms and applications to full-stack deployment barriers and Kudithipudi et al.'s account of the challenges in scaling neuromorphic systems and their software ecosystems. For a formal benchmarking framework that separates proxy evaluation from hardware-aware measurement, a distinction Section 14 applies but does not itself standardize, see NeuroBench. For edge-oriented deployment methods organized by proxy-versus-hardware evaluation depth, see Deng et al.'s EdgeSNN survey.

18.5 Conclusion

A deployment claim about a spiking neural network is not one fact but a bundle of eleven separable fields, each of which can be mathematically defined, representable in software, and faithfully executed on a target, or can fail any one of those three tests while passing the other two. This survey's central argument is that the field-by-field contract in Section 10, not a single adjective like portable, supported, or efficient, is the unit a reader should demand before trusting a stated route, a reported timestep count, or a measured energy figure. Applied at that granularity across sixteen sections of derivation and audit, the evidence shows a field that is genuinely more capable than a one-line verdict would suggest, physical silicon reached from the synchronous digital family through Loihi 1, the asynchronous digital family through Speck, the software-programmable many-core family through SpiNNaker 1, and the FPGA and custom-ASIC family through Akida 1.0, with the analog and mixed-signal family's own audited route to BrainScaleS-2 physical only for its non-spiking hagen mode rather than for a spiking network, on-chip learning machinery present but rarely exercised, event-driven substrates executing without any discrete timestep at all, and also a field narrower and more code-dependent than a survey organized around headline accuracy numbers would reveal, a specific and repeatedly confirmed gap between a low-timestep rate-coded conversion method's own published contribution and that same method's own measurement on named hardware, closed in the audited literature only by abandoning the rate code itself or by a later, unrelated paper's engineering effort.

Neither half of that picture is this survey's verdict on whether neuromorphic computing succeeds or fails as a research program. The gap documented in Section 16 is a demonstrated finding about a bounded, named population of papers audited through a stated date, not a general claim about what rate coding, or any other representation, can or cannot achieve on future silicon. What this survey offers instead is the vocabulary and the audit method that let that gap, and the dozens of narrower findings alongside it, be stated precisely enough to check, to cite, and eventually to close.
