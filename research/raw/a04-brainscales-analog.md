## 1. BrainScaleS-1 and BrainScaleS-2 architecture

### BrainScaleS-1 (wafer-scale system)

- BrainScaleS-1 (HICANN-based wafer-scale system) is a **physical, analog emulation** of neuons and synapses, not a numerical simulator: "a very large analog neuromorphic hardware system... able to use complex neural models as well as realistic network topologies... to allow the direct execution of models which previously could have been simulated numerically only" [Schemmel et al., 2010, "A wafer-scale neuromorphic hardware system for large-scale neural modeling," ISCAS 2010, https://doi.org/10.1109/ISCAS.2010.5536970].
- Built from HICANN ("High Input Count Analog Neural Network") ASICs; the system as deployed emulates 512 neurons per chip and reaches up to ~10,000× biological real time on the wafer-scale system: "The neuromorphic waferscale system in Heidelberg" description and 10,000× acceleration figure are given at [https://brainscales.kip.uni-heidelberg.de/] and reiterated in [Schmitt et al. 2017, "Neuromorphic Hardware In The Loop," https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1703.01909]: "enabling an acceleration factor of 10 000 compared to the biological time domain."
- Each neuron on BrainScaleS-1/HICANN is calibrated via translation rules mapping target biological parameters (e.g., membrane time constant) to hardware control parameters, accounting for transistor mismatch from the wafer manufacturing process [Schmitt et al. 2017, Section II-C, same URL as above].
- A dedicated thesis on BrainScaleS-1 device variability states: "Its analog circuits underlie time-independent variations that occur during the manufacturing process; this effect is known as mismatch... The variation of the obtained parameters from trial to trial are significant and set the limit for the reachable precision" [Device Variability in Synapses of Neuromorphic Circuits, heiDOK thesis, 2017, https://archiv.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/volltextserver/22742/].

### BrainScaleS-2 (single-chip ASIC, 65 nm)

- Primary architecture reference: Pehle, Billaudelle, Cramer, Kaiser, Schreiber, Stradmann, Weis, Leibfried, Müller, Schemmel, "The BrainScaleS-2 Accelerated Neuromorphic System With Hybrid Plasticity," Front. Neurosci. 16:795876, 2022, https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2022.795876 (also arXiv:2201.11063).
- **Architecture**: "a single-chip ASIC with an analog core consisting of 512 neurons and 217 synapses [i.e., 512×256 synapse array], as well as two embedded plasticity and control processors" [Pehle et al. 2022, Conclusion]. Fabricated in 65 nm CMOS [arXiv:2006.13177].
- **Analog neuron circuits**: each neuron circuit is individually configurable via 80 bits of local digital SRAM plus 24 analog parameters set by an on-chip 10-bit DAC, controlling all potentials/conductances of the neuron model; this allows calibration of production-induced fixed-pattern deviations per-neuron [Pehle et al. 2022, Section 2.2].
- **Digital plasticity processor (PPU)**: BrainScaleS-2 has two "plasticity processing units" (PPUs) — embedded digital microprocessors implementing a subset of the 32-bit POWER ISA plus custom SIMD vector instructions (VMX-like) — that read/write synaptic weights and neuron state via the columnar ADC (CADC), enabling programmable "hybrid plasticity" (analog measurement + digital computation), on-chip calibration, in-the-loop learning readout, orchestration of ANN-mode computation, and virtual-environment simulation [Pehle et al. 2022, Section 2.3].
- **Acceleration factor — precise meaning**: "Compared to biological model dynamics, the emulation runs one thousand times faster... Thus, a single BrainScaleS ASIC can perform several tera-correlation measurements per second" [Pehle et al. 2022, Section 2.3]. Concretely, this means all analog time constants (membrane, synaptic) are physically scaled down by 1000× relative to the values they represent in "biological time" — a 10 ms biological membrane time constant corresponds to a physical circuit time constant of 10 µs. This is NOT digital timestep speedup; it is a physical circuit property (the analog circuit literally evolves faster because component values — capacitances, bias currents — are chosen accordingly). BrainScaleS-1 (wafer-scale) uses a 10,000× acceleration factor instead [Schmitt et al. 2017, quoted above], showing the factor is a hardware design parameter, not a universal constant. The BrainScaleS-2 "Accelerated Analog Neuromorphic Computing" overview paper also frames this as "all time-constants are several orders of magnitude smaller than in biology" [arXiv:2003.11996, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.11996].
- **Neuron models**: LIF (leaky integrate-and-fire) with synaptic currents as superpositions of spike-triggered exponential kernels, reset-by-conductance to a reset potential for a finite refractory period; extended with adaptation and exponential near-threshold terms for the full AdEx (adaptive exponential I&F) model, plus optional multi-compartment/dendritic interaction [Pehle et al. 2022, Section 2.2; "Versatile emulation of spiking neural networks on an accelerated neuromorphic substrate," arXiv:1912.12980, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1912.12980]. The full AdEx model was validated against parameter sets from Naud et al. 2008, "honoring the acceleration factor of 1,000, the physical membrane capacitance, and the voltage range of the silicon neuron" [Pehle et al. 2022, Section 3.1].
- **Time model / scheduling of stimulus and readout**: BrainScaleS-2 experiments run in fixed wall-clock "experiment frames" — the PPU orchestrates spike injection, batched execution, and readout. Pehle et al. describe "batch mode execution... used for parameter sweeps, evaluation of biology-inspired learning rules, or inference" and "in-the-loop operation" that closes control loops "on several time scales and at different points of the system hierarchy" [Pehle et al. 2022, Section 2.1]. For the non-spiking (ANN/MAC) mode, an explicit fixed "input interval" Δt_input (~100 ns to ~500 ns) is used: "All input is applied during the time interval Δt_input, after which the membrane voltage is digitized [via CADC] and the neuron is set to the reset voltage by a reset signal from the PPU" [arXiv:2003.11996]. This is a hard architectural fact: even though the underlying neuron circuit dynamics are continuous analog, the *experiment* is scheduled by the digital PPU/FPGA into discrete batched frames with explicit stimulus-injection and readout timing controlled digitally — continuous analog dynamics are sampled/triggered by a surrounding digital control layer, not run in unstructured free time.
- **Precision / variability / calibration requirement**: "Analog neuromorphic systems can usually suffer from temporal noise, fixed-pattern parameter deviations, and a divergence from the original model equations" [Pehle et al. 2022, Section 3]. Calibration is "an iterative process involving a measurement on each of the specific circuits" [ibid.]. Even after calibration, "Despite calibration and even with the same inputs and weights, the outputs of the different neurons are not identical. On the one hand, each output has a statistical noise due to the analog nature of the neuron, on the other hand, fixed-pattern deviations show up between the individual neurons" [BrainScaleS-2 Documentation tutorial, "Exploring the analog MAC operation," https://electronicvisions.github.io/documentation-brainscales2/latest/brainscales2-demos/tp_01-properties.html].
- **Weight precision**: BrainScaleS-2's synaptic crossbar stores a **6-bit** signed weight per synapse (range [−63, 63]), with 5-bit unsigned input resolution and 8-bit resulting neuron-activation (ADC) resolution in the non-spiking ANN mode [hxtorch paper, arXiv:2006.13138: "5-bit unsigned inputs and 6-bit 'signed' weights ([−63, 63])"; also arXiv:2006.13177 abstract: "digital resolution of the input values (5 bit), matrix weights (6 bit) and resulting neuron activations (8 bit)"]. Calibration equalizes synaptic strength "to a precision of 7%" across the 512 neurons [arXiv:2006.13177].
- **On-chip learning**: BrainScaleS-2's "hybrid plasticity" scheme combines analog correlation measurement (via CADC, tens of thousands of measurements/s per synapse) with digital plasticity-rule computation on the PPU. Demonstrated learning rules include reward-modulated STDP (R-STDP), homeostatic plasticity, structural plasticity, and small-ANN-based update rules [Pehle et al. 2022, Section 2.3, citing Wunderlich et al. 2019, Cramer et al. 2020a, Bohnstingl et al. 2019, Billaudelle et al. 2021]. Separately, surrogate-gradient in-the-loop (ITL) training is used for both SNN and ANN modes (Section 3 below).

**Classification**: BrainScaleS-2 architecture facts are E1/E4 hybrid — the architecture itself is documented physical silicon (E1-adjacent as a description of hardware), but specific claims are backed by measured, published experiments on physical chips throughout (see Sections 3–4 for E1 latency/energy numbers).

## 2. hxtorch — PyTorch interface to BrainScaleS-2

- Primary papers: Weis et al. (Spilger et al.), "hxtorch: PyTorch for BrainScaleS-2 — Perceptrons on Analog Neuromorphic Hardware," arXiv:2006.13138, https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.13138 (also published version https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.13138v3.pdf); code at https://github.com/electronicvisions/hxtorch.
- **Two operating modes** are supported on the same chip:
  1. **Non-spiking / "hagen" mode (matrix-multiply / perceptron mode)**: "BrainScaleS-2 is such a neuromorphic system; it includes analog neurons and can operate in both a spiking and non-spiking mode. The non-spiking mode can be used to implement analog vector-matrix multiplication... In this work, we present software that allows BrainScaleS-2 to be used as a backend for PyTorch in the non-spiking mode (i.e. for vector-matrix multiplication)" [arXiv:2006.13138 abstract/intro]. hxtorch implements `torch.autograd.Function`-compatible primitives matching `matmul` and `conv{1,2,3}d` semantics, executed directly on hardware [same source, Section 3.1]. The API includes `hxtorch.perceptron.matmul`, `hxtorch.perceptron.nn.Linear`, `hxtorch.perceptron.nn.ConvertingReLU`, and a `mac` (multiply-accumulate) primitive [BrainScaleS-2 API reference, https://electronicvisions.github.io/documentation-brainscales2/latest/api_hxtorch.html; demo notebook "Train DNNs on BrainScaleS-2," https://electronicvisions.github.io/documentation-brainscales2/stable/brainscales2-demos/tp_02-yin_yang.html].
  2. **Spiking mode (`hxtorch.snn`)**: a separate, later-added PyTorch wrapper library for describing and training SNNs (LIF/LI neuron layers, `Synapse` modules) directly on BSS-2, integrated with PyTorch autograd via `HXModule`/`Handle`-based promises resolved after hardware execution [Pehle, Müller et al., "hxtorch.snn: Machine-learning-inspired Spiking Neural Network Modeling on BrainScaleS-2 Hardware," arXiv:2212.12210, http://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.12210v1]. "hxtorch.snn, enables the hardware-in-the-loop training of spiking neural networks within PyTorch, including support for auto-differentiation in a fully-automated hardware experiment workflow" [same source]. As of the 2022 paper, "hxtorch.snn currently supports LIF and LI neuron layers... a forward and backward PyTorch implementation of the AdEx neuron model is planned" [same source] — i.e., AdEx support in the SNN training wrapper was **not yet complete** as of that publication.
- **Hardware-in-the-loop (HITL) training procedure**: the forward pass (matrix multiply, or SNN dynamics) is executed on the physical chip; the backward pass / gradient computation is done in software using an approximate linear (or LIF/AdEx) model of the hardware, injecting hardware-measured quantities (activations, membrane traces) into the computation graph so that gradients reflect actual device behavior:
  - For the perceptron/matmul mode: "y_i = Σ_j x_j · w_ij · g_BSS-2 + κ_i, with κ_i ~ N(0,σ)" — a simple linear model with a calibrated gain g_BSS-2 and measured Gaussian output noise σ, both obtained via `hxtorch.perceptron.measure_mock_parameter()` [BrainScaleS-2 documentation, "Train DNNs on BrainScaleS-2," https://electronicvisions.github.io/documentation-brainscales2/stable/brainscales2-demos/tp_02-yin_yang.html].
  - For the SNN mode: CADC-sampled membrane traces from the physical chip are injected into a PyTorch computation graph built from the LIF/AdEx equations so that "we aligned the computation graph with the actual dynamics of our silicon neurons" [Pehle et al. 2022, Section 3.3.1].
  - A **"mock" mode** exists that runs the same linear/noise model purely in software (no hardware access) for fast prototyping, matching hardware resolution and value-range restrictions [BrainScaleS-2 docs, same URL].
- **Deployment workflow**: (1) train/pretrain in software or mock mode with hardware-realistic quantization and noise injected; (2) load onto chip via `hxtorch.init_hardware()` with a stored calibration (`CalibrationPath`); (3) optionally continue training with hardware in the loop for one or more epochs to compensate for residual fixed-pattern/calibration mismatch [documented across arXiv:2006.13177 and the BrainScaleS-2 tutorials cited above]. Automatic partitioning of networks that exceed a single chip's 256×512 synapse array onto multiple chips is supported by hxtorch [arXiv:2006.13138 abstract: "Automatic partitioning of neural networks onto one or multiple chips is supported"].

**Classification**: hxtorch itself is software infrastructure (not a physical-silicon claim per se), but every quantitative claim about its behavior below is backed by measured runs on physical BSS-2 chips (E1).

## 3. Conversion support: ANN → BrainScaleS via rate coding, and published converted-CNN runs

**Yes — there is a documented, published path, and it predates BrainScaleS-2.** Two independent lines of evidence:

### (a) Explicit rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion on BrainScaleS-1 (wafer-scale), 2017

- Schmitt, Klähn, Bellec, Grübl, et al. (incl. Schemmel, Meier, Petrovici, Maass, Legenstein), "Neuromorphic Hardware In The Loop: Training a Deep Spiking Network on the BrainScaleS Wafer-Scale System," 2017, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1703.01909 (also igi-web PDF: https://igi-web.tugraz.at/PDF/235.pdf).
- Explicit statement of the pipeline: "We first convert a deep neural network trained in software to a spiking network on the BrainScaleS wafer-scale neuromorphic system, thereby enabling an acceleration factor of 10,000 compared to the biological time domain... For mapping activities from the abstract domain to spikes, we have used a rate-coding scheme" [abstract and conclusion, same source].
- Concrete correspondence used: ReLU units were mapped to LIF neurons with adaptation/exponential features of the AdEx model **disabled**, i.e. the AdEx neuron was configured into a near-linear, threshold-gated regime to approximate a ReLU: "Not all neuron features are required to mimic the ReLU behavior, therefore we disable the adaptation and exponential features of the AdEx model... The refractory period is set as small as possible to be close to a linear relation of the input to the output activity" [Section III-B-3, same source].
- Weights were converted to 4-bit hardware weights on this (BSS-1) system [Section III-B-4, same source] — note this is a coarser weight resolution than BSS-2's 6-bit.
- **Result, directly after conversion, no retraining**: accuracy dropped from 97% (software) to 72% (+12/−10%) [Section IV, same source] — i.e., naive rate-coded transfer without HITL correction produced a large accuracy collapse due to analog distortion.
- **After in-the-loop training** (recording hardware activity, computing gradients in software using the ReLU derivative as an approximation of the untractable hardware activation function, several tens of iterations): accuracy recovered to 95% (+1/−2%), "close to the performance of the software model" [Section IV, same source]. Task: handwritten digit classification (a small network, not deep CNN-scale).
- Explicit methodological point, directly relevant to the exact-IF-equivalence question: "the algorithm had no explicit knowledge about exact parameter values of the neurons and synapses in the BrainScaleS system" and "computing the cost function gradients using a ReLU activation function is sufficient for adapting the weights in the spiking network. This circumvents the difficulty of otherwise having to determine an exact derivative of the cost function with respect to the LIF activation function, which would be further exacerbated by the diversity of neuronal activation functions on the analog substrate" [Conclusion, same source]. **This is a direct statement that the software conversion literature's assumption of an exactly known/tractable IF activation function does not hold on analog hardware — the authors work around it rather than solve it.**

**Classification: E1** — physical silicon, published accuracy AND (indirectly, via the acceleration factor and the wafer-scale timing model) latency characterization, for a rate-coded ANN→SNN converted network.

### (b) Non-spiking "hagen"/ANN mode on BrainScaleS-2 (a related but distinct conversion path)

This is not strictly rate-coded spiking conversion; instead the same physical substrate is reconfigured (via disabling neuron leak/adaptation and using a fixed integration window) to act as a **non-spiking, stateless analog vector-matrix multiplier**, with ReLU realized by the ADC's saturation at the reset voltage:

- "Since the reset voltage of the neuron membrane can be aligned with the lower bound of the CADC conversion range the neuron acts like a ReLU unit in this setting" [Accelerated Analog Neuromorphic Computing, arXiv:2003.11996, Section 3.2, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.11996]. This shows the same silicon substrate (leaky-integrate neuron circuit) can be reconfigured, by disabling leak and choosing the reset point, into an approximate ReLU compute primitive without ever using genuine multi-timestep IF spiking dynamics or rate coding — an architecturally distinct "ANN mode" rather than the "SNN mode + rate coding" pathway.
- Published CNN result on this mode: a 3-layer CNN (2D conv + 2 dense layers) classifying MNIST, weights/activations quantized to 6-bit/5-bit: 98.29% (float32 software) → 98.10% (6-bit software) → 92.13% (as directly transferred to BSS-2, uncorrected) → **98.01% after hardware-in-the-loop training** [Weis et al., arXiv:2006.13177 (also summarized in hxtorch paper and in Pehle et al. 2022 Section 2.2), Table 1]. A smaller dense network: 97.43% → 97.36% → 92.46% → 96.30% [same table].
- A deployed compact system ("BrainScaleS-2 mobile system") ran this ANN mode for atrial-fibrillation detection from ECG on a physical, standalone (non-lab) board: "At a system power consumption of 5.6 W, we measure a total energy consumption of 192 µJ for the ASIC and achieve a classification time of 276 µs per... patient sample. Patients with atrial fibrillation are correctly identified with a detection rate of 93.7 ± 0.7% at 14.0 ± 1.0% false positives" [Stradmann et al., "Demonstrating Analog Inference on the Mobile System," arXiv:2103.15960, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2103.15960]. This is a genuine measured latency+energy+accuracy result on physical silicon for a CNN-derived network — **E1**.

**Classification (ANN-mode CNN deployment): E1** — physical silicon, latency AND energy measured (276 µs/sample, 192 µJ/ASIC, 5.6 W system power; 98.0% MNIST accuracy after HITL training).

### (c) Genuinely spiking, surrogate-gradient-trained SNNs on BrainScaleS-2 (not conversion, but relevant for latency/energy benchmarking)

- Cramer, Billaudelle, Kanya, et al. (incl. Schemmel, Zenke), "Surrogate gradients for analog neuromorphic computing," PNAS 2022 / arXiv:2006.07239, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8794842/, https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2006.07239.
- This is **not** ANN-to-SNN rate-coded conversion — it is direct surrogate-gradient (spike-timing-based) training with hardware in the loop, explicitly contrasted against rate coding: "previous work relied on rate-based or time-to-first-spike coding schemes. Here, we expanded ITL techniques into the realm of surrogate gradient learning, which flexibly interpolates between rate- and timing-based coding schemes" [Discussion, same source].
- Measured results on physical BSS-2 (MNIST, 256-246-10 topology): 97.6% accuracy, 2.4 µJ per classified image, throughput up to 85,000 classifications/second, latency to decision ~8 µs, full-chip power ~200 mW [Table/Results, same source].
- Directly relevant to the analog-precision question: "learning self-corrects for device mismatch... During training, our framework automatically corrected for device mismatch and thus abolished the need for explicit calibration" [Abstract/Discussion, same source] — i.e., their solution to analog imprecision is to fold calibration into the learned weights via HITL gradient descent, again sidestepping (not achieving) exact correspondence to an ideal, precisely known neuron model.

**Classification**: E1 (measured latency 8 µs, energy 2.4 µJ/image, throughput 85k/s, accuracy 97.6%/97.2%, on physical BSS-2 silicon) — but for surrogate-gradient-trained spiking networks, not converted ANNs.

### Summary on conversion support

- A documented rate-coding ANN-to-SNN conversion path to BrainScaleS (wafer-scale, 2017) exists and has been run on physical hardware with measured accuracy recovery via HITL training [Schmitt et al. 2017].
- No source found reporting a rate-coded ANN-to-SNN converted **deep CNN** (i.e., beyond a small MLP/shallow classifier) running on BrainScaleS hardware; the CNN-scale demonstrations on BSS-2 use the non-spiking "hagen"/ANN mode instead (MAC-based, ReLU via ADC saturation, not multi-timestep IF spike counting) [Weis et al. arXiv:2006.13177; Pehle et al. 2022 Section 2.2]. NOT FOUND: a published CNN evaluated via genuine rate-coded spike-count IF conversion (multi-timestep firing-rate readout) at ImageNet or CIFAR scale on BrainScaleS silicon.
- The dominant, better-supported strategy in the BrainScaleS literature for deploying trained ANNs/SNNs is **hardware-in-the-loop (HITL) training**, not "convert once, deploy" rate coding: every conversion-adjacent result above (Schmitt et al. 2017 wafer-scale rate-coding case, Weis et al. 2020 ANN-mode CNN case, Cramer et al. 2022 surrogate-gradient case) required HITL fine-tuning to reach acceptable accuracy after transfer to hardware, because static/one-shot transfer degraded accuracy substantially (e.g., 97%→72% before HITL in the 2017 paper; 98.1%→92.1% before HITL in the 2020 paper).

## 4. Analog constraints: device mismatch, calibration, and why exact IF equivalence is unattainable

This section is the crux for framing why the software ANN-to-SNN conversion literature's assumption of exact, noiseless IF dynamics does not transfer to BrainScaleS hardware.

- **Fixed-pattern (manufacturing) mismatch**: "Transistor-level mismatch in the manufacturing process of an ASIC leads to inhomogeneous electrical properties of the fabricated circuits. Due to the analog nature of BrainScaleS-2, the resulting fixed-pattern variations cause each neuron and synapse to behave differently when presented with similar input" [Weis et al., arXiv:2006.13177]. Before calibration, synaptic input amplitudes differed by up to a factor of 4 across neurons, "interpreted as a reduction of the effective weight resolution from 6 bit to only 4 bit" [same source] — mismatch directly erodes the nominal weight precision.
- **Trial-to-trial (temporal) variation, on top of fixed-pattern mismatch**: on BrainScaleS-1, "the remaining variability is mostly caused by the trial-to-trial variation of the analog parameter storage... Despite the overall match [to a NEST software simulation with identical parameters and input], it can be seen that the calibration is not perfect" [Schmitt et al. 2017, Section II-C]. On BSS-2: "Due to its analog nature, the BrainScaleS-2 ASIC provides slightly different values for each call" even with identical inputs, weights, and calibration [BrainScaleS-2 tutorial, tp_01-properties.html]. The heiDOK thesis on BrainScaleS-1 device variability states plainly: "The variation of the obtained parameters from trial to trial are significant and set the limit for the reachable precision" [https://archiv.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/volltextserver/22742/].
- **Calibration is necessary but explicitly incomplete**: "In order to reduce the mismatch between different neurons and synapses the BrainScaleS-2 system can be calibrated" via the `calix` library, itself an iterative multi-minute process ("generating a calibration takes around five minutes") [BrainScaleS-2 documentation, Calibration page, https://electronicvisions.github.io/documentation-brainscales2/latest/pynn-brainscales/calibration.html]. Even so, Pehle et al. state generically that analog systems "can usually suffer from temporal noise, fixed-pattern parameter deviations, and a divergence from the original model equations" [Pehle et al. 2022, Section 3] — i.e., calibration reduces but does not eliminate deviation from the target model equations (including the IF/LIF equations assumed by conversion theory).
- **Decalibration/robustness experiment quantifying the gap**: Weis et al. deliberately detuned calibration (interpolating toward the uncalibrated median-parameter state) and showed accuracy collapses without retraining, but "within [a] single epoch, the network adapted to the changed parametrization of the substrate... After one epoch, a test accuracy of 96.07% was restored... representing only a slight drop down from 96.31% for the calibrated network" [arXiv:2006.13177]. This demonstrates that HITL learning — not calibration alone, and certainly not an assumption of exact IF correspondence — is the mechanism that makes deployment work.
- **Explicit conceptual acknowledgment that exact model correspondence is not achievable, only approximable via gradient-based self-correction**: "learning self-corrects for device mismatch... abolished the need for explicit calibration" [Cramer et al. 2022]. The framing throughout the BrainScaleS literature is never "the hardware exactly implements equation X" but rather "we adjust software-side weights/gradients so behavior approximately matches equation X, and any residual mismatch is treated as a form of noise the network must be trained to tolerate or exploit as a regularizer" — e.g., Cramer et al. speculate residual analog noise even acts as a beneficial regularizer, improving generalization on a speech benchmark [arXiv:2006.07239, Results/Discussion].
- **Why this matters for conversion theory specifically**: ANN-to-SNN rate-coding conversion theory (QCFS-style and earlier ReLU-to-IF proofs) relies on an IF neuron's firing rate over T timesteps converging deterministically and monotonically to a quantized/clipped version of the ReLU pre-activation, driven by *exact*, noiseless, deterministic integrate-and-fire dynamics with reset-by-subtraction. On BrainScaleS hardware, none of the three components of that argument hold exactly: (i) the "integration" is a physical analog RC-type process subject to unremovable trial-to-trial noise and continuous-valued fixed-pattern deviation, not an exact discrete accumulator; (ii) the "reset" is itself imprecisely calibrated (7% precision on synaptic strength calibration alone, per Weis et al.; membrane reset/threshold values have their own calibration error); (iii) achieving usable accuracy in practice required hardware-in-the-loop gradient correction rather than relying on the conversion mapping being exact. No source claims bit-exact or even tightly-bounded numerical equivalence between the BrainScaleS analog IF/LIF circuit and a software IF model; all sources instead report calibration + HITL as the practical countermeasure to persistent, irreducible analog deviation.

**Classification**: These are architectural/measurement facts about physical silicon (E1-level evidence for the *existence and magnitude* of mismatch/noise), used here to support a conceptual (not itself an experimental) claim about conversion theory's assumptions.

## 5. Availability: EBRAINS access, Heidelberg, purchasability

- **Location**: Both BrainScaleS-1 (wafer-scale) and BrainScaleS-2 (single/multi-chip) systems are physically hosted in Heidelberg, Germany, developed and operated by the Electronic Vision(s) group at the Kirchhoff-Institute for Physics, Heidelberg University [https://brainscales.kip.uni-heidelberg.de/; EBRAINS platform page https://ebrains.eu/data-tools-services/tools/brainscales].
- **Access mechanism**: Remote access via the **EBRAINS** research infrastructure (successor to the EU Human Brain Project's Neuromorphic Computing Platform). "EBRAINS hosts Europe's most powerful large-scale neuromorphic computing systems BrainScaleS and SpiNNaker. Scientists can access these systems remotely via the EBRAINS infrastructure" [https://ebrains.eu/data-tools-services/tools/brainscales]. "Free, remote access to a fast, powerful, programmable, brain-inspired computing device... Enables high-speed, low-energy simulations or emulations of simplified spiking neural networks with synaptic plasticity" [same source].
- **Cost**: "Remote access to fast, powerful, programmable, brain-inspired computing devices (**free of charge for evaluation and basic research**)" [https://ebrains.eu/data-tools-services/computing-infrastructure/neuromorphic-computing]. Test access explicitly "does not need a project description" and is granted after creating a free EBRAINS account and emailing neuromorphic@humanbrainproject.eu to have a "Collab" (workspace) created; larger "production run" quota requires an additional quota request via a Job Manager tool inside the user's Collab [HBP Wiki, "Getting access," https://wiki.ebrains.eu/bin/view/Collabs/neuromorphic/Getting%20access/]. **The system is not sold/purchasable** — access is exclusively as a shared remote research-infrastructure service; no source found describing commercial sale of a BrainScaleS unit (contrast with DYNAP-SE/SynSense, which is a commercial hardware vendor — see Section 6).
- **Interface for access**: Interactive access (BrainScaleS-2 only) and batch access (both BSS-1 and BSS-2) via Jupyter notebooks in the EBRAINS Collaboratory Lab, using PyNN (a simulator-independent Python API for describing spiking neural network models, usable identically against NEST/software simulators or BrainScaleS/SpiNNaker hardware) or lower-level hxtorch/hxsnn interfaces [https://wiki.ebrains.eu/bin/view/Collabs/neuromorphic/BrainScaleS/; PyNN reference already appears in the original Schemmel et al. 2010 paper as "a unified neural system description language, called PyNN, that allows the scientist to describe a model and execute it in a transparent fashion on either a neuromorphic hardware system or a numerical simulator"].
- **Operational scale/robustness**: an EBRAINS integration report describes health checks every 30 minutes, SLURM-based fair-share job scheduling and queuing for hardware access, and continuous integration testing of the notebook-based user workflow end-to-end [Müller et al., "The BrainScaleS-2 Neuromorphic Platform — A Report on the Integration and Operation of an Open Science Hardware Platform within EBRAINS," Zenodo, 2023, https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.8375522].

**Classification**: N/A (availability/administrative fact, not an experimental result), but well-documented (multiple institutional/EBRAINS sources).

## 6. Other analog/mixed-signal platforms

### DYNAP-SE / DYNAP-SE2 (SynSense / INI Zurich)

- DYNAP-SE1: "4 chips and each chip contains 4 cores featuring 256 silicon neurons... 64 synapses to receive the incoming connections" [Samna documentation, https://synsense-sys-int.gitlab.io/samna/0.47.1/devkits/dynapSeSeries/dynapse1/contents/intro.html]. Neurons are AdExpIF (adaptive exponential I&F), fully analog, subthreshold-operated: "1024 AdExpIF... analog ultra-low-power spiking neurons... Transistors of the neural cores operate in the subthreshold region, which results in power consumption of about one-thousandth to one-millionth of the state-of-the-art digital neuromorphic chips, below mW" [Rockpool documentation, https://rockpool.ai/devices/DynapSE/dynapse-overview.html].
- DYNAP-SE2 successor: Richter, Wu, Whatley, Köstinger, Nielsen, Qiao, Indiveri, "DYNAP-SE2: a scalable multi-core dynamic neuromorphic asynchronous spiking neural network processor," 2024, https://doi.org/10.1088/2634-4386/ad1cd7 (arXiv:2310.00564). "Computation is centered on the 1024, analog, integrate-and-fire neurons arranged in 2×2 cores of grids of 16×16 neurons each. Each neuron has 64 synapses and four dendritic branches... the neurons do not receive analog current injection directly, and the membrane potential is also not accessible" [same source] — an explicit architectural point: **unlike BrainScaleS, DYNAP-SE(2) provides no direct analog membrane readout**; only digital spike output is accessible, "because there is no easy way to access thousands of analog values at the same time" [same source]. 4-bit configurable synaptic weight [Table 1, same source].
- **Simulator explicitly does not match hardware exactly**: the companion software simulator, DynapSim, "does not simulate the hardware numerically precisely but executes a fast and an approximate simulation... In principle, the device and the simulator do not react exactly the same to the same input" [Rockpool documentation, https://rockpool.ai/devices/DynapSE/dynapse-overview.html]. This is a direct, explicit statement (from the platform's own documentation) that exact numerical equivalence between a software IF/AdEx model and the physical DYNAP-SE circuit is not claimed or achieved.
- **Rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion feasibility on DYNAP-SE**: NOT FOUND — no source located reporting a trained-ANN-to-DYNAP-SE rate-coding conversion pipeline or a converted CNN benchmark result on this hardware. Found literature on DYNAP-SE instead concerns native spiking feature detection via device-mismatch-exploiting dynamic synapses [Synaptic Integration of Spatiotemporal Features paper, https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1392851/FULLTEXT01.pdf], i.e., research on this platform is oriented toward exploiting analog temporal dynamics/mismatch directly rather than approximating rate-coded ANN inference.

**Classification: E4** (well-documented hardware model/architecture with an explicit approximate, non-hardware-exact simulator) for the platform generally; **NOT FOUND** for rate-coded ANN conversion specifically (no E1–E3 evidence located).

### Neurogrid (Stanford, Boahen group)

- Benjamin, Gao, McQuinn, Choudhary, Chandrasekaran, Bussat, Alvarez-Icaza, Arthur, Merolla, Boahen, "Neurogrid: A Mixed-Analog-Digital Multichip System for Large-Scale Neural Simulations," Proc. IEEE, 2014, https://doi.org/10.1109/jproc.2014.2313565 (PDF: https://web.stanford.edu/group/brainsinsilicon/documents/BenjaminEtAlNeurogrid2014.pdf).
- Architecture: 16 "Neurocore" chips, each with a 256×256 silicon-neuron array; "we emulated all neural elements except the soma with shared electronic circuits... we realized all electronic circuits except those for axonal arbors in an analog manner" [abstract, same source]. Simulates "a million neurons with billions of synaptic connections in real time... using 16 Neurocores integrated on a board that consumes three watts" [abstract, same source]. Interconnected via a digital, asynchronous, event-driven (AER) binary-tree packet network, not a mesh [Section V, same source].
- Neurogrid is explicitly a **biological-real-time** (not accelerated) system, unlike BrainScaleS — used for computational-neuroscience modeling and NEF (Neural Engineering Framework)-style function computation with spiking neurons [Choudhary et al., "Silicon Neurons that Compute," https://web.stanford.edu/group/brainsinsilicon/documents/NEFonNeurogrid.pdf], rather than for accelerated deep-network inference.
- Energy efficiency figure: "about 100,000 times more energy efficient than a personal computer simulation of 1 million neurons" [Stanford News summary of Boahen's work, https://neuroscience.stanford.edu/news/stanford-scientists-create-circuit-board-modeled-human-brain, 2023].
- **Rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion feasibility**: NOT FOUND. No source located reporting a converted-ANN/CNN benchmark (e.g., MNIST/CIFAR classification accuracy) on Neurogrid; published Neurogrid applications found in this search are computational-neuroscience/NEF function-approximation demonstrations, not ANN-to-SNN deployment.

**Classification: E1** for the architecture/energy claims (physical hardware, measured); **NOT FOUND** for rate-coded ANN conversion specifically.

### ROLLS (Reconfigurable On-Line Learning Spiking neuromorphic processor, INI Zurich / Indiveri group)

- Qiao, Mostafa, Corradi, Osswald, Stefanini, Sumislawska, Indiveri, "A reconfigurable on-line learning spiking neuromorphic processor comprising 256 neurons and 128K synapses," Front. Neurosci., 2015, https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2015.00141.
- Architecture: 256 analog adaptive-exponential I&F silicon neurons; 256×256 array of analog **bi-stable** long-term-plasticity (LTP) synapses (spike-based STDP-like learning) plus a separate 256×256 array of short-term-plasticity (STP) synapses; fabricated in 180 nm CMOS, 51.4 mm², ~12.2 million transistors, ~4 mW typical power [abstract and Section 2.1, same source]. Device mismatch between neurons quantified at "9.4% avg" [Open Neuromorphic summary, https://open-neuromorphic.org/neuromorphic-computing/hardware/rolls-ini/].
- On-chip learning is a first-class design goal (unlike DYNAP-SE, which has only programmable weights, not on-chip plastic learning circuits): the paper demonstrates, "for the first time, both computational neuroscience models of attractor networks and image classification neural networks implemented exclusively on custom mixed-signal analog-digital neuromorphic hardware, with no extra pre- or post-processing done in software" [Qiao et al. 2015, Introduction].
- Application demonstrated: real-time image classification using a DVS (event camera) feeding a two-layer spiking network (128 hidden + 128 output VLSI neurons) trained via a supervised on-chip learning protocol, not via ANN-to-SNN conversion [Section 3, same source].
- **Rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion feasibility**: NOT FOUND. ROLLS's published use cases are on-chip supervised/plasticity-based learning and attractor-network modeling, not conversion of a pretrained software ANN.

**Classification: E1** for the architecture/power/mismatch figures (physical, measured chip); **NOT FOUND** for rate-coded ANN conversion.

### Memristor-based / analog in-memory-computing SNN accelerators (2023–2025)

Several recent, fabricated (not merely simulated) memristor-based SNN chips were found; none report rate-coded ANN-to-SNN conversion as their training/deployment paradigm — the opposite is generally true, they explicitly favor direct/native spike-based (often temporal, not rate) training over conversion:

- Li, Zhu, Wang, Du, Mao, Xiao, et al., "Fully Integrated Memristive Spiking Neural Network with Analog Neurons for High-Speed Event-Based Data Processing," 2025, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.04960. A 128×24 Ta/TaOx/Pt 1T1M memristor crossbar integrated with custom analog CMOS spike-response-model (SRM) neurons, fabricated in 180 nm CMOS. **Explicitly rejects rate coding in favor of direct temporal-stream processing**: "Operating solely on asynchronous input events, our system performs direct temporal stream processing that fully exploits the temporal dynamics within input streams without relying on rate-based artificial neural coding schemes." On ANN-to-SNN conversion specifically, the paper states: "ANN-to-SNN conversion... can yield high accuracy, [but] to accurately approximate the continuous activations of an ANN, the converted SNN has traditionally required long inference times (many time steps), which diminishes the latency and energy advantages inherent to spiking computation... This makes conversion suboptimal for tasks where information is encoded in the precise timing of individual spikes" [same source] — a direct, explicit rejection of the rate-coding-conversion paradigm by fabricated-chip authors, on latency/energy grounds. Measured (not simulated) result: 93.06% accuracy on DVS128 Gesture, 44.31 µs/sample, 101.05 TSOPS/W energy efficiency, on physical fabricated silicon [same source].
- "Wafer-scale fabrication of memristive passive crossbar circuits for brain-scale neuromorphic computing," Nature Communications, 2025, https://preview-www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-63831-2. Fabricated on a full 4-inch wafer (>95% device yield), CMOS-compatible passive crossbar; demonstrated an **ex-situ-trained** (software-trained, then weights written to the crossbar) multilayer SNN with surrogate-gradient learning and latency encoding (not rate coding) on Fashion-MNIST, achieving 85.12% measured hardware accuracy vs. 86.26% software baseline, with weight quantization down to 4-bit sufficient [same source]. This is an ANN-trained-then-deployed pathway, but with **latency encoding**, not rate-coded spike counting — distinct from the QCFS/rate-coding conversion family.
- Nowshin & Yi, "Memristor-based Deep Spiking Neural Network with a Computing-In-Memory Architecture," and follow-up "Towards Energy-Efficient Spiking Neural Networks: A Robust Hybrid CMOS-Memristive Accelerator" [https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10354395; https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10554653]. Fabricated memristor crossbar (Cu/TaOx/Rh/Cr, 4% cycle-to-cycle variation), using **inter-spike-interval (ISI) and time-to-first-spike (TTFS) temporal encoding** rather than rate coding, explicitly because "Temporal encoding is more energy efficient compared to rate encoding because of the fewer spike events generated." Measured MNIST classification: 2.9 mW power, 2 µs/image, 2.51 pJ/synaptic-connection.
- Pallathuvalappil & James, "Rate Coding With 3D Memristor Crossbar," IEEE TCAS-AI, 2025, https://doi.org/10.1109/tcasai.2025.3532406. This paper **does** use rate coding, but only for the **output spike-counting circuitry** (an analog memristor-based counter replacing digital clocked counters for tallying output spikes in an SNN's rate-coded readout layer), not for ANN-to-SNN conversion of the compute pipeline itself; evaluated on the Skywater 130 open-source PDK (simulation/PDK-based, not confirmed as a fabricated chip in the retrieved abstract).

**Classification**: E1 for the two fully fabricated/measured chips (Li et al. 2025 memristive SRM-neuron chip; Nature Comms 2025 wafer-scale passive crossbar) — both are physical silicon with measured accuracy, and the former also has measured latency and energy. **However, neither uses rate-coded IF conversion**: the Li et al. chip explicitly argues against rate coding, and the Nature Comms crossbar uses latency (time-to-first-spike-adjacent) encoding, not rate/spike-count coding. NOT FOUND: a fabricated memristor or analog IMC chip demonstrating genuine rate-coded (spike-count) ANN-to-SNN conversion of a CNN.
