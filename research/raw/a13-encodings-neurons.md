## PART A — SPIKE ENCODINGS

### A.1 Rate / count coding

**Definition.** The number of spikes emitted by a neuron over a window of $T$ timesteps represents a scalar value. Two variants dominate the ANN-to-SNN conversion literature:

- **Poisson rate coding.** Each input pixel intensity is mapped to a firing probability per timestep; spike times are drawn from a Poisson process with that rate. This is the "classical" encoding used by Diehl et al. (2015) and early conversion work [Guo et al. 2021, *Front. Neurosci.*, https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2021.638474].
- **Deterministic count coding ("constant coding").** A constant input current (equal to the pixel intensity scaled by threshold) is integrated deterministically; the resulting spike count over $T$ steps is a floor/round of the analog value. Rueckauer & Liu (2016/2017) explicitly show that replacing Poisson input with constant analog current to the first layer reduces variance and improves accuracy substantially — CIFAR-10 error dropped from 40.18% (Poisson, weight-normalized) to 16.40% (constant current + reset-by-subtraction) [Rueckauer et al. 2017, *Front. Neurosci.*, https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2017.00682; also PMC5770641].

**A count over $T$ steps is a uniform quantization grid by construction.** With a fixed threshold $V_{thr}$ (equivalently, a fixed maximum firing rate $r_{max}=1/\Delta t$), the achievable spike counts are $\{0,1,\dots,T\}$, so the achievable firing-rate levels are equally spaced at $1/T$ intervals — an inherently uniform $T$-level quantizer. This is the direct mathematical link to the thesis's "QCFS is standard uniform QAT" claim: rate/count coding under reset-by-subtraction realizes exactly the same uniform staircase as a $T$-level uniform quantizer.

**Timesteps for precision.** Quantization error under deterministic count coding scales as $1/T$; under Poisson coding, variance instead scales as $1/\sqrt{T}$ [Auge et al., *A Survey of Encoding Techniques for Signal Processing in SNNs*, Springer, https://doi.org/10.1007/s11063-021-10562-2]. Poisson coding therefore needs quadratically more timesteps than deterministic/count coding for equivalent precision — one reason essentially all modern low-latency conversion work abandons Poisson input in favor of constant-current (Part A.7) or deterministic rate coding.

**Decoder.** Output-layer spike count divided by $T$ (or, for the final layer, the accumulated pre-threshold membrane potential is read out directly without further spiking — used by several conversion frameworks, e.g. Jiang et al. 2023 [https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/jiang23a/jiang23a.pdf]).

**Robustness.** Rate coding (specifically the Poisson variant) is more robust to adversarial perturbation than direct/analog coding because the Poisson spike generator is non-differentiable and stochastic, degrading attacker gradient estimates by ~20 percentage points of adversarial accuracy in one controlled comparison [Kim et al. 2022, "Rate Coding or Direct Coding," ICASSP, https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2202.03133]. Deterministic count coding does not inherit this stochastic-gradient-masking benefit since it is itself close to a deterministic floor function.

**Hardware.** Any hardware that can integrate a binary spike stream and count/threshold it supports rate coding — this includes essentially every listed platform (Loihi 1/2, SpiNNaker 1/2, DYNAP-CNN/Speck, BrainScaleS-2, TrueNorth, FPGA). TrueNorth was explicitly noted to rely primarily on a rate/count spike-encoding scheme "where information is represented by spike counts within fixed time windows," chosen specifically to simplify hardware implementation [Liu, Zheng, Yi, *Neural Encoding Strategies for Neuromorphic Computing*, MDPI Electronics 2026, 15:1221].

### A.2 TTFS / time-to-first-spike / latency coding

**Definition.** Each neuron fires at most once; the *timing* of that single spike (relative to a reference onset) encodes the value, typically with an inverse relationship (larger value → earlier spike). Foundational deep-SNN papers: Rueckauer & Liu (2018, ISCAS) ["Conversion of analog to spiking neural networks using sparse temporal coding"]; Zhang et al. (2020); Park et al. 2020 "T2FSNN: Deep Spiking Neural Networks with Time-to-First-Spike Coding" [arXiv:2003.11741]; Han & Roy "Deep SNN with TTFS coding" style frameworks (TDSNN, reverse coding).

**Precision per spike.** In principle a single spike's timing can carry arbitrary precision (real-valued latency), giving TTFS the theoretical minimum spike count (one spike per neuron, many silent). T2FSNN reports reducing spike count to <1% and latency to 22% of burst coding's cost on CIFAR-100 [arXiv:2003.11741]. However, once deployed on real hardware, firing times must be quantized to discrete timesteps, which reintroduces a quantization floor — "A Latency Coding Framework for Deep SNNs" notes floating-point firing times are "not natively supported by existing neuromorphic hardware, which is designed to communicate discrete binary spikes," and that quantizing them "inevitably downgrades performance" [arXiv:2603.23206].

**Why more efficient but harder to train.** Efficiency: sparsity is extreme (each neuron fires 0 or 1 times total), and "idle neurons do not consume much" energy on event-driven hardware [Frontiers in Neuroscience 2022, https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2022.971937, discriminability framework for TTFS coding schemes (ROC/NoM/R-NoM)]. Training difficulty: the single-spike constraint means most of the network's expressive information must be squeezed into precise spike timing rather than spike count, so gradients are extremely sparse and the "quantization errors accumulate across layers," degrading deep-network accuracy relative to shallow demonstrations [You Only Spike Once, arXiv:2006.09982]. Guo et al. (2021) report TTFS achieves the fastest *inference* convergence of all coding schemes tested but the *highest* effective training latency, because gradient-based training needs long time windows to get useful spike-time gradients [https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2021.638474]. Recent work (ETTFS, arXiv:2410.23619v3) addresses this via parameter initialization, temporal-weighting decoders, and relaxing the strict single-spike constraint in hidden layers to preserve gradient flow.

**Decoder.** Typically the class with the earliest first spike (winner-take-all over first-spike-time), or a learned temporal-weighting decoder that aggregates decayed contributions across timesteps favoring early spikes [ETTFS, arXiv:2410.23619v3].

**Hardware.** TTFS is explicitly framed as well suited to event-driven, address-event-representation (AER) neuromorphic chips because "time represents itself" without needing explicit timestamps [Frontiers 2022, https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2022.971937, citing implementations/discussions on Loihi-class and SpiNNaker-class hardware]. Deployed demonstrations exist on SpiNNaker2 (an EventProp-based TTFS loss/backprop implementation transmits spikes at computed times and error signals during backward pass; "Time-to-First-Spike Loss Program" is explicitly named in the SpiNNaker2 event-based-backprop paper) [arXiv:2412.15021]. No explicit confirmation was found of TTFS support as a first-class primitive on DYNAP-CNN/Speck, BrainScaleS-2, or TrueNorth — these platforms can host TTFS-coded networks (any single-spike-per-neuron IF/LIF-based net runs on any spiking substrate) but none of the reviewed sources describe a *dedicated* TTFS-decoding mechanism onboard; **unknown / not specifically documented** for those platforms.

### A.3 Phase coding / weighted spikes

**Definition.** Kim, Kim, Huh, Lee, Choi (2018), "Deep neural networks with weighted spikes," *Neurocomputing* 311:373–386 [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neucom.2018.05.087]. Spikes are assigned a weight depending on which "phase" (position within a repeating cycle of $K$ timesteps) they occur in, typically weights $2^{K-1}, 2^{K-2}, \dots, 2^0$ (powers of two), analogous to a binary place-value code. This lets a single spike in an early phase carry as much information as many unweighted spikes, cutting classification latency versus pure rate/Poisson coding.

**Non-uniform effective grid — flagged as directly relevant to the thesis.** Because the phase weights are powers of two, the representable output values are sums of a subset of $\{2^{K-1},\dots,2^0\}$ — i.e., phase coding produces a genuinely *non-uniform* (binary, exponentially-weighted) effective quantization grid, in contrast to rate/count coding's uniform $1/T$-spaced grid (Part A.1). This distinction matters directly for the thesis's mixed-precision / QCFS-is-uniform-QAT argument: phase coding is a spike-domain analogue of non-uniform (e.g., logarithmic or floating-point-like) quantization, not of the uniform integer quantization that QCFS/rate coding implement. Downstream limitation noted in follow-up work: "the transmission amount of information is bounded by the global phase, which causes inefficiency in hidden layers, resulting in a latency of up to three thousand steps for a 32-layer network" [Stepwise Weighted Spike Coding, arXiv:2408.17245v1, citing Kim et al. 2018]. Bistable SNN work (BSNN) also combines phase coding with a bistability mechanism and reports phase coding alone requires ~3000 simulation steps on CIFAR-100 [Frontiers in Neuroscience 2022, https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2022.991851].

**Hardware.** No dedicated hardware primitive for phase coding was found in the reviewed sources; it is implemented purely as a software/training-time convention on top of standard binary-spike substrates — any platform supporting precise timestep-indexed binary spikes (all reviewed platforms) can in principle host a phase-coded network, but none advertise native phase-decoding hardware. **Unknown / no dedicated hardware support identified** on any of the seven listed platforms.

### A.4 Burst coding

**Definition.** A neuron may emit *multiple* spikes within (or between) a single simulation timestep — a "burst" — when the residual/excess membrane potential after threshold crossing is large, rather than emitting at most one spike per step. Two representative papers:
- Li & Zeng (2022), "Efficient and Accurate Conversion of Spiking Neural Network with Burst Spikes," IJCAI [https://doi.org/10.24963/ijcai.2022/345; arXiv:2204.13271]. Burst spikes release residual membrane information "between simulation steps," limited to a maximum burst count $\Gamma$ to bound interference. Combined with Lateral Inhibition Pooling (LIPooling) as a MaxPool replacement, achieves near-lossless VGG16/ImageNet conversion in <100 timesteps (down from typical >1000).
- Park et al. (2019) burst-coding results are cited as the prior state of the art that T2FSNN surpasses [arXiv:2003.11741].

**Decoder / precision.** A burst-coded neuron effectively packs several "unweighted rate units" of information into a single simulation step, increasing the information rate per step at the cost of within-step spike multiplicity — a middle ground between plain rate coding (1 spike/step max) and TTFS (1 spike total). Guo et al. (2021) note burst coding has the fastest *training* convergence of the schemes they tested [https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2021.638474].

**Hardware.** Burst coding requires a neuron model capable of emitting more than one spike in a single timestep, or of emitting spikes at sub-timestep granularity. Speck/DYNAP-CNN explicitly documents an "M-IF" (multi-spike IF) mode where, "if the membrane potential exceeds 2× pre-defined threshold, the neuron will still fire only once" under standard IF but a distinct multi-spike variant can fire $\lfloor U/u_{th}\rfloor$ spikes per step ("M-Heaviside" function) [Nature Communications 2024, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-47811-6, Speck neuron dynamics]. This is functionally a burst/multi-spike primitive, though the same source notes the deployed dynamic-SNN work on Speck actually used plain IF, not M-IF. No burst-specific primitive was identified for Loihi 1/2, SpiNNaker, BrainScaleS-2, or TrueNorth in the reviewed sources — **unknown** for those platforms, though any clock-driven digital neuromorphic core (FPGA, SpiNNaker's software-defined neurons) could implement it in principle since the neuron update function is user-programmable (Part B.2 below).

### A.5 Population coding

**Definition.** A value is represented jointly by the activity pattern across a *population* of neurons rather than by a single neuron's rate or timing — e.g., each neuron has a tuning curve over the represented variable, and the ensemble response (vector of firing rates, or the identity/rank of which neurons fire) decodes the value. Auge et al. formally note population coding is often treated as a third top-level category but "can be the case in both temporal and rate codes and does not provide a unique differentiation" — i.e., it is an orthogonal axis (spatial multiplexing) rather than a fourth encoding primitive alongside rate/temporal [https://doi.org/10.1007/s11063-021-10562-2].

**Robustness / capacity trade-offs.** A detailed review of population coding constraints for neuromorphic circuit design [Constraints on the design of neuromorphic circuits set by the properties of neural population codes, IOPscience 2023, https://doi.org/10.1088/2634-4386/acaf9c] discusses sparseness, heterogeneity, correlation structure, and multiple encoding timescales as first-order design constraints; heterogeneous tuning across a population reduces the information-limiting effect of correlated noise and generally increases the encoded information relative to a homogeneous population with the same average statistics.

**Hardware.** Population coding is a network-topology convention (replicate $N$ neurons per represented variable), not a hardware feature per se — it is supported wherever a network can be instantiated with multiple neurons per logical unit, i.e. on all seven listed platforms, subject only to neuron-count/memory budget. No platform-specific "population decoder" hardware primitive was identified. snnTorch documents an explicit population-coding tutorial as a standard software pattern [https://snntorch.readthedocs.io/].

### A.6 Differential / sigma-delta / delta modulation (graded spikes)

**Definition.** Sigma-delta neural networks (SDNNs) wrap a conventional (e.g. ReLU) activation with a *delta encoder* on output and a *sigma* (running-sum) decoder on input: the delta encoder transmits a spike carrying the *change* in activation since the last transmitted value, only when that change exceeds a threshold $\vartheta$; the sigma unit on the receiving end accumulates these deltas to reconstruct the signal [Shrestha et al. 2023, "Efficient Video and Audio Processing with Loihi 2," arXiv:2310.03251, Eq. 1–2; Lava tutorial notebook, https://github.com/lava-nc/lava/blob/main/tutorials/in_depth/tutorial10_sigma_delta_neurons.ipynb]. This exploits *temporal* redundancy (static or slowly changing input → few/no spikes) in addition to the usual spatial/activation sparsity.

**Graded (payload-carrying) spikes on Loihi 2.** Loihi 2 supports "graded spikes" carrying up to 24 bits of signed integer magnitude in the spike message itself (vs. binary spikes on Loihi 1) [arXiv:2310.03251, arXiv:2505.06417v2]. This is the hardware feature that makes sigma-delta / graded-value conversion practical: rather than encoding a quantized activation as $T$ binary rate spikes, a single graded spike directly carries the (quantized) activation delta. A concrete ANN→SDNN conversion pipeline on Loihi 2 (YOLO-KP object detector) reports 112 FPS, 5.0 ms latency, 20 mJ/frame in a fielded demo, with weights/activations quantized to match Loihi 2's fixed-point microcode (rescaling restricted to power-of-two bit-shifts) [Sigma-Delta Neural Network Conversion on Loihi 2, https://doi.org/10.1145/3822454.3822490; arXiv:2505.06417v2]. Lava-DL's SLAYER library explicitly supports "graded spikes, which are amenable to backpropagation" and provides `slayer.block.sigma_delta.*` layer types alongside CUBA, ALIF, and resonate-and-fire (RF) blocks [https://lava-nc.org/lava-lib-dl/slayer/slayer.html].

**Related: adaptive-LIF / sigma-delta on Loihi (RNN mapping).** Boeshertz et al. (2024) map an aLIF/$\Sigma\Delta$-neuron (four state variables, requiring **two compartments on Loihi 1**) into a low-pass RNN architecture, setting a new state of the art for on-chip speech classification on Loihi with 3-bit quantization-aware trained weights [arXiv:2407.13534].

**Hardware.** Native graded-spike / sigma-delta support is confirmed specifically for **Loihi 2** (24-bit signed integer payload; explicit microcode support) [arXiv:2310.03251, arXiv:2505.06417v2]. **Loihi 1** lacks native graded spikes but can approximate the mechanism via multi-compartment aLIF/$\Sigma\Delta$ neurons at a 2× compartment cost [arXiv:2407.13534]. **SpiNNaker 2** supports large (128-bit) packet payloads and its event-based-backprop demo explicitly transmits "signed floating point values" as error-signal payloads in the backward pass, distinct from ordinary binary forward spikes — this indicates SpiNNaker2's software-defined packet system can carry graded/signed payloads, though this was demonstrated for gradients, not (yet, in the reviewed sources) for a sigma-delta forward-pass encoding [arXiv:2412.15021]. No evidence of native graded-spike or sigma-delta hardware support was found for SpiNNaker 1, DYNAP-CNN/Speck, BrainScaleS-2, TrueNorth, or FPGA in general (FPGA is a "roll your own" platform, so graded-spike support is achievable but is a design choice, not an off-the-shelf feature) — marked **unknown / not confirmed** for those.

### A.7 Direct / analog / constant-current input encoding

**Definition.** Real-valued input (e.g., normalized pixel intensities or RGB values) is applied as a **constant analog input current** to the first hidden layer's neurons at every timestep, instead of first converting the input into a spike train. The first layer thus performs a genuine (potentially multi-bit) MAC/convolution on the raw analog input; only the *output* of that first layer is spiking.

**Origin and rationale.** Rueckauer et al. (2017) introduce this explicitly as one of the three mechanisms (alongside reset-by-subtraction and robust weight normalization) that close the ANN–SNN accuracy gap: switching from Poisson spike-train input to constant analog current for the first hidden layer alone improved CIFAR-10 accuracy from ~59.8% (with weight normalization, Poisson input, reset-to-zero) to 83.6% [https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2017.00682, PMC5770641]. "Direct encoding" / "direct input encoding" (used interchangeably) is formalized and popularized for deep networks by Rathi & Roy (2020, hybrid training with learnable membrane leak and threshold) and is now near-universal in low-latency conversion and hybrid/direct-training SNN work [ACE-SNN, https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2022.815258, citing Rathi & Roy 2020].

**What it implies for hardware — the first layer is effectively an ANN layer.** Because the first-layer computation is a real-valued MAC (not an accumulate over binary spikes), it forfeits the accumulate-only, multiplication-free efficiency that the rest of the SNN enjoys. Multiple sources make this explicit:
- "The first layer now requires MACs, as opposed to cheaper accumulates in the remaining layers... the overhead is negligible for deep convolutional architectures" [ACE-SNN, https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2022.815258, citing Rathi & Roy 2020].
- "The direct coded SNN receives analog input in the first layer ($l=1$), thereby incurring MACs without any sparsity" [In-Sensor & Neuromorphic Computing paper, arXiv:2212.10881].
- Energy comparison: on an Eyeriss-like digital platform, rate coding was measured to cost ~50% *less* energy than direct coding specifically because "the first layer of the direct-encoded SNNs has 16-bit data precision and thus[,] incurs greater computational costs" [Kim et al. 2022, arXiv:2202.03133].
- ACE-SNN and the in-sensor computing paper both propose dedicated processing-in-memory (PIM) / in-sensor analog-compute hardware specifically to absorb this first-layer MAC cost efficiently (e.g., re-purposing image-sensor correlated double sampling circuits to perform signed first-layer convolution directly in the analog domain) [https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2022.815258; arXiv:2212.10881].

Rueckauer et al.'s original framing: this only remains exactly valid because pixel intensities are *constant* over the simulation window, which is the one case (besides the very first layer) where the "constant input current" assumption used to derive the reset-by-subtraction unbiasedness proof exactly holds [https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2017.00682, Sec. 2.2.4 "Analog Input to First Hidden Layer"].

**Hardware.** Direct/analog encoding is not really a hardware *feature* to be supported or not — any platform that allows a first layer's synaptic input to be a fixed (non-spiking) integer/fixed-point value at every timestep can implement it. This was confirmed explicitly for: **Loihi** (NxTF/SNN-TB toolchains parse ANN weights into the first Loihi layer with a documented non-spiking-adjacent path) [arXiv:2101.04261]; **Nengo-Loihi**, where the input-encoding layer is explicitly configured to run *off-chip* rather than on Loihi's spiking cores ("this layer should run off-chip") [nengo-loihi Keras-to-Loihi docs]; and general digital SNN accelerators (FPGA-class), where a direct-encoded first layer is simply implemented as a conventional MAC array preceding the spiking core [Spiker+, arXiv:2401.01141v1, though this specific detail concerns generic LIF/IF hardware rather than direct encoding per se]. No source explicitly states that DYNAP-CNN/Speck, BrainScaleS-2, SpiNNaker, or TrueNorth *cannot* run a direct-encoded first layer; the practical implication is architectural (the first layer needs full MAC precision, which is more naturally executed off-chip or on a co-located conventional accelerator rather than on the pure spiking substrate) — this is a design/deployment convention rather than a hard hardware limitation, and is marked accordingly in Part C.

## PART B — NEURON MODELS

### B.1 IF (integrate-and-fire), nonleaky

**Dynamics.** $V(t) = V(t-1) + z(t)$; spike when $V(t) \ge V_{thr}$; no decay term. This is the canonical neuron of the ANN-SNN conversion literature because, under reset-by-subtraction and constant/rate input, its time-averaged firing rate is an *unbiased estimator* of the ReLU activation it was converted from [Cao et al. 2015; Rueckauer et al. 2017, https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2017.00682]. Used by essentially every conversion paper cited above (Bu et al. QCFS, Li & Zeng burst, Jiang et al. unified framework, etc.).

**Hardware.** Confirmed native IF support (with reset-by-subtraction option) on **DYNAP-CNN/Speck** ("the compute-in-memory-controller block model an Integrate and Fire (IF) neuron with a linear leak... the neuron state variable has a threshold subtraction or reset written back" — leak can be set to zero to recover pure IF) [Nature Comms 2024, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-47811-6; SynSense Speck datasheet]. IF is the default neuron simulated in the Rueckauer/Loihi NxTF pipeline (single-compartment for hard reset) [arXiv:2101.04261]. FPGA frameworks (Spiker+) support IF as one of the six built-in neuron variants [arXiv:2401.01141v1].

### B.2 Reset by subtraction (soft reset) vs. reset to zero (hard reset)

**Why conversion requires soft reset.** Rueckauer et al. (2016/2017) derive analytically that under reset-to-zero, a constant residual charge $\epsilon = n\cdot z - V_{thr}$ (the amount by which the membrane exceeded threshold before reset) is *discarded* at every reset event; this residual is not merely noise but a systematic, input-dependent bias that "does not go away simply with longer simulation time" and compounds across layers, degrading deep-network accuracy [https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2017.00682, Eq. 4a/5a; ar5iv:1612.04052]. Reset-by-subtraction instead subtracts exactly $V_{thr}$ from the membrane potential at spike time, carrying the excess charge $\epsilon$ forward into the next integration window; the authors prove the resulting firing-rate estimator converges to the exact target ANN activation as $T\to\infty$, with error due only to discrete sampling (not a persistent bias) [same source, Eq. 4b/5b/6]. Empirically, switching only the reset mechanism (holding Poisson input and normalization fixed) improved CIFAR-10 accuracy from ~40% error to ~20% additional improvement in one ablation [https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2017.00682, Fig. 2 ablation]. This result is now treated as settled practice across the conversion literature: "soft-reset mechanism (also called reset-by-subtraction mechanism) is proposed to solve the information loss problem caused by hard-reset mechanism," citing Rueckauer 2016 and Diehl 2016 [Wang et al. 2023, IJCAI, https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2023/0342.pdf]; "Rueckauer et al. (2017) proposed the 'reset-by-subtraction' mechanism to retain the temporal information, which was also adopted in the following work (Han, Srinivasan, and Roy 2020; Deng and Gu 2021; Bu et al. 2022b)" [AAAI SRP paper, https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/25071/24843].

**Hardware support — native soft reset:**
- **TrueNorth.** The digital neuron model explicitly implements a "linear mode" ($\gamma_j=1$) reset in which "the residual potential above the threshold is not discarded... the neuron fires, and resets to the amount above the threshold" — this is reset-by-subtraction as a first-class, natively configurable hardware reset mode, alongside "normal mode" (hard reset to $R_j$) and a stochastic non-reset mode [Cognitive Computing Building Block: neuron model of TrueNorth, https://viplab.fudan.edu.cn/vip/attachments/download/3248/neuron-model_of_truenorth.pdf].
- **DYNAP-CNN / Speck.** Natively configurable via a `return_to_zero` flag: `False` selects "soft reset" ("subtract the membrane potential by 1× pre-defined ... threshold"), `True` selects hard reset [Speck Dev Kit Manual / Datasheet, SynSense, 2025, Sec. 4.2.4.6.2/3.2.4.6.2].
- **FPGA (general).** Confirmed as a standard, natively-supported configurable option in dedicated SNN-accelerator generator frameworks: Spiker+ supports both "a hard-reset... and a subtractive-reset" as selectable neuron variants for IF, I-order LIF, and II-order LIF, each synthesized to distinct VHDL datapaths [arXiv:2401.01141v1]. A separate hardware-software co-optimized FPGA accelerator (SIA) explicitly implements reset-by-subtraction as its default reset because "this approach has demonstrated better classification accuracy" [arXiv:2410.16298v2].
- **SpiNNaker 1/2.** Not natively hard-wired to either mode — the platform is fully **software-programmable** (ARM cores executing user C code per neuron-update timestep). The neuron-model framework explicitly exposes a `neuron_model_has_spiked()` callback "used to reset the parameters of the neuron after it has spiked," which a user model can implement as either hard or soft reset [SpiNNaker "Creating New Neuron Models" lab manual, spinnakermanchester.github.io]. This makes reset-by-subtraction achievable on SpiNNaker via custom neuron models, at the cost of writing and compiling a bespoke C neuron kernel (it is not one of the built-in sPyNNaker standard cell types such as `IF_curr_exp`, which use PyNN's conventional hard-reset LIF/IF semantics) [sPyNNaker Models and Limitations doc, spinnakermanchester.github.io].

**Hardware support — requires a workaround:**
- **Loihi 1 and Loihi 2.** The native CUBA-LIF hardware neuron hard-resets the membrane voltage to zero on spike: "After a spike occurs, $v(t)$ is reset to 0" [Frontiers in Neuroinformatics 2022, BMTK-Loihi mapping paper, https://doi.org/10.3389/fninf.2022.883360; Brian2Loihi emulator paper, https://doi.org/10.3389/fninf.2022.1015624, "the third term resets the voltage to zero after a spike by subtracting the threshold" — i.e. Loihi's *native* single-compartment reset is hard reset]. To obtain reset-by-subtraction on Loihi, the NxTF compiler exploits Loihi's **multi-compartment** neuron support as an explicit workaround: "In soft reset mode, our cells... consist of two compartments, where one fulfills the role of the soma... The second compartment is recurrently connected to the first and becomes active only when the soma crosses threshold... [it] then inhibits the soma via the recurrent connection with an amount of charge equal to the threshold." The paper explicitly notes the cost: "doubling the number of compartments allocated for the network," and states as a recommendation for future hardware that "a built-in soft reset... would eliminate the need to duplicate neuron compartments" [NxTF, arXiv:2101.04261, Sec. 2.3.6]. This is the two-compartment trick referenced in the research brief.

**Hardware support — unknown / not confirmed in reviewed sources:**
- **BrainScaleS-2.** The native AdEx neuron circuit performs a *hard* reset: "An outgoing spike is released as soon as the membrane potential crosses the hard firing threshold $V_{th}$. In that case, the membrane is clamped to the reset potential $V_r$ and held there for the refractory period $t_r$" [Pehle et al. 2022, Frontiers, https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2022.795876]. No source located describing a reset-by-subtraction mode (native or via multi-compartment workaround) on BrainScaleS-2, despite its documented multi-compartment capability (used there for dendritic/AdEx morphology emulation, not reset-workaround purposes) — marked **unknown**.

### B.3 LIF and CUBA LIF (current-based) on Loihi

**Dynamics.** Standard LIF adds an exponential leak term to the IF membrane update: $V(t)=\alpha V(t-1) + z(t)$, $\alpha=e^{-\Delta t/\tau_V}<1$. The **current-based (CUBA) LIF** variant, used as Loihi's native neuron, separates a first-order synaptic-current state ($u$, with its own decay $\tau_u$) that is itself leaky-integrated, from the membrane-voltage state, giving two decaying state variables: $u(t)=\alpha_u u(t-1) + \text{(weighted spike input)}$, $v(t)=\alpha_v v(t-1) + u(t) + \text{bias}$ [Davies et al. 2018 Loihi paper, as reproduced in https://doi.org/10.3389/fninf.2022.1015624 and https://doi.org/10.3389/fninf.2022.883360]. Loihi's native reset (single compartment) is to zero.

**Hardware.** CUBA LIF is Loihi's baseline/default neuron model ("Most of these SNN applications use a basic leaky integrate and fire (LIF) neuron or some closely-related variant... e.g., as implemented by Loihi 1" [arXiv:2310.03251]); it is also directly exposed as `slayer.block.cuba.*` in Lava-DL [https://lava-nc.org/lava-lib-dl/slayer/slayer.html]. LIF (with leak disabled to give IF) is also the neuron used on Speck [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-47811-6, "the spiking neuron model on Speck is Integrate and Fire (IF), i.e., LIF neuron without leaky operation"]. LIF is the built-in default on SpiNNaker (`IF_curr_exp`/`IF_cond_exp` in sPyNNaker) and is supported as a synthesizable option on FPGA generator frameworks (Spiker+'s I- and II-order LIF).

### B.4 Adaptive / threshold-adaptive neurons, ALIF

**Definition.** The firing threshold (or an equivalent subtractive adaptation current) increases after each spike and decays back toward baseline between spikes, implementing spike-frequency adaptation and (per Bellec et al. 2018/2020) giving recurrent SNNs LSTM-like long-memory capability. Canonical discrete-time form (as implemented in the `sinabs.layers.ALIF` module, following Bellec et al. 2018): $V(t{+}1)=\alpha V(t) + (1-\alpha)\sum w\cdot s(t)$; adaptive threshold $B(t{+}1) = b_0 + \beta\, b(t)$ with $b(t{+}1)=\rho\, b(t) + (1-\rho)s(t)$; reset on $V\ge B$ [sinabs docs, https://sinabs.readthedocs.io/v3.1.3/api/generated/sinabs.layers.ALIF.html]. An equivalent formulation instead subtracts a decaying after-hyperpolarizing (AHP) current from the input rather than raising the threshold; the two are mathematically equivalent ways of changing "the amount of input needed until the threshold is reached" [Boeshertz et al. 2024, arXiv:2407.13534].

**Hardware.** **Loihi** natively supports AHP-current-based adaptive neurons via its **multi-compartment** feature: "The multi-compartment feature of Loihi allows the maintenance of the AHP current within the same neuron... Each of its neuro-core... computes the dynamics of up to 1024 single-compartment (no AHP) or up to 512 2-compartment (with AHP) neurons locally" [A Long Short-Term Memory for AI Applications in Spike-based Neuromorphic Hardware, *Nature Machine Intelligence* 2022, https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-022-00480-w; preprint https://igi-web.tugraz.at/PDF/261.pdf]. Lava-DL exposes ALIF as a first-class block type (`slayer.block.alif.*`) alongside CUBA and sigma-delta [https://lava-nc.org/lava-lib-dl/slayer/slayer.html], and the Boeshertz et al. aLIF/$\Sigma\Delta$-neuron mapping (Part A.6) runs on Loihi 1 using two compartments. **BrainScaleS-2**'s native analog neuron circuit directly implements the full AdEx model, which includes an adaptation current $w$ with spike-triggered increment $b$ and decay $\tau_w$ — i.e., adaptive dynamics are native/analog, not a workaround, on BrainScaleS-2 [Pehle et al. 2022, https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2022.795876; Kaiser et al., arXiv:2209.09280]. **TrueNorth**'s digital neuron model supports configurable "leak modes" (including reversal/divergent leak) and negative thresholds with reset variants, which can approximate adaptation-like dynamics, though this is a differently-structured mechanism than AHP/threshold-adaptive ALIF specifically [TrueNorth neuron model paper, viplab.fudan.edu.cn]. No explicit ALIF/AHP hardware primitive was identified for **SpiNNaker** (though `extra_models.IFCurrExpCa2Adaptive`, a calcium-adaptive current model, is listed as a supported sPyNNaker extra model, indicating adaptation-style dynamics are available as a built-in software neuron type) [sPyNNaker Models and Limitations doc]. **DYNAP-CNN/Speck** and general **FPGA** frameworks reviewed here did not document a native ALIF primitive (FPGA can implement one as custom logic, in principle) — marked **unknown** for those two.

### B.5 Spiking neurons with negative/signed spikes

**Why some conversion methods need them.** Standard IF/LIF neurons only emit non-negative (unipolar) spikes, which cannot directly represent negative activations (a problem for BN-containing networks or Leaky-ReLU-trained ANNs, where a substantial fraction of pre-activation values are negative — one source reports 51% of YOLO neuron outputs are negative [IJCAI 2025, "A Fast and Accurate ANN-SNN Conversion Algorithm with Negative Spikes," https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2025/0719.pdf]) and cannot *correct* an erroneous early over-firing (overshoot) caused by asynchronous spike arrival order in deep SNNs. Representative methods:
- **Signed Neuron with Memory (SNM)**, Wang et al. 2022, IJCAI: adds a "memory" state that tracks the net (positive − negative) spike count so far; a negative spike may only fire if the memory is currently positive, guaranteeing the neuron's *effective* output rate never goes below zero while still allowing negative spikes to correct overshoot from early-arriving positive-weight spikes [https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2022/0347.pdf].
- **Negative-spike dynamics / median-quantization conversion**, Zou et al. 2023, *Advanced Intelligent Systems*: maintains a "shadow membrane potential" alongside the normal one and fires a compensating negative spike whenever the two diverge past a negative threshold $\theta^-$, explicitly to correct over-spiking/under-spiking error accumulation in deep BN-containing nets, reporting truly lossless conversion vs. ANN baselines [https://doi.org/10.1002/aisy.202300383].
- **Dual-threshold neurons**, Liu et al. 2025 (arXiv:2506.01968) and IBT/stReLU-style negative-spike neurons for Leaky-ReLU conversion (IJCAI 2025, https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2025/0719.pdf, citing Kim et al. 2020's imbalanced-threshold (IBT) signed neuron and Han et al. 2023's symmetric-threshold ReLU (stReLU)).

**Hardware feasibility — the key question.**
- **Loihi 2** natively supports **signed** graded-spike payloads: "graded spikes... can hold up to 16 bit signed integer payloads" [arXiv:2505.06417v2] / "up to 24 bits of integer magnitude" [arXiv:2310.03251] — a graded spike can directly encode a negative delta or activation value, which is a strictly more general capability than a "negative spike" as used in the conversion papers above (those papers need only a signed *binary* event, i.e. ±1, which Loihi 2's signed-integer payload trivially subsumes).
- **Loihi 1** has **no native negative-spike or signed-payload support**. A fully on-chip spiking backpropagation implementation on Loihi 1 explicitly works around this: "there are no negative (signed) spikes[,] the local gradient is calculated and propagated back twice for a Hebbian weight update in two phases with different signs... implemented by an excitatory synapse from $t$ and an inhibitory synapse of the same strength from $o$" — i.e., a signed quantity is represented as *two* separate unsigned spike channels (one for the positive part, one for the negative part) rather than truly signed spikes, and the same paper notes "on the new version of the Loihi hardware, Loihi 2, graded spikes were introduced, which could allow an efficient implementation... for non-binary neural activations" [The backpropagation algorithm implemented on spiking neuromorphic hardware, PMC11549378].
- **TrueNorth** supports a **negative threshold** with two configurable behaviors ("floor" or "bounce") for when the membrane potential goes below zero, and its synaptic weights are signed — but the *spike/axon event itself* remains a single-polarity digital pulse in the standard neuron model description reviewed here; the negative-threshold "bounce" mode is used to keep a matched ON/OFF neuron *pair* in lock-step (e.g. representing a signed quantity via two neurons), not to emit a genuinely bipolar spike from one neuron [TrueNorth neuron-model paper, viplab.fudan.edu.cn]. This is analogous to the Loihi-1 two-channel workaround: **negative values via a dual-neuron/dual-channel encoding, not a hardware-native signed spike.**
- **SpiNNaker.** No explicit statement of native negative/signed *spike* support was found; SpiNNaker's software-defined neuron/packet framework could in principle transmit a signed payload in a packet's key/payload field (as demonstrated for signed floating-point *error signals*, not forward-pass activations, in the SpiNNaker2 EventProp paper [arXiv:2412.15021]), but this is not documented as a standard forward-pass negative-spike primitive — marked **unknown / not confirmed for standard SNN spikes** (as opposed to the demonstrated gradient/error-signal payload case).
- **DYNAP-CNN/Speck, BrainScaleS-2, FPGA (general).** No native negative/signed spike primitive identified in reviewed sources. Speck's IF neuron does support a configurable `threshold_low` (negative floor) that clips how negative the *membrane potential* may go under negative input, but the emitted spike event itself is documented as a single (positive) event type — this is a state-variable feature, not a signed-spike feature [Speck Dev Kit Manual, Sec. 4.2.4.6.3]. FPGA is fully flexible in principle (any synthesizable logic) but no example of a synthesized negative-spike neuron was found among the reviewed FPGA accelerator papers.

**Bottom line for feasibility:** genuinely native hardware support for signed/negative spike *events* was confirmed only for **Loihi 2** (via signed graded-spike payloads). Every other platform reviewed either has no documented support, or achieves an equivalent effect only through a **software/architectural workaround** — dual excitatory/inhibitory channels (Loihi 1, effectively also TrueNorth's paired-neuron pattern) — that doubles wiring/neuron cost, analogous to the reset-by-subtraction multi-compartment workaround in Part B.2.

### B.6 Multi-compartment neurons

**Definition.** A single logical neuron is built from several electrically/algorithmically coupled sub-units ("compartments"), each with its own state variables, enabling dendritic-tree-like spatial structure, localized nonlinearities (dendritic spikes), or — as used repeatedly above — auxiliary state for mechanisms the single-compartment hardware primitive cannot express natively (soft reset, AHP-based adaptation, aLIF/$\Sigma\Delta$).

**Hardware.**
- **Loihi (1 and 2).** Confirmed multi-compartment support, used in the reviewed literature for three distinct purposes: (i) soft-reset workaround (Part B.2, NxTF, arXiv:2101.04261); (ii) AHP-current adaptive neurons / LSTM-like memory (Part B.4, Nature Machine Intelligence 2022); (iii) four-state aLIF/$\Sigma\Delta$-neuron mapping, needing two compartments on Loihi 1 (Part A.6, arXiv:2407.13534). Loihi's architecture document describes each neuron as "designed to resemble an actual biological neuron model... hierarchical connectivity, dendritic compartments, synaptic delays" [Frontiers in Neuroinformatics 2022, https://doi.org/10.3389/fninf.2022.883360].
- **BrainScaleS-2.** Native, extensively documented multi-compartment support: individual analog neuron circuits can be electrically connected (via configurable switches $S_{mh}$/$S_{mv}$ and conductances $R_{ms}$) to form compartment chains or branching dendritic trees, replicating passive EPSP attenuation along a chain and dendritic (plateau-potential) spikes at branch points [BrainScaleS-2 official docs, https://electronicvisions.github.io/documentation-brainscales2/latest/brainscales2-demos/fp_multicompartment.html; Pehle et al. 2022, https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2022.795876; "A Scalable Approach to Modeling on Accelerated Neuromorphic Hardware," https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2022.884128]. This is arguably the most developed native multi-compartment capability among the platforms reviewed — it is used for genuine dendritic-computation research (plateau potentials, branch-dependent spike thresholds), not only as a workaround for a missing primitive.
- **SpiNNaker, DYNAP-CNN/Speck, TrueNorth, FPGA.** No native multi-compartment neuron primitive was identified in the reviewed sources for these four platforms. SpiNNaker's full software programmability means a multi-compartment model *could* be written as a custom neuron kernel (state variables are just C struct fields, updated by user code each timestep, and inter-compartment coupling would be implemented as ordinary synaptic-like connections between two SpiNNaker neurons) — but this is an implementation possibility, not a documented feature, so marked **unknown / possible-via-software but not confirmed as used**.

## ADDENDUM — RANK ORDER CODING (A.8)

Added at coordinator request; rank order coding is a first-class, historically prior encoding scheme that was missing from Part A. It is placed here as A.8 rather than renumbering the body of the document.

### A.8 Rank order coding

**Definition and origin.** Rank order coding (ROC) was introduced by Simon Thorpe and colleagues to explain how the primate visual system can perform complex recognition tasks (e.g., animal/no-animal categorization) in under 150 ms, a time budget that permits at most one spike per neuron per processing stage and therefore rules out conventional rate coding, which needs at least two spikes to estimate an interspike interval [Thorpe & Gautrais 1996/1998, NeurIPS "Rapid Visual Processing using Spike Asynchrony," https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/1996/file/fd5c905bcd8c3348ad1b35d7231ee2b1-Paper.pdf; Thorpe, "Spike-based strategies for rapid processing," https://sccn.ucsd.edu/~arno/mypapers/ThorpeSpiking_Neurons.pdf; CNRS SpikeNet documentation, https://cerco.cnrs.fr/pagesp/arno/spikenet/order.html]. Each neuron in a population fires **at most once** (typically modeled as an integrate-and-fire "analog-to-delay converter," where more strongly activated neurons cross threshold sooner); the information is carried entirely by the **relative order** in which the population's neurons fire, not by which timesteps they fire at and not by how many times they fire. With $N$ neurons there are $N!$ possible orderings, giving a theoretical channel capacity of $\log_2(N!)$ bits — for $N=10$, over 21 bits, versus $\log_2(N+1)$ bits available to a rate code counting up to $N$ spikes in the same population over the same window [Thorpe, ThorpeSpiking_Neurons.pdf].

**What quantity the spike train represents.** Not a firing rate and not an absolute latency value — purely the **rank** (1st, 2nd, 3rd, ... to fire) of each neuron within one feedforward "wave" of activity triggered by a single stimulus presentation. Crucially, ROC is explicitly **contrast-invariant**: changing the input intensity or contrast changes the absolute latencies of the analog-to-delay converters, but leaves the *rank ordering* of which neuron fires before which unchanged, so the code is intrinsically robust to a whole class of nuisance transformations that would corrupt a literal latency/TTFS code [CNRS SpikeNet order-coding page, https://cerco.cnrs.fr/pagesp/arno/spikenet/order.html, explicit figure demonstrating rank-order invariance to intensity and contrast changes].

**Decoder.** The canonical ROC decoder is a feedforward **shunting-inhibition** mechanism: every afferent spike progressively desensitizes ("inhibits") the postsynaptic neuron, so an afferent's contribution to the postsynaptic potential decays geometrically with its arrival rank (first-to-arrive counts most). If the postsynaptic neuron's synaptic weights are set so the strongest weight lines up with the input expected to fire first, the strongest weight second, and so on, then that neuron reaches maximal activation exactly when the input population fires in the order matching its weight ranking — giving a simple, local, biologically plausible mechanism for detecting a specific rank-order pattern without needing explicit timestamp storage or delay lines [Thorpe, ThorpeSpiking_Neurons.pdf, "Decoding Rank Order" section, worked numerical example with weights 5,4,3,2,1 and 50%-per-spike desensitization].

**Precision and latency versus rate coding — why it is cheaper.** Rate coding needs multiple spikes (in general $O(T)$ spikes per neuron for $T$-level precision, Part A.1) and, for a population of $n$ neurons each able to emit up to $T$ spikes, can represent only $O(n\log T)$ bits in a window of length $T$; a rank-order code over the same $n$ neurons, using only one spike per neuron, represents $\log_2(n!) = O(n\log n)$ bits — asymptotically comparable information capacity per neuron, but obtained with roughly $n$ spikes total instead of $nT$ spikes, and it needs only a **single feedforward wave** rather than a full multi-timestep integration window [Thorpe, ThorpeSpiking_Neurons.pdf, capacity comparison to rate coding]. Guo et al.'s comparative study places rank-order coding's descendant literature (temporal/order-based codes generally) as needing "30 simulation steps" versus "hundreds" for two-layer rate-coded SNNs in one hardware ROC-SCNN implementation [Kim & Kim et al., ScienceDirect 2020, "Rank order coding based spiking convolutional neural network architecture with energy-efficient membrane voltage updates," https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925231220308663]. Auge et al.'s survey frames ROC precisely: it "encodes the information without considering the precise timing of the spikes... functions as a discrete normalisation filter with the loss of the absolute amplitude information," so it "is not possible to reconstruct the absolute signal amplitude" — the price of ROC's efficiency and contrast-invariance is that it discards the analog amplitude/intensity information a rate or TTFS code would preserve [Auge et al., https://doi.org/10.1007/s11063-021-10562-2].

**Robustness.** ROC's ordinal (rather than cardinal) nature makes it inherently invariant to any monotonic rescaling of input intensity (Part A.8 definition above). However, Auge et al. also note a specific fragility: "the scheme is further limited by the distinction of the spikes and jitter due to a huge effect for small ISI" — when spikes cluster very close together in time, small timing jitter can flip their rank order and corrupt the code, a failure mode a pure count-based rate code does not share. This trade-off was measured directly in an electronic-nose gas-classification application: "encoding the samples with rank order coding achieved a classification accuracy of 95.2% and with TTFS 100%... [because] in ROC the spikes are really close together and a small spike jitter has a large effect on the classification accuracy" [Auge et al., citing Chen, Ng, Bermak, Law, Martinez, https://doi.org/10.1007/s11063-021-10562-2].

### Precise relationship to TTFS/latency coding and to N-of-M coding

These three schemes are frequently conflated in casual usage; a 2022 theoretical unification paper makes the distinctions precise and is the authoritative source for this comparison [Denève et al., "Analyzing time-to-first-spike coding schemes: A theoretical approach," Front. Neurosci. 2022, https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2022.971937 / PMC9548614]:

- **TTFS / latency coding (Part A.2)** reads the **precise timing value** of each neuron's single spike — the decoder needs to know *when* (in absolute or relative time) each spike occurred, and that continuous timing value is itself the encoded quantity.
- **Rank order coding (ROC)** reads only the **relative order** of spikes across a population — *which* neuron fired 1st, 2nd, 3rd, etc. — and discards the actual inter-spike time intervals entirely. ROC is thus TTFS with the timing information thrown away and only the ordinal information kept; conversely, TTFS is strictly more informative than ROC per spike (a TTFS decoder can always recover the rank order from the timings, but a ROC decoder cannot recover the timings from the ranks). This ordinality is exactly what buys ROC its contrast invariance and (per Denève et al.) requires a **shunting-inhibition** desensitization mechanism, with synaptic weights *and* the desensitization both decreasing (geometrically, in Thorpe's original formulation) with rank — every one of the $M$ afferents fires and is used.
- **N-of-M coding** is a distinct third scheme (Furber, Bainbridge, Cumpstey, Temple 2004, "Sparse distributed memory using N-of-M codes," https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15541946/; formalized alongside ROC and TTFS by Thorpe et al. 2019 and by Denève et al. 2022): only the **first $N$ spikes among $M$ afferents** are propagated at all (a hard winner-take-$N$ cutoff), and — critically — the *order* of those $N$ spikes does **not** matter; the readout uses homogeneous (binary, 0/1) synaptic weights and no desensitization, so any of the $N!$ orderings of the surviving $N$ spikes decodes identically. N-of-M is therefore a **sparsity-only** code (which $N$ of $M$ neurons fired) with the ordinal information of ROC deliberately discarded, in exchange for being "the easiest to implement in hardware because it only requires binary synapses" [Denève et al., PMC9548614].
- Denève et al. additionally propose a **fourth, hybrid** scheme, **Ranked-NoM (R-NoM)**, which keeps N-of-M's hard cutoff to the first $N$ of $M$ spikes but restores ROC-style order-sensitivity via inhomogeneous (graded) weights and a linearly (not geometrically) decreasing modulation function; they show analytically that R-NoM has substantially higher discriminability than either pure ROC or pure N-of-M, especially in the earliest phase of the response, and argue it is more hardware-friendly than classical ROC because it uses only integer-valued weights/modulation (unlike ROC's geometric/real-valued desensitization) [same source].

In short: **TTFS = timing value; ROC = order only (all $M$ afferents fire, geometric/graded weighting); N-of-M = sparsity only (first $N$ of $M$ fire, order discarded, binary weighting); R-NoM = sparsity + order (first $N$ of $M$ fire, order preserved via graded weighting).** These are four points on a single design space (which afferents count, and whether their order counts), not synonyms for one idea.

### Hardware using rank order coding

**MorphIC — verified measured ~10× energy improvement.** Frenkel, Legat, Bol (2019), "MorphIC: A 65-nm 738k-Synapse/mm² Quad-Core Binary-Weight Digital Neuromorphic Processor with Stochastic Spike-Driven Online Learning," IEEE TBioCAS 13(5):999–1010 [https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.1904.08513; https://doi.org/10.1109/tbcas.2019.2928793]. On the same MNIST classification task, using the same 2k-LIF-neuron, four-core chip fabricated in 65 nm CMOS, the authors directly measured:
- **Rate coding:** 97.8% accuracy, **205 µJ per classification** at 0.8 V / 55 MHz.
- **Rank order coding** (inferred class = whichever output-layer neuron spikes *first*): 95.9% accuracy (a 1.9-percentage-point drop), **21.8 µJ per classification** at 0.8 V / 55 MHz (5.45 mW at 250 classifications/s).

That is a measured **205/21.8 ≈ 9.4×** energy reduction — the coordinator's cited "roughly 10×" figure is verified and precisely quantified: the paper's own text states the result as "a 10-fold energy improvement" [arXiv:1904.08513, Sec. III result summary and Table IV]. With three further architectural optimizations (crossbar addressing to avoid dummy synaptic operations, symmetric ±1 weight interpretation instead of asymmetric 0/1 rescaling, and manual clock gating) the authors project the rank-order-coding energy could be reduced further to 8.2 µJ per classification — a ~25× improvement over the rate-coding baseline — though this projection was not re-measured on silicon, only estimated from the chip's power model [same source, Table IV, Fig. 14(b)]. The paper also situates MorphIC's rank-order energy-accuracy point as competitive with TrueNorth's and with a separate SNN chip from Chen et al., both benchmarked on the same normalized-to-65nm MNIST comparison [same source, Fig. 14].

**BrainChip Akida — confirmed native/default use of rank order coding, tracing to Thorpe's SpikeNet lineage.** BrainChip's own technical documentation states directly: "The Akida processor uses an encoding scheme called 'rank coding,' in which information is expressed as the time and place it occurs" [BrainChip, "What is the Akida" tech brief, https://brainchip.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/BrainChip_tech-brief_What-is-Akida_v3-1.pdf; also https://brainchip.com/case-studies/what-is-the-akida-event-domain-neural-processor/]. This is corroborated by an independent hardware-survey source: "Akida does not support LIF neurons. Instead, it operates similarly to an event camera, converting pixels to events and using Rank Order Coding (ROC) to encode the input" [Open Neuromorphic, "A Look at Akida," https://open-neuromorphic.org/neuromorphic-computing/hardware/akida-brainchip/]. An independent space-applications energy-efficiency study that benchmarked models on real AKD1000 silicon likewise reports "the use of ROC decoding and the integrate-and-fire behavior in the Akida processor is confirmed" by their own testing, and separately notes the AKD1000 "does not support leaky neurons," i.e., its native neuron is a nonleaky IF variant paired with ROC decoding, and effectively reduces conversion to a single simulation timestep [Lunghi, Silvestrini, Dold, Meoni, Hadjiivanov, Izzo, "Energy efficiency analysis of Spiking Neural Networks for space applications," arXiv:2505.11418v1; they measured AKD1000 inference energy of 0.63–1.38 mJ/frame on an EuroSAT classification task]. Akida's product architecture document also describes the fixed hardware neuron as "compute only when events occur... Integrate and fire neuron" with "up-to 4 bits for weights and activation" [BrainChip AKD1000/Akida 1.0 IP Product Brief, https://brainchip.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/Akida-1.0-IP-Product-Brief_final.pdf]. While BrainChip's marketing material does not itself cite Thorpe/SpikeNet by name in the documents retrieved here, the naming ("rank coding"), the single-spike/order-based mechanism, and BrainChip's well-documented technology lineage (BrainChip's earlier SNN research and IP portfolio descend from work connected to Thorpe's rank-order/SpikeNet research program) make the connection to Thorpe's original ROC formulation direct at the level of the encoding *mechanism*; no single document retrieved in this pass explicitly narrates the corporate/technical lineage from SpikeNet to Akida, so that specific historical claim is presented here as strongly consistent with, but not independently document-verified beyond, the shared "rank order coding" terminology and mechanism.

**Custom ASIC (65 nm), STDP-trained ROC-SCNN.** A dedicated rank-order-coding spiking CNN accelerator (not one of the seven platforms in the main cross table, included here for completeness) implements ROC input encoding with a "novel low complexity ROC that efficiently removes sorting operations," reporting 2.85K classifications/s at 6.79 µJ/classification and 90.2% MNIST accuracy with unsupervised STDP learning — explicitly motivated by rank-order coding needing only ~30 simulation steps versus "hundreds" for two-layer rate-coded SNNs [Kim et al., ScienceDirect 2020, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0925231220308663].

**Other platforms — no source found.** No document retrieved in this research pass describes native rank-order-coding support (or a documented absence thereof) on Loihi 1/2, SpiNNaker 1/2, DYNAP-CNN/Speck, BrainScaleS-2, TrueNorth, or general FPGA frameworks. Given ROC's origin as a software/algorithmic convention realized on top of ordinary IF/LIF spiking substrates (any hardware that can determine "which neuron spiked first" from a population — which is also exactly the TTFS-decoder requirement, Part A.2 — could in principle run ROC), the absence of a source is most likely an absence of *documentation* specifically discussing ROC on these chips, not necessarily an absence of *capability*; marked **unknown** rather than assumed supported or unsupported.

### Does any ANN-to-SNN conversion method target rank order coding?

**No conversion method targeting rank order coding was located in this research pass**, and there is a structural reason to expect none exists in the form conversion theorems currently take. Every ANN-to-SNN conversion proof reviewed for this survey and its predecessor (Rueckauer et al. 2017; Ding, Yu, Tian, Huang, "Optimal ANN-SNN Conversion for Fast and Accurate Inference in Deep Spiking Neural Networks," IJCAI 2021, https://doi.org/10.24963/ijcai.2021/321; the AAAI SRP paper; Jiang et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023 IJCAI) is built on the same foundational correspondence: **a ReLU activation value in the ANN equals a firing *rate* (spike count over $T$ steps, or equivalently an accumulated/averaged membrane-potential quantity) in the SNN**, proved by showing the SNN's time-averaged firing rate converges to the ANN activation as $T\to\infty$ under reset-by-subtraction (Part B.2). Ding et al.'s Theorem 1, for instance, is stated and proved entirely in terms of matching per-layer *firing rates* $r_l(t)$ between ANN and SNN [https://doi.org/10.24963/ijcai.2021/321]. This machinery is fundamentally **cardinal** (it matches a count/magnitude on one side to a count/magnitude on the other) and has no natural target for a code whose entire content is **ordinal** (a permutation of $N$ neurons, not a magnitude per neuron). A ReLU layer's output is a vector of $N$ independent real numbers; a rank-order code over the same $N$ output neurons encodes only their *relative order*, which is a single combinatorial object (one of $N!$ permutations) rather than $N$ independently-conveyed magnitudes — there is no obvious bijection or convergence argument analogous to "spike count $\to$ activation value" that would let a per-neuron firing-rate-matching proof carry over.

This is precisely the gap the coordinator's framing anticipated: **the uniform-grid rate-coding correspondence (Part A.1's observation that count coding is a uniform $T$-level quantizer, and QCFS is that same uniform grid) has no obvious analogue for an order-based code**, because there is no single scalar "quantization level" per neuron to be uniformly or non-uniformly gridded — the represented object is a *joint* combinatorial state of the whole population. What such a conversion theorem *would* have to establish, by analogy with the existing rate-based proofs, is at minimum: (i) a way to map a real-valued ANN activation vector $a\in\mathbb{R}^N$ onto a target *permutation* (or partial order, given ROC's practical requirement of finite spike-timing resolution) of $N$ spiking units, such that the ordinal relationships in $a$ (which components are larger than which others) are preserved by construction — this is comparatively natural, since $a\mapsto \text{argsort}(a)$ is already well-defined; and (ii) — the harder, unsolved half — a proof that a *downstream layer's* rank-order-coded output, computed from an upstream layer's rank-order-coded *input* via the shunting-inhibition ROC decoder mechanism (Part A.8), converges to the rank ordering the source ANN's next-layer activations would have produced, layer after layer, despite the fact that the ROC decoder mechanism (geometric desensitization keyed to a specific target weight ranking) is a fundamentally different nonlinearity from a ReLU and was designed as a single-layer feature detector, not as a composable building block with a known fixed point under repeated application. No source reviewed here attempts this; the closest adjacent published work is (a) the Denève et al. unifying discriminability framework, which analyzes ROC/NoM/R-NoM as detectors of a *fixed preferred pattern* rather than as a general-purpose value-preserving layer-to-layer transform, and (b) BrainChip's Akida toolchain, which does convert trained ANNs to run on ROC-decoding hardware [Lunghi et al., arXiv:2505.11418v1, describing the MetaTF ANN$\to$quantized-model$\to$"spiking model" pipeline] but — per that same independent benchmarking paper — collapses to a **single simulation timestep** rather than implementing a genuine multi-layer rank-preserving conversion proof; the documentation available does not explain the conversion's operating principle in the rank-order-theoretic terms used by the ROC literature, and no accuracy-preservation theorem for it was located.
