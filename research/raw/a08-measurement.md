# A08 — How Neuromorphic Efficiency Is Actually Measured, and Why Published Claims Are Often Not Comparable

Research pass for the neuromorphic deployment survey. Topic: measurement methodology and comparability of efficiency claims across the SNN/neuromorphic literature. All claims sourced; "NOT FOUND" used where a specific claim could not be verified.

---

## 1. SynOps / operation counting: origin and critique of the 0.9 pJ MAC / 0.1 pJ AC numbers

**Origin of the numbers.** The energy-per-operation table nearly every SNN paper cites traces to Mark Horowitz's ISSCC 2014 plenary talk, "1.1 Computing's Energy Problem (and what we can do about it)" (Horowitz, ISSCC 2014, pp. 10–14, doi:10.1109/ISSCC.2014.6757323). Figure 1.1.9 of that talk gives "rough energy costs for various operations in 45nm technology" at 0.9V. The exact table entries, as reproduced and cited by later papers (e.g., Han et al.'s EIE accelerator paper, safari.ethz.ch mirror of arXiv:1602.01528), are:

| Operation | Energy (pJ) | Relative cost |
|---|---|---|
| 32-bit int ADD | 0.1 | 1× |
| 32-bit float ADD | 0.9 | 9× |
| 32-bit int MULT | 3.1 | 31× |
| 32-bit float MULT | 3.7 | 37× |
| 32-bit access, 32KB SRAM | 5 | 50× |
| 32-bit access, off-chip DRAM (LPDDR2) | 640 | 6400× |

Source: https://gwern.net/doc/cs/hardware/2014-horowitz-2.pdf (full talk text), mirrored at https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~markhill/restricted/isscc2014_horowitz_power_scaling.pdf; also cited verbatim in Han et al., "EIE: Efficient Inference Engine on Compressed Deep Neural Network" (arXiv:1602.01528), Table I, "Energy Table for 45nm CMOS process," at https://safari.ethz.ch/architecture/fall2025/lib/exe/fetch.php?media=1602.01528v2.pdf.

**What "0.9 pJ MAC / 0.1 pJ AC" actually means.** The commonly cited "MAC = 0.9pJ, AC = 0.1pJ" shorthand used across the SNN literature is a simplification built on top of Horowitz's ADD figure (0.1pJ int ADD, taken as the accumulate-only cost of a spiking synapse) versus a MAC estimate that composes a multiply and an add (float ADD ≈0.9pJ is sometimes substituted directly as the "MAC" cost, or a MULT+ADD combination is used). The number is **process-node- and precision-specific**: it applies to a 45nm CMOS process at 0.9V supply, for 32-bit fixed operations. It is not a hardware-agnostic constant. Horowitz's own text stresses that computation is a minority of total system energy once memory access is included: "the total energy is dominated by the required memory access if there is no data reuse... running a 1G connection neural network at 20Hz would require (20Hz)(1G)(640pJ)=12.8W just for DRAM accesses" (Han et al., citing Horowitz's data).

**What SynOps proxies exclude.** Horowitz's talk itself flags that (a) DRAM access costs 100–6400× more than the compute operation itself, (b) SRAM access at 5pJ already dwarfs the 0.1pJ ADD, and (c) "the programmable nature of a processor has high energy overhead, 70pJ/instruction, vs. a few pJ for an operation" due to instruction fetch and register clocking. None of this is captured by a SynOps or FLOP count that multiplies operation-count by a single per-op constant. Source: https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~markhill/restricted/isscc2014_horowitz_power_scaling.pdf.

**Critiques of SynOps as an SNN efficiency proxy (multiple independent papers):**

1. **Bhattacharjee, Yin, Moitra, Panda (Yale), "Are SNNs Truly Energy-efficient? — A Hardware Perspective"** (arXiv:2309.03388, 2023). Direct quote from the abstract: "their energy efficiency claims rely on certain impractical estimation metrics... we find that the actual energy-efficiency improvements of recent SNN algorithmic works differ significantly from their estimated values due to various hardware bottlenecks." The paper benchmarks published SNN algorithms on two hardware-realistic platforms — SATA (a sparsity-aware systolic-array accelerator) and SpikeSim (an in-memory-computing/RRAM crossbar simulator) — and finds large gaps between claimed and measured energy-efficiency gains (their Table II reports "estimated" vs. "hardware-realistic" energy-efficiency improvements on CIFAR-10; the estimated energy is explicitly "proportional to the product of FLOPs, timesteps and the sparsity," i.e., the standard SynOps-style proxy). Roadblocks identified: repeated computation/data movement over timesteps, neuronal module overhead (membrane update, reset), and vulnerability of SNNs to crossbar non-idealities. Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.03388.

2. **Shen, Zhao, Li, Li, Zeng (CASIA), "Are Conventional SNNs Really Efficient? A Perspective from Network Quantization"** (CVPR 2024). Direct quote: "modern SNN configurations remain deeply rooted in the methodologies of deep ANNs. These SNN designs typically rely on optimistic energy usage projections that overlook the tangible overheads associated with deploying hardware in real-world environments... this prevalent comparative framework fails to substantiate the computational efficacy of SNNs in a comprehensive manner." The paper proposes replacing SynOps with a "Bit Budget" metric that unifies timesteps (in SNNs) and quantized activation bit-width (in ANNs) as the same underlying resource, explicitly to enable fair SNN-vs-quantized-ANN comparison. Source: https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Shen_Are_Conventional_SNNs_Really_Efficient_A_Perspective_from_Network_Quantization_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf.

3. **Narduzzi, Zenke, Liu, Dunbar, "EFLOP: a sparsity-aware metric for evaluating computational cost in spiking and non-spiking neural networks"** (Neuromorphic Computing and Engineering, 2025). Direct quote: "existing computational frameworks fail to accurately estimate the cost of running sparse networks on modern time-stepped hardware, which exploits sparsity by skipping zero-valued operations... metrics such as spike count and synaptic operations (SynOps) fail to account for these operations [membrane updates, gating dynamics] and lack a direct counterpart for ANNs. As a result, there is no unified and clear definition of a hardware-agnostic metric to fairly evaluate computational cost across these paradigms." Proposes "EFLOP" (effective FLOP) that accounts for sparse pre-activation updates in both SNNs and ANNs, applied to weight-sparsified GRU and LIF models. Source: https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/2634-4386/addee8.

4. **Guobin et al. note a specific asymmetry:** a SynOps-based comparison is typically made against un-optimized ANN baselines, not against ANNs that have themselves been through quantization/pruning: "Conventionally, SNNs are often compared to ANNs that have not undergone similar optimizations... they do not fairly consider the significant advancements in ANN energy-efficient optimization." Same source as item 2 above.

**Bottom line for the survey:** a SynOps-based energy number establishes, at best, a relative *operation-count* ratio under an implicit, usually unstated, hardware model (one process node, one precision, no memory-access cost, no neuron-state-update cost, no routing cost). It does not establish a real joule figure for any actual chip, and multiple independent hardware-realistic studies (SATA/SpikeSim, EFLOP, Bit Budget) show the gap between the SynOps estimate and measured hardware energy is large and systematic, not a rounding error.

## 2. NeuroBench (Yik et al., 2023–2025)

**Citation:** Yik, J., Van den Berghe, K., den Blanken, D., Bouhadjar, Y., Fabre, M., Hueber, P., et al. "The NeuroBench framework for benchmarking neuromorphic computing algorithms and systems." *Nature Communications* 16, 1545 (2025). doi:10.1038/s41467-025-56739-4. Earlier preprint: arXiv:2304.04640 (v1 April 2023, iterated through v5 January 2025). Community effort: nearly 100 co-authors across 50+ institutions, including Intel Labs personnel (Mike Davies, Sumit Bam Shrestha, Yulia Sandamirskaya, Alessandro Pierro, Philipp Stratmann, Jonathan Timcheck) and academic neuromorphic groups.

**What it standardizes — the dual-track structure.** NeuroBench "consists of an algorithm track for hardware-independent evaluation and a system track for fully deployed solutions." Direct quote: "As an emerging technology, neuromorphic hardware has not converged to a single platform which is commercially available, thus a large fraction of neuromorphic research explores algorithmic advancement on conventional systems which may not be optimal for performance." Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-56739-4 and https://arxiv.org/html/2304.04640v3.

- **Algorithm track**: hardware-independent, uses "complexity metrics to analyze solution costs" — four benchmarks (few-shot continual learning, event-camera object detection/computer vision, motor cortical decoding, chaotic/low-dimensional forecasting), wrapped in an open-source harness (github.com/NeuroBench/neurobench) for standardized data loading, metric computation, and reporting.
- **System track**: "defines standard protocols to measure the real-world speed and efficiency of neuromorphic hardware... ranging from standard machine learning tasks to promising fields for neuromorphic systems, such as optimization." Uses metrics such as execution time, throughput, power and energy consumption at the *task* level.

**Explicit statement on non-comparability of existing results.** From the arXiv v1 (2304.04640) text: "The extensive heterogeneity of neuromorphic algorithms and systems complicates the formulation of proportional, equitable, and standardized approaches for comparison and evaluation, which is needed to systematically assess state-of-the-art advances in neuromorphic computing." And: "the lack of portable frameworks across different solutions makes it particularly challenging to make apples-to-apples comparisons of neuromorphic solutions." And on hardware measurement specifically, from the system-track discussion (openreview.net/pdf?id=r3r5M5T6YK, a NeuroBench companion article): "neuromorphic platforms are implemented at various solution scales, from single-chip, ultra-efficient edge devices up to multi-board server machines containing hundreds of chips... neuromorphic devices often utilize analog or mixed-signal technology, which have significantly different characteristics than fully-digital platforms... the state of many neuromorphic systems may not be a mature, standalone machine, and thus development and experimental hardware may populate the system which shouldn't reasonably be included in measurements. All of the challenges make a singular, fully-consistent measurement methodology that everyone can strictly apply currently infeasible." Because of this, "the NeuroBench system track instead favors transparency [over strict standardization]... offers guidelines for measurement protocols and reporting documentation to contextualize system track results" rather than a single enforced protocol.

**Three named challenges the paper says block comparability (Nature Communications version):**
1. "Lack of a formal definition" — no agreed criterion for what counts as a "neuromorphic" solution, risking either overly narrow or overly permissive benchmarking.
2. "Implementation diversity" — the field's frameworks (Lava, snnTorch, SpikingJelly, Nengo, Fugu, etc.) are not interoperable; "SNNs lack a common network exchange format such as ONNX for traditional deep learning," which "prevents simple portability of algorithms and datasets across the neuromorphic community."
3. "Rapid research evolution" — the field changes fast enough that any fixed benchmark risks obsolescence, requiring an iterative, versioned framework (explicit analogy to MLPerf).

Source (challenges list, verbatim): https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-56739-4 and arXiv:2304.04640v5 (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.04640v5.pdf).

**Related/earlier critique cited by NeuroBench itself:** Kulkarni, Parsa, Mitchell, Schuman, "Benchmarking the performance of neuromorphic and spiking neural network simulators," *Neurocomputing* 447 (2021), 145–160, doi:10.1016/j.neucom.2021.03.028 — an earlier, narrower attempt that NeuroBench explicitly says "have not seen widespread adoption due to a lack of inclusive, actionable, and iterative benchmark design."

## 3. The measurement boundary problem

**General framing.** What counts as "the energy of an inference" varies paper to paper: input encoding, host-to-chip data transfer, on-chip spike routing/communication, neuron state update (membrane leak/integrate/reset), output readout, and static/idle power over the measurement window are all candidates for inclusion or exclusion, and different labs draw the boundary differently. No single source lays out this boundary problem as a taxonomy explicitly under that name (search did not surface a paper using the phrase "measurement boundary problem"), but the *practice* is extensively documented in the primary hardware papers below, which show the boundary choice changing the reported number by 1–2 orders of magnitude.

**Direct empirical evidence that the boundary matters enormously (Loihi 2, PilotNet self-driving network, Shrestha et al. 2023):**

| Configuration | Total energy/frame | Dynamic energy/frame | Latency | Throughput |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Loihi 2, IO-unconstrained (weights pre-staged, no host I/O) | 0.09 mJ | 0.05 mJ | 1.21 ms | 7403.8 samples/s |
| Loihi 2, IO-limited (streaming from host) | 1.26 mJ | 0.07 mJ | 65.41 ms | 137.6 samples/s |

Same network, same chip — a **14× difference in total energy per frame and a 54× difference in latency**, purely as a function of whether host-to-chip I/O is included in the measurement window. Source: Shrestha, Timcheck, Frady, Campos-Macías, Davies, "Efficient Video and Audio Processing with Loihi 2" (arXiv:2310.03251, published version ICASSP 2024, doi:10.1109/icassp48485.2024.10448003), their Table 1.

**Intel's own reporting convention** (as described and followed by multiple Loihi papers citing Shrestha et al. as the reference methodology): report *static power*, *dynamic power*, and *total power* separately. "Static power is calculated only from the chips that were used by the network. The dynamic power was calculated as the measured total power minus measured static power. The reported total power is the sum of the adjusted static power and the dynamic power." Energy-delay product (EDP) is then computed as power × timestep × latency. This methodology is described as "Intel's recommended reporting structure for Loihi benchmarks" and is explicitly followed by later independent papers (e.g., the Sigma-Delta Neural Network Conversion on Loihi 2 paper, arXiv:2505.06417, states: "Following the format of Shrestha et al., we report static, dynamic, and total power... This approach for reporting power, latency, and EDP is consistent with Intel's recommended reporting structure for Loihi benchmarks"). Sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.03251, https://arxiv.org/html/2505.06417v2.

**What "dynamic energy" as reported by Intel excludes/handles specially:**
- Static/leakage power is measured "from boot" or from idle-chip baseline and *subtracted out* to get "dynamic" — meaning headline "dynamic energy" numbers by construction exclude the (often dominant) leakage/static contribution, which must be added back for a true total-energy comparison. The PilotNet example above shows static power dominating the IO-limited total (1.26 mJ total vs. only 0.07 mJ dynamic — over 94% of the reported energy in that configuration is static/leakage, not "spiking computation").
- Host CPU / "Lakemont" (on-die x86 cores used for I/O and control) power is tracked as a *separate* component from neurocore power and can be included or excluded depending on the paper. A GitHub technical discussion on NengoLoihi power probes documents this directly: "the neurocores dynamic power is computed as: nc_dyn_pwr = total_power − idle_power − lakemonts_dyn_power," and reports empirically measured idle power (941 mW) nearly equal to total power (944 mW) in one I/O-bound run, with Lakemont dynamic power only ~4 mW but I/O wait dominating execution time. When I/O dominates, "neurocore dynamic power drowned out by noise... giving strange results like negative dynamic power measurements." Source: https://github.com/nengo/nengo-zoo/discussions/840 (Nengo/NengoLoihi maintainer discussion, citing NxSDK internals `nxsdk/graph/nxenergy_time.py`).
- Spike-transfer/input-loading time can dominate total wall-clock time even when it is excluded from the reported "execution time": the same discussion documents a case where `loihi_sim.run()` took 13.35s total but only 1.14s was "network execution," the rest being spike-data upload to the board — and that only the 1.14s figure is what gets reported as "inference time" / used for power sampling in the profiler output.

**Batch-size asymmetry as another boundary issue.** Shrestha et al. (arXiv:2310.03251) show ANN baseline energy is batch-size dependent (PilotNet ANN energy per inference: 21.94 mJ at batch=1 vs. 6.14 mJ at batch=16 on the same Jetson Orin Nano, fp32), while Loihi's SDNN is "fundamentally non-batched." Comparisons that pick a favorable batch size for the ANN side (or the unbatched, worst case) change the reported advantage by up to ~3.5×. Their paper is explicit about this and reports both configurations rather than picking one, a transparency practice not universal in the literature.

**Input encoding cost (rate/Poisson coding) is frequently excluded or under-weighted.** Kim, Park, Moitra, Bhattacharjee, Venkatesha, Panda, "Rate Coding or Direct Coding: Which One is Better for Accurate, Robust, and Energy-efficient Spiking Neural Networks?" (arXiv:2202.03133, 2022) is one of the few papers to explicitly cost the Poisson spike-generation circuit itself into the SNN energy total, evaluated on a 16-bit Eyeriss platform (65nm CMOS): "Rate-encoded SNNs also involve a digital circuit for achieving the Poisson rate coding that is also accounted for in our energy evaluation... the first layer mainly contributes to the total energy cost." Their normalized energy table (VGG5/CIFAR-10, 10 timesteps) shows rate coding at 2.73×10^7 units vs. direct coding at 4.22×10^7 units of normalized MAC-energy — a real but frequently omitted cost in papers that only count post-encoding SynOps. Source: https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2202.03133.

**Conclusion for the survey on this point:** because static power, host I/O, encoding circuitry, and batching convention are all independently choosable, two papers reporting "energy per inference" for nominally the same chip and the same network can differ by more than an order of magnitude without either paper being fraudulent — they are answering different questions. The Loihi 2 PilotNet case (0.09 mJ vs 1.26 mJ, 14× apart, same chip, same network) is the cleanest single documented example.

## 4. Davies et al. 2021, "Advancing Neuromorphic Computing With Loihi: A Survey of Results and Outlook" (Proc. IEEE, 109(5), 911–934, doi:10.1109/JPROC.2021.3067593)

**The headline claim, verbatim from the abstract:** "While conventional feedforward deep neural networks show modest if any benefit on Loihi, more brain-inspired networks using recurrence, precise spike-timing relationships, synaptic plasticity, stochasticity, and sparsity perform certain computation with orders of magnitude lower latency and energy compared to state-of-the-art conventional approaches." Source: https://doi.org/10.1109/jproc.2021.3067593 (abstract mirrored at https://proceedingsoftheieee.ieee.org/advancing-neuromorphic-computing-with-loihi-a-survey-of-results-and-outlook/ and https://www.researchgate.net/publication/350705246).

**Specific technical statement on why converted feedforward/rate-coded CNNs are a poor fit, verbatim:** "Loihi's poor latency scaling with increasing workload size results from two factors. First, higher layer counts demand an increasing number of time steps in order to achieve maximum accuracy. This is a well-understood and fundamental property of rate-coded feedforward networks. Second, the need to distribute larger networks across multiple Loihi chips leads to congestion in the links between the chips and often a dramatic increase in execution time per time step. Converted deep networks, with dense cross-sectional connectivity and high aggregate spike rates, stress Loihi's off-chip mesh links, which have roughly 30× lower bandwidth than its on-chip links." And the paper's own conclusion on this class of network: "Our results... show that ANNs converted to rate-coded deep SNNs on Loihi may offer significant gains in energy efficiency compared to ANNs on conventional architectures but generally result in long latencies, especially for large-scale problems spanning multiple chips... These attributes render converted rate-coded models less attractive for neuromorphic architectures optimized for sparsity and, therefore, demand other approaches." Source: https://doi.org/10.1109/jproc.2021.3067593 (full text via IEEE Xplore document 9509196; excerpts retrieved via search index).

**Workload characteristics the paper associates with a genuine neuromorphic advantage (from the abstract and body):**
- Recurrence
- Precise spike-timing relationships (temporal coding, not just rate coding)
- Synaptic plasticity (on-chip learning)
- Stochasticity
- Sparsity (both weight and activation sparsity)
- Constrained/sparse optimization problems solved iteratively (e.g., LASSO via the Locally Competitive Algorithm, LCA) — the paper reports LCA on Loihi outperforming FISTA on CPU "by one to two orders of magnitude" in a regime where problem size fits on a single neuron core, with the advantage driven by increasing multicore parallelism as problem size grows, up to an inter-chip communication limit.

**Workload characteristics that predict Loihi performing worse or only modestly better (synthesized from the paper's own analysis):**
- Feedforward, rate-coded, large/deep CNNs applied to static images (batch-style classification) — "modest if any benefit."
- Networks large enough to require multi-chip distribution — inter-chip mesh bandwidth (30× lower than on-chip) becomes the bottleneck, and latency degrades faster than energy improves.

## 5. SNN vs. quantized (INT8) ANN on comparable hardware

**Most directly on-point study:** Shen, Zhao, Li, Li, Zeng, "Are Conventional SNNs Really Efficient? A Perspective from Network Quantization" (CVPR 2024) — already discussed in §1. Its central finding is a reframing rather than a single hardware benchmark: SNN timesteps and ANN activation bit-width are "analogous representations" of the same underlying resource ("Bit Budget"), and papers that compare a T-timestep SNN against a full-precision (unquantized) ANN are not making a fair comparison, because a quantized ANN at a comparable "bit budget" closes much of the claimed efficiency gap. Source: https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Shen_Are_Conventional_SNNs_Really_Efficient_A_Perspective_from_Network_Quantization_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf.

**Hardware-realistic comparison (not INT8-ANN-specific but directly relevant):** Bhattacharjee et al. (arXiv:2309.03388, §1 above) benchmark SNNs on SATA and SpikeSim — both platforms designed to be hardware-realistic accelerator classes (systolic array; RRAM in-memory-computing crossbar) — and find that "the actual energy-efficiency improvements of recent SNN algorithmic works differ significantly from their estimated values." This is evidence within the same hardware class rather than an explicit SNN-vs-INT8-ANN horse race, but it directly undercuts SynOps-based superiority claims made against unquantized ANN baselines.

**Loihi 2 vs. INT8 ANN on Jetson (closest available "same hardware class" data point found):** Shrestha et al. (arXiv:2310.03251) report both fp32 and int8 quantized ANN baselines on the Jetson Orin Nano GPU for PilotNet, alongside the Loihi 2 int8 SDNN:

| Network | Precision | Platform | Total energy/frame | Latency | EDP |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PilotNet SDNN | int8 | Loihi 2 (no IO) | 0.09 mJ | 1.21 ms | 0.11 µJs |
| PilotNet ANN, batch=1 | fp32 | Jetson Orin Nano | 21.94 mJ | 5.77 ms | 126.69 µJs |
| PilotNet ANN, batch=1 | int8 | Jetson Orin Nano | 13.72 mJ | 3.43 ms | 47.08 µJs |
| PilotNet ANN, batch=16 | int8 | Jetson Orin Nano | 5.31 mJ | 18.88 ms | 100.30 µJs |

Quantizing the ANN from fp32 to int8 on the same Jetson (batch=1) cuts its energy by ~37% (21.94→13.72 mJ) and its EDP by ~63% (126.69→47.08 µJs) — i.e., a substantial fraction of the "SNN advantage" over an *unquantized* ANN baseline evaporates once the ANN baseline is quantized to the same precision the SNN uses. Even so, Loihi 2 (IO-unconstrained) still shows an order-of-magnitude-plus advantage over the int8-quantized, batch=1 ANN in this specific case (0.09 mJ vs 13.72 mJ). Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.03251, their Table 1.

**Finding for the survey:** direct three-way (SNN, unquantized ANN, quantized ANN) comparisons on the *same* silicon are rare in the literature; the Shrestha et al. Loihi 2/Jetson data is the clearest example found, and it shows quantization narrows but does not eliminate the reported SNN advantage for this workload — but the workload (PilotNet, a small regression CNN with sparse sigma-delta activations) is itself close to the "sparse, low-dynamic-range" sweet spot the Davies 2021 survey identifies, not a generic large-scale classifier.

## 6. The T-scaling question: does reducing T reduce hardware energy proportionally?

**Short answer from measured (not simulated) evidence: No — not on the chips studied, because static/leakage power does not scale with reduced computation, and per-timestep dynamic power is dominated by chip-level draw that stays roughly flat.**

**Direct measured evidence, Loihi 2 (Sigma-Delta Neural Network Conversion on Loihi 2, arXiv:2505.06417):** Direct quote: "The total power usage was lower for Loihi 2 compared to the Jetson and **remained relatively consistent for all experiments as it is dominated by static draw**." The paper varies the sigma-delta activation threshold (which changes the effective sparsity/activity level, analogous to reducing effective spike traffic), and even though throughput improves substantially (176→362 FPS as sparsity increases from 18× to 250×), *total power stays roughly flat* because static power dominates. The paper further notes: "one of the key design choices of Loihi is the use of on-chip SRAM for all weights and activations. This reduces memory access latency but increases static power consumption, which scales with the number of Loihi chips and makes higher-resolution inputs more power-intensive." Source: https://arxiv.org/html/2505.06417v2.

**Direct measured evidence, LCA on Loihi 2 (arXiv:2307.13762):** Table 3/4 data show static power essentially constant (~0.54 W) across all threshold/sparsity settings on Loihi 2, while dynamic power varies modestly (0.17–0.39 W). At the sparsest setting, static power is nearly double the dynamic power (0.54 W static vs 0.39 W dynamic even at the most favorable operating point), meaning the theoretical energy savings from increased sparsity are substantially damped by the static floor. Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.13762v1.pdf.

**Interpretation for T-scaling specifically.** None of the retrieved sources directly sweep T (timesteps) while holding everything else fixed and reporting hardware-measured joules per inference on Loihi; the closest analogues (sparsity threshold sweeps, which have the same qualitative effect of reducing effective per-timestep computation) show that:
1. On synchronous/clocked digital neuromorphic hardware, **static (leakage) power is drawn continuously for the full duration a chip is powered on and mapped with a network, independent of whether that timestep does useful spiking work.**
2. Total measured energy is therefore closer to (static power × wall-clock duration) + (dynamic power × active cycles), and because static power dominates in the regimes measured, **cutting T (or spike traffic) reduces the dynamic term but does not proportionally reduce total energy** — it can even *increase* energy-per-inference if the T reduction also reduces throughput/parallelism benefits, since the same static floor is paid over more real time.
3. The Bhattacharjee/Yin/Moitra/Panda paper (§1) frames this generally as "repeated computations & data movements over timesteps" being a *roadblock*, not a free lever — i.e., every timestep re-touches memory and control logic regardless of spike sparsity, so naive T-count-based energy estimates (energy ∝ T × SynOps) overstate the achievable savings from cutting T. Source: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.03388.

**Caveat / limitation:** No source in this search explicitly isolates "energy at T=32" vs. "energy at T=4" on identical Loihi silicon for a single trained network while independently varying nothing else. The evidence base for the T-scaling claim in this file is therefore indirect (sparsity-threshold sweeps and static/dynamic power breakdowns) rather than a T-only ablation; the survey should present this as strong circumstantial/mechanistic evidence (static power dominance is a structural property of the SRAM-per-core Loihi design, confirmed by two independent measurement papers) rather than a directly measured T-only scaling curve.

## 7. Critiques of the field: overstated claims, simulation-to-hardware gap, lack of deployment

**Schuman, Kulkarni, Parsa, et al., "Opportunities for neuromorphic computing algorithms and applications," Nature Computational Science 2, 10–19 (2022), doi:10.1038/s43588-021-00184-y.** This is the most directly quotable field-insider critique found. Section titled "Closing the Gap Between Promises and Reality" (verbatim, from the OSTI preprint text, https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1881146):

> "Though neuromorphic hardware is available in the research community and there have been a wide variety of algorithms proposed, the applications have been primarily targeted towards benchmark datasets and demonstrations. **Neuromorphic systems are not currently being used in real-world applications**, and there are still a wide variety of challenges that restrict or inhibit rapid growth in algorithmic and application development."

> "There has yet to be a machine learning algorithm/application combination for which neuromorphic computing substantially outperforms deep learning approaches in terms of accuracy, though there have been compelling demonstrations in which neuromorphic solutions outperform other hardware implementations such as neural hardware and edge GPUs in terms of energy efficiency. This has led to the argument that neuromorphic systems are primarily interesting because of their low power computing abilities."

The same section explicitly names "Lack of Benchmarks and Metrics" as a top-level open challenge (§3.4, echoing the NeuroBench motivation two years before NeuroBench's full publication), and "Limited Access to and Usability of Existing Hardware and Software" (§3.2) as a second — the latter noting slow simulators limit rapid algorithmic iteration, which indirectly perpetuates reliance on simulation-based (not hardware-measured) efficiency estimates. Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s43588-021-00184-y (published version); full-text preprint at https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1881146.

**Indiveri, "Neuromorphic is dead. Long live neuromorphic," Neuron (Cell Press), Oct. 2025, doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2025.09.020.** A field-insider (Institute of Neuroinformatics, Zurich; one of the discipline's founding labs) perspective arguing the term "neuromorphic" has been diluted by "recent shifts toward digital and AI-centric approaches" that have "very little to do with the original term," and that the field should return to "analog, brain-inspired systems." This is a critique of *identity/direction* more than of *measurement practice* specifically, but it is directly relevant to the survey's "comparability" theme because it implies that papers labeled "neuromorphic" today span architectures (digital synchronous many-core chips like Loihi/TrueNorth vs. analog subthreshold circuits) that are not efficiency-comparable to each other by construction, let alone to conventional accelerators. Direct quote (from the ETH Zurich interview with Indiveri accompanying the piece): "Currently, neuromorphic engineering has shifted towards a digital or AI-centric approach which has very little to do with the original term... an important exploitation potential of the neuromorphic approach lies not in outperforming AI algorithms in the data center, but in complementing them." Source: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2025.09.020; interview at https://ee.ethz.ch/news-and-events/d-itet-news-channel/2025/10/the-goal-of-the-neuromorphic-approach-should-not-be-to-compete-with-ai-systems-but-to-complement-them.html. Note: this piece drew public pushback from other neuromorphic researchers (e.g., Brad Aimone, Sandia National Labs, via LinkedIn) defending digital platforms (TrueNorth, Loihi, SpiNNaker) as legitimate and valuable even though they depart from Carver Mead's original analog conception — worth noting in the survey as evidence the field itself is split on what "neuromorphic" should even mean, which is itself a comparability problem.

**Frontiers, "Neuromorphic Engineering Needs Closed-Loop Benchmarks" (2022), doi:10.3389/fnins.2022.813555.** Direct quote: "Sensing accuracy is but an arbitrary proxy for the actual system's goal—taking a good decision in a timely manner... The majority of approaches to formulating an evaluation metric exclude training and execution time from the loss function and thus from the performance evaluation. Similarly, power consumption, throughput and operations performed per unit time are not considered." Argues the field has imported machine-learning-style static-dataset accuracy benchmarks (MNIST-like) that do not exercise or reward the real-time, low-power, closed-loop properties neuromorphic systems are supposed to be good at, meaning most reported "neuromorphic advantage" numbers are measured on tasks unrepresentative of the claimed use case. Source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2022.813555/full.

**Narrower, hardware-specific critiques already covered in §1** (Bhattacharjee et al. 2023; Shen et al. 2024; Narduzzi et al. 2025) are also, in substance, critiques of the field's self-reported efficiency claims — they are listed there because they are more technical/quantitative than the broader field-critique pieces in this section.

**Not found:** a single canonical "the neuromorphic emperor has no clothes"-style polemic essay outside the peer-reviewed literature (e.g., a widely circulated blog post or magazine article specifically targeting neuromorphic efficiency claims, analogous to well-known AI-hype critiques in other subfields). The critiques that exist are embedded in peer-reviewed papers (Schuman et al., the hardware-benchmarking papers) or in a field-insider perspective (Indiveri) rather than as outsider skeptical commentary. If the survey wants an explicit "outside skeptic" voice, that gap should be flagged as such rather than filled with a weak source.
